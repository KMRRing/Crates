# Quest
**Renamed roles** (Korbi, 2 November 2026: "very close copies", so roles in the codex's own words). The Seer became the *Lookout* (an eye), the Guardian the *Pilot* (a ship's wheel), the Assassin the *Mutineer* (the dagger, kept), the Impostor the *Wrecker* (the lantern that lures a ship onto the rocks, the Hermit's old drawing), the Veiled the *Stowaway* (the veil, kept), the Hermit the *Smuggler* (a cask), and loyal knights *loyal hands*. Only the words and three drawings changed: the ids in the state, the database and the tests (`seer`, `guardian`, `assassin`, `impostor`, `veiled`, `hermit`) are the old ones, so a game in a room reads as before, and every name is at most eight letters so a seat's tag fits as before. What each role knows is Avalon's, unchanged; making the turn less of a copy is still to come (Cabal, To come).

Hidden loyalties round a table (October 2026): the mechanics of The Resistance and Avalon, with roles, names and drawings of its own, for five to ten, against the computer or with friends each on their own phone (Invite players: seats.js runs it, the computer taking the seats nobody has). Most at the table are loyal and a few are traitors (two at five and six, three from seven to nine, four at ten), and only the traitors know who the traitors are. Five quests set out one after another: three succeeding win it for the loyal, three failing for the traitors.

For each quest the leader (the lead passing clockwise, a quest at a time and after every refusal) names a party of the size the quest asks, and may go too: two or three at five players, up to five at eight and more (`SIZES` in quest-rules.js, the published table). Everyone votes on it, openly; more approvals than refusals and it sets out, otherwise the lead passes on, and five parties refused in a row hand the game to the traitors at once. On the quest each member plays success or fail, unseen: the loyal can only succeed, a traitor may do either, and one fail fails the quest, except the fourth from seven players up, which needs two. Only the number of fails is told, never whose. If the loyal win three, the traitors confer and name the one they think is the Lookout, and naming the Lookout wins it for them (The strike, below).

The roles, chosen in the menu: the Lookout (loyal) knows the traitors, and the Mutineer (a traitor, the traitors' last word on the Lookout) comes with it; the Pilot (loyal) knows the Lookout and the Wrecker (a traitor) but not which is which; the Stowaway (a traitor) is hidden from the Lookout; the Smuggler (a traitor) neither knows the other traitors nor is known by them. The rest are loyal hands, who know only themselves, and traitors, who know each other. The Pilot and the Stowaway come only with the Lookout, and a table has room for as many traitors' roles as it has traitors: at five, the Lookout and the Pilot take both, and the Stowaway and the Smuggler are left out. The narrator never says a role; at the end every role is shown.

The page seats everyone round the table's edge, you at the foot and the lead passing clockwise, evenly along a rounded oblong rather than an oval, since the players' chips are wider than they're tall and round an oval at ten the ones beside you touched. A chip shows the player's name, a crown on the leader, a pennant on the party, the last vote (approve or refuse, from its reveal until the next vote; a dot while one is going on means they've voted, not how), what your role tells you of them (traitor, or the Pilot's "Lookout?") and, at the end, their role, in verdigris or oxblood. The middle has the five quests (the party's size, a red 2 on the quest that needs two fails, then each result and its count of fails), the refusals so far, and the step; tap the quests for every party so far, who named it and who voted how, which is what a deduction game is played on. Your role card sits under the table (beside it, held sideways), with what the role means; a tap covers it, for a table where neighbours can see your phone. To name a party, tap its members round the table and Send; vote Approve or Refuse, and on a quest Succeed or Fail (Fail only a traitor can press). A vote or a card can be given before your turn: it's kept until your turn and goes in then, so nobody waits on you, and the computer players vote and play their cards at once, since they're secret till all are in, while a party named waits a second so you can see it, and the computer's strike two, for the traitors' names.

Talk (October 2026). A deduction game is played out loud, and alone with the computer it wasn't: you could only vote. Now a tap on a player opens what's been said of them (who accuses them, who trusts them) and your word: Accuse, or Trust, pressed again to take it back. Nobody may accuse more players than there are traitors, nor trust more than the others who could be loyal, so a word means something and nobody can accuse the whole table. A word isn't a move: the game never waits for one, so it goes on the table as a change of its own (`talk()` in quest-rules.js, the line kept with how many parties had been voted on), not through the runner, whose moves are turn by turn. What's been said shows at each player's foot (thumbs down accused, thumbs up trusted, counted, yours ringed in brass), the newest line, the narrator's or a player's, under the table (held sideways, the last several), the one who spoke marked at the table for a moment, and all of it between the parties in the parties so far. Leading, a tap chooses a member, so Talk turns it to talk for a word before you choose. The computer players talk too (a switch in the menu stills them): from the phone that runs the table, in the lulls, a beat after the table last changed, so they speak while someone thinks or a result is shown rather than over the moves; one at a time, at most one word of their own each party and three a step, a word back to an accusation sooner and always.

The strike. When the loyal have won three, the traitors confer: they know each other now, the table sees who they are, each may name whom they think is the Lookout (shown as a dagger with a count at the foot of the named), and then one strikes. Which one: the Mutineer when a person plays it, else the first traitor round the table from the Mutineer that a person plays, else the computer's Mutineer, going with the name most of the traitors gave. So a person among the traitors always has the strike, even when the computer plays the Mutineer: whom to strike is the traitors' decision together, and leaving it to the computer took the end of the game out of the hands of the person who'd played it. Which seats people play is read from the moves (the computer's carry bot, `s.people` the last word on each seat), so a friend who takes over a computer's seat has the strike once they've voted, and the computer has it back for a seat whose person has been away long enough for the computer to play it. The striker sees in short who names whom; the others among the traitors tap to name; the loyal wait.

The computer players (`quest-bot.js`) reason from what their role lets them know and from the play, never from roles they can't see. Each weighs every way the traitors could be seated (at most 210, at ten) by how well it explains what has happened: a quest's fails need that many traitors in its party, and a traitor fails more often than not; every vote, by how often a loyal player and a traitor, on the party or off it, approve a party clean or foul, as measured from their own games at each level (a traitor refuses a clean party far more often than the loyal do; on Sharp it votes on a foul one much as they do, on Easy it approves one with its own on it nearly every time); a traitor leading takes no other traitor along, except for a quest that needs two fails; and what's been said, each standing word by who says it of whom: a loyal player's accusation doubles the odds against its target (the Lookout's is always true), a traitor's says nothing, since traitors accuse the loyal and their own alike; a loyal player's trust counts a little for its target, a traitor's covers its own a little. With no Lookout in the game only the first few to accuse or to trust each player count (two at five or six, three at seven, six from eight), since those who join in mostly repeat what the first saw; with the Lookout, all of them, since any of them may be the one who knows. From that, each player's chance of being a traitor and each party's chance of being clean. The loyal name the cleanest party with themselves on it, approve their own, one nearly as clean as the best they could name, and always the fifth, which the loyal can't afford to refuse; at five, also one with nobody on it they suspect more than most (so the first quest's goes, with nothing yet against anyone), and from six one as clean as the next leader is likely to name if it's refused (each rule plays best at its sizes). The Lookout votes and leads as a plain hand would on what everyone can see, steering by what it knows only some of the time, so its votes don't hand it to the traitors' strike. The Pilot hears its two: in each seating the loyal one of them is the Lookout, whose word is true and whose votes follow what it knows, and once they've spoken it votes for a party only when it's more likely clean than not. Traitors on Sharp vote as a hand in their seat would, no more readily for a party with one of their own (which would tie them together), against a clean one now and then once the loyal are two quests up, and against the fourth clean party in a row when the next leader is one of them, since the loyal must send the fifth; they lead with themselves and the players the table trusts most (one more of their own when two fails are needed); on a quest the one of them the table suspects most fails it, having least to lose, and the others keep clean; they let the first quest pass now and then to be trusted, and fail every quest once two have failed. Easy players know only their own side, choose and vote loosely, fail every quest they can (two fails show two traitors), vote for a party with one of their own more readily, and half guess at the Lookout.

They talk as they think, each with a temper of its own for the game, some quicker to speak than others. Nobody says anything of their own before the first vote. A hand accuses the players it's fairly sure of (two at most), trusts the ones it's surest are loyal once a quest has been played, and takes back what it no longer thinks, giving its reason: on the failed quest, chose the party that failed, approved it, agreeing with others, or nothing it can name. The Lookout accuses only traitors (or, with the Stowaway in the game, players it can't see), never trusts one, and waits for the play to give it some cover; on Sharp, with a Pilot to follow its lead, it speaks up sooner and more often. A traitor blames the loyal player who was on a failed quest with it, joins in against the loyal the table doubts more than their share, and trusts a loyal player the table trusts; on Sharp it also accuses one of its own once the table suspects them as much as most (a word that costs them little and keeps it clear of them), on Easy it now and then defends one under fire instead. Accused, a player answers: back at the accuser when it doubts them (a traitor, mostly anyway), else, half the time, "not me"; the rest it lets pass, decided once so asking again changes nothing. Conferring, each traitor names the loyal player whose votes, parties and words best fit someone who knew the traitors (a vote against a party with a traitor on it, or for a clean one; leading a clean party; accusing a traitor, all the more before a failed quest showed them; not accusing a loyal player or trusting a traitor), and agrees, once, with another's pick it nearly made. The page words each line from its reason, with the player named after the verb, so "you" fits.

Between Sharp players alone, talking, over 400 games at each size from five to ten, the loyal win 44%, 35%, 26%, 28%, 30% and 28% with no Lookout, and 54%, 45%, 49%, 56%, 37% and 47% with the Lookout and the Pilot (the traitors finding the Lookout 42%, 50%, 41%, 33%, 39% and 30% of the time they get the chance); with the Lookout and no Pilot, 42%, 34% and 46% at five, seven and ten, the Lookout having nobody to hide behind. Easy against Easy at seven, 34% with the Lookout and the Pilot and 50% with no Lookout: on Easy the traitors fail every quest they can, and the Lookout blurts what it knows and is found. Until October 2026 the loyal won more with no Lookout than with one (79%, 74% and 61% at five, seven and ten): the computer traitors gave themselves away, voting for every party with one of their own on it, failing quests by their seats rather than by who could best take the blame, and leaving a partner the table had caught to hang alone, so the loyal won on the quests, and with no Lookout there was nothing to win back. Now the traitors play as above, the game with no Lookout is a hard one for the loyal, the Lookout, who knows the traitors, is what evens it, and the strike keeps it from being more than even. Getting there showed how a table of computer hands can be led. Counting every hand's word, a whole table agreeing with a traitor's first accusation buried a loyal player (at nine, with no Lookout, the loyal won 14% and named worse parties than chance would), so with no Lookout only the first few voices count; and with one threshold for every player, a little either way swung whole games (the same table of six won 41% or 58%), hence each player's temper, so a table doesn't move as one. Nine with the Lookout is still the hardest table for the loyal. Before the talk the same players won 49%, 40% and 52% with the Lookout: what the loyal say pools what each knows (a hand on a failed quest of two knows the other was the traitor), and the first weights, which read a traitor never accusing another as near proof, made the loyal win nearly every quest at ten (62%); the weights were lightened, and traitors given the habit of blaming the loyal player beside them on a failed quest and answering back, until the table was even again. An earlier version that told the traitors to refuse every clean party gave them away at once and lost nearly every quest; one that read a leader's own place on the party as a sign of guilt let them win nearly all. The tile counts a run of wins.
