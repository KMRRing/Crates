# Harbour
Plan, then run. A harbour of hexes, ships you place where you like, and a program for each: one instruction per hour. Ships steer as ships do, relative to where they're heading: ahead; port, a 60° turn to the left and a hex on; starboard, the same to the right; astern, a hex back still facing the same way; and load, discharge, wait. A ship can't turn on the spot, so quays sit on the channels round islands, and a dead end means backing out. A program written that way runs the same turned to any of the six directions, or mirrored with port and starboard swapped, so a group of ships and their programs can later be reused facing another way. Every program loops, the longest setting the loop for all of them, and they run in lockstep, so the whole plan is a machine you watch go round. A ship that would end on land or off the map runs aground; two that would end on one hex, or pass through each other, collide; either ends the run. A ship may follow another onto the hex it's leaving. Ships come in classes, the same in every level: a Schnigge holds 4 and hires at 20 mk (marks), a Cog holds 10 for 30 mk (cheaper for what it carries); each level's fleet offers so many of each, shown under the map. The sizes aren't multiples of each other on purpose: a ship of N ingots alloys in steps of 1/N (a Schnigge in quarters, a Cog in tenths), so a standard can suit one and not the other, and a fleet of both reaches totals neither does alone. Targets count ingots delivered. A quay works a parcel an hour, in full or not at all, and one that can't happen does nothing: a load needs the parcel on hand and room for it aboard; a discharge takes the quay's parcel out of the ship's alloy, every metal in its share, and needs at least that much aboard and the alloy up to standard (a refused cargo stays aboard, and the status line says why). What a discharge leaves stays aboard and alloys into the next load. A smelter's stockpile fills at its rate up to what its yard holds, and the smelter waits when it's full. The levels to come build their logic on the rule that an impossible transfer is no transfer, the way Opus Magnum's machines rest on an arm that can grab nothing. The aim is Opus Magnum's depth over time: stockpiles ships both fill and draw from, plans tested against several runs of arrivals, ship-to-ship transfers and tides, stamped and reusable modules, and assay tickets.

Harbour is the Hanseatic metal trade (2 November 2026, Korbi: "harbour: same", that is re-skinned for Hanseatic trade, "also the fuels need to switch to something more hanseatic appropiate. either gunpowder or metals. thinking metals."). Until then it was a game of liquid fuels, and his rule of 7 October, "keep Harbour within the liquid-fuels supply chain, no metals", is reversed: metals, not gunpowder. Only what things are called and drawn as changed. The rules, every number, every par and the plan that reaches it, the level and region ids, the files and every saved key (`harbour:v3:…`, `harbour:flat`, `harbour:sound`) are as they were, so a plan, a best or a campaign from before is still there, and `tests/harbour.mjs` holds the same pars under the new names. The code keeps its old words where changing them would be churn (a `jetty`, a `tank`, a `drop`, a `truck`; the header of `harbour-engine.js` says which is which) and says in its comments what each is now. The trade teaches what the fuels did, and a fuels trader reads it the same way: an alloy is a blend to a standard (bronze with at least 20% tin is B20), a smelter's stockpile is a tank that fills at a rate, a warehouse is a terminal whose throughput is what pays and whose spare room is rent, contracts are term deals beside spot orders, and the Zwin's lane to the Baltic is ARA's to the Baltic, the long haul that makes the Baltic's long-term contracts possible. Pewter's lead cap is the blend wall, a ceiling that pays for what's under it.

What was what. Metals: a level's one oil, diesel, gasoil or gasoline became a metal, so each level has its colour (copper in First cargo, Casting and Roundabout and the first product of Bell metal and Heels; iron in Up the creek, Trickle and Toll queue; lead in Two smelters and Lockstep; tin in Passing places and Pewter stamp), FAME became tin in Bell metal and calamine (the zinc ore that makes brass) in Heels, ethanol lead, and Trickle's cheaper oil bog iron (iron dug from the bogs). A blend is an alloy and a spec a standard: B20 became bronze with at least 20% tin (Bell metal, for the bell-founder), E10 pewter with at most 10% lead (Pewter stamp, the guild's mark) and Heels' second grade brass with at least 30% calamine. Places: a refinery or plant became a smelter, a terminal a warehouse, a tank a stockpile (a yard on a level's map; at a warehouse, room let in bays of 6), a jetty a quay and a customer a buyer (a founder, a smithy, a toll). Ships and carriers: a coaster is a Schnigge (a small Baltic coaster, plural Schniggen), a Handy a Cog (the Hanse's high-sided sailing ship) and an MR a hulk; a truck is a cart, a train a pack train on the winter trails, and a barge is still a barge. Money and score: thousands of dollars became marks (mk), the League's money of account, and a drop an ingot. The chart: Muuga became Reval, the name the League knew Tallinn by; the plant and ORLEN's ship from Klaipėda the smelter and Falun's copper in a ship from Stockholm; Tallinn, Rakvere and Tartu Pernau, Wesenberg and Dorpat; ARA the Zwin, the inlet to Bruges, its terminal at Sluis, its towns Rotterdam, Antwerp and Duisburg Bruges, Antwerp and Cologne, and its Houston ships Cornwall's tin from Fowey; North-West Europe's chapter Bruges and London, the world the League and the Straits the Sound. Levels: Rundown is Casting, First blend Bell metal, Two refineries Two smelters, Blend wall Pewter stamp and Queue Toll queue; the rest keep their names.

Any plan that delivers counts; tightening it is the second game. Four measures, each kept on its own: cost (ships hired, each at its class's rate, and metal lifted at its price), hours (to the last delivery), water (every hex a ship was on) and instructions (each written instruction once, a loop's body once however often it plays, empty hours free). They pull against each other, so no plan is best at all four. Each level carries a par on each measure and the plans that reach it (found by searching every plan of up to three ships on each level's routes, from every starting point); `tests/harbour.mjs` runs those plans and holds the par there, so a rule change can't quietly make a level easier, harder or impossible. A par met is an ingot (October 2026: what were a level's stars became drops, the regions' diesel; November 2026: a drop became an ingot, the regions' metal, below): a run's line marks each par it meets with an ingot, and Menu shows your best beside the par, an ingot after each one met. The plan that met a par is kept (under `harbour:v3:kept:` keys; a later run that does better on that measure replaces it), so an ingot in Menu is a button: tap it to watch the plan that earned it. Short of a par, a level keeps only your plan as you left it. An ingot earned before plans were kept is kept from your plan as it stands, if it still meets that par. Tap a par to watch the plan that reaches it. A plan watched, yours or the par's, takes your plan's place until Back to yours, runs and steps like yours, and you can pick and copy from it to paste into your own, but not change it, and running it keeps no bests; Use this plan makes it yours. The plans live with the levels in `harbour-levels.js`, written compactly (one character an hour, a loop as its count and body in brackets: `(2S)`), and the tests run those same plans.

The campaign comes in chapters, and a new player meets it a piece at a time (`harbour-campaign.js`, pure: the page tells it what's finished). Opening Harbour the first time puts you on First cargo, the picker showing the Baltic's chapter alone, every step after the first greyed with why ("Finish Up the creek"). A finish opens the next step, and a banner in the brief's place says so with a button to go there ("Up the creek is open. Go to Up the creek"). Casting finished opens the first region, the Baltic. Bruges and London open when the Baltic reaches its long-term contracts, which need more metal than the smelter and Falun can bring (so you build a warehouse on the Zwin, and its harbours come first: Bell metal, then Two smelters); Two smelters opens the Zwin, the League and Trickle, with Pewter stamp and Heels after Trickle; the Sound opens when the Baltic runs on its own. Menu's Continue goes to the first open step not done, passing over a region that's waiting on another (the Baltic in its long-term season with no lane bringing it the Zwin's tin). Menu lists each chapter reached, every step done (✓, a level's ingots beside it), here, open or locked and why, and under them a glimpse of the next chapter and what opens it. The campaign's progress lives under `harbour:v3:` keys: everyone started fresh when it came in, earlier plans and bests left unread, and the games screen's Settings › Start a game over clears them all (Menu's Start this region over clears one region). The cheat is the par: on a level, tap a par in Menu and Use this plan makes it yours (one edit, so Undo takes it back); in a region, tap the engine's par to watch the par run week by week, and Use this week makes the region the par's as it stands that week, its moves made, ready to play (the other regions and the lanes stay yours).

A region is a season board (`harbour-season.js`, the rules, pure; `harbour-season-ui.js`, the screen): one picture of the region, not to scale (the sea, your warehouse and its room, the smelter or smelters feeding it, the towns with a bubble of the ingots they want this week and a face for how last week went, the roads, winter trails and rivers with your carts, pack trains and barges on them, the ships at sea), this week's three offers, and Play the week. A tall screen shows more of the sea. Every week you pick one offer (a cart, a pack train or a barge into the yard, another bay of room rented, a supply ship hired, or a cheap cargo now), tap a town to send it carriers from the yard or call them back, and play the week: supply comes into the warehouse first, as much as it holds (the rest is turned away and not paid for), then contracts are served, then the lane is loaded, then spot orders, each town getting what its carriers take and the warehouse holds. A week with every town supplied in full earns a star; a contract left short costs one, and 2 mk an ingot. Stars win contracts: a town with one flies a flag (★2 deal), filled when your stars reach it (and, in the Baltic, what else it wants is there), and its sheet has Take it. A season is four weeks: spot orders, then medium contracts (spot orders half as big), then long-term ones, bigger and dearer to keep; an event comes once in a region's life (the fair at Pernau, ice in the gulf halving Falun's ship, the furnace relined and the smelter running at half; the fair at Bruges, the Rhine's low water halving barges, a furnace cracking). A region runs on its own, solved, when all its long-term contracts are held and kept on what comes in each week, not on stock that will run out. The Baltic's contracts want more than stars (October 2026): its medium contracts want 6 of its harbours' 12 ingots (2 of 4 a harbour on average) or the Zwin's tin by the League's lane, and its long-term ones 8 (2.5 a harbour) and the lane both, however much else comes in; the reason is the metal, since bronze and pewter need tin and the Baltic has none. So a player with few ingots takes the medium contracts once the Zwin's lane runs, and finishing the Baltic takes the Zwin and the puzzles both. A contract's button says what it still needs ("Needs 6 ingots or the Zwin", "Needs the Zwin and 8 ingots"), its town's sheet says what its contracts want and what the Baltic has, and its flag fills only when it can be taken. Running on its own also wants the lane's cargo landing that week; a week that would have done it otherwise says what it's short of. Its engine is what it then makes a week (the next four weeks averaged, as everything stands), against a par.

A region's score is its net a week (October 2026): the header shows last week's net, what the week took in less what it bought, hire, rent and penalties, and not a balance that grew with every week played, which rewarded simply playing on. Room beyond the warehouse's own 12 is rented as bays, 3 a week for each 6 more, so room pays only while it's used: everything comes into the warehouse before it goes out, so a warehouse wants room for a week's throughput (its terminal's, a fuels trader would say) and no more, and room beyond that is rent and stock bought only to sit in it (a free bay was once worth picking every week). The warehouse's sheet shows the room and its rent and gives a bay back, when what's in the warehouse still fits without it; a carrier left in the yard costs nothing, so a week whose offers add nothing can take one rather than a bay.

The harbours feed the regions, an ingot a week for every ingot earned in them (October 2026; it was the pace of one harbour's fastest plan, which was hard to read): every par met in the Baltic's three harbours (First cargo, Up the creek, Casting) is an ingot a week from the smelter into Reval, twelve at most, and every par met in Bruges and London's five an ingot a week from the smelters into Sluis (eight at most when the Zwin opens, after Bell metal and Two smelters; Trickle, Pewter stamp and Heels add theirs as they're played, twenty in all). The Sound's ingots wait for their own region. That's the reason to go back to a puzzle: on eight ingots a week the Baltic's first season ends with ★3, on six with ★2, a star short of Wesenberg's contract. The warehouse's sheet lists its harbours with their ingots and a button to each with pars still to meet, and a run that earns an ingot says what it now feeds. With eleven of its twelve ingots the smelter and Falun alone would bring the 19 a week its long-term contracts want, which is why the Baltic asks for the Zwin's lane outright. The Baltic: Reval holds 12 (6 more for each bay rented); Pernau and Wesenberg by road (a cart carries 6 and 4 a week, hiring at 1.5), Dorpat by road (2) or by the winter trails (a pack train, 6 a week at 2.5); Falun's ship from Stockholm, 8 a week at 2.1–3.2 an ingot and 4 to hire; medium contracts 6, 4 and 3 a week (★2, ★2, ★3), long-term 9, 6 and 4 (★5, ★5, ★6). The Zwin: Sluis holds 12; Bruges by road, Antwerp by road or barge, Cologne by barge up the Rhine (a barge 6 or 8 a week at 2); Cornwall's ships from Fowey, 10 a week each, two at most; long-term contracts 6, 8 and 4 a week.

Every region runs on the company's clock: a week played in one plays every region you've opened, importers first, so a cargo lands before the next one loads, and a solved region earns while you play elsewhere. The world (`harbour-world-ui.js`) is the League, a chart of the North Sea and the Baltic drawn by hand from an atlas, with your regions lit, the ports still to come marked (Bergen, Novgorod, Lübeck, Visby, Danzig, London), and the lanes between yours: the Zwin to the Baltic, up the North Sea, round Skagen and through the Sound. Its hulks (8 ingots a week each, 3 a week to hire, two at most) load the Zwin's tin after its contracts are served and land it at Reval the week after, booked at 2.5 an ingot; a cargo that doesn't fit Reval's room waits aboard. With the lane running, the Baltic's long-term contracts can be kept, and the Baltic is solved; so is the Zwin, its lane counted as one more promise. Each region's card shows its season, stars and last week's net, or its engine once solved. The par run is the intended path, 22 company weeks played by a player with eight of the Baltic's twelve ingots and seven of the eight Bell metal and Two smelters offer (the Baltic's two seasons alone, then the Zwin from the week it opens, the lane, both regions solved, each engine tidied), renting two bays in each region, the room that makes most: the Baltic is solved in its 14th week, the Zwin in its 13th, engines 25.1 and 21.3 a week. `tests/harbour.mjs` holds the par run to those numbers, checks what the Baltic's contracts want (six ingots or the lane for a medium one, the lane and eight ingots for a long-term one, stars first), that without the lane even all twelve ingots can't take its long-term contracts, nor seven with it, while eight take all three and it runs on its own, and that a week kept with no lane cargo landing, or on seven ingots, isn't solved and says why; that the par's room is the best size in both regions (a bay fewer or more makes less a week, once its stock has settled); that rented bays cost their rent and go back only when what's in the warehouse fits; and the rules case by case and the campaign's gates.

The levels, chapter by chapter. The Baltic, learning to move: Level 1, First cargo: sixteen ingots from the smelter's quay (L) to the founder's (D), four Schnigge loads, round an island. One Schnigge is cheapest (20 mk, 43 hours); four, spaced so none reaches a quay another is still at, are fastest (par 13 hours). Level 2, Up the creek: the ironworks' quay (L) is at the head of a creek too narrow to turn in, so a ship backs out (Astern keeps its heading), and the smithy (D) is on the shore straight on from it. One Schnigge shuttling, ahead to D and astern home, never turns at all: cheapest, least water, fewest instructions (par 20 mk, 7 tiles, 4 instructions). Faster are two Schniggen on that same line: each backs from the ironworks down the shore to the smithy and comes home ahead, ducking into the ring at the creek's mouth for an hour (starboard, astern, port) while the other backs past (par 37 hours, found by the level lab); the creek holds one ship at a time, so a third only gets in the way. Level 3, Casting (a stockpile's rhythm: two ships taking turns): the smelter's stockpile fills an ingot an hour and holds ten, and both quays work two an hour; twenty-four ingots within forty hours. No ship can do it alone (the best is 49 hours): the cheapest answer is two Schniggen on one loop, half a turn apart, taking turns at the stockpile, one lifting while the other delivers (40 mk, 39 hours, the cheapest of 174 two-ship fleets that make it); three Schniggen are quickest (par 31 hours). Bruges and London, learning to trade and alloy: Level 4, Bell metal: bronze for the bell-founder, who takes two ingots an hour with at least 20% tin; copper (C) lifts two at 1 mk an ingot, tin (T) one at 5 mk; twenty ingots. A Schnigge's four can't be 20% tin with those lifts (its best up to standard is half tin); a Cog's ten can, exactly: eight copper and two tin, five parcels up to standard. So the bigger ship is the cheaper plan (par 66 mk): the size that suits the standard alloys to it without giveaway. A Schnigge's richest cheap try, two copper and one tin with its heel topped up every lap, is up to standard but gives away tin (69 mk); tin is priced at 5 mk so that the giveaway outweighs the Schnigge's lower hire (at 3, the level lab found, the Schnigge won). Fewest instructions is a Schnigge hauling pure tin and never visiting the copper quay: dear and slow, and seven instructions. Level 5, Two smelters: the near smelter (N) is closing with 12 ingots left in its stockpile, the far one (F) casts two an hour, and forty ingots are wanted within 100 hours. The long haul wants the Cog and the near lap a Schnigge: the other way round takes 111 hours, the Cog alone 117, two Schniggen 122 (par 50 mk; three ships, 56 hours). The expert's Schnigge does a near lap and a far run every loop, and keeps earning after the near stock runs dry. Level 6, Trickle (found by the level lab): two sources, bog iron (B) beside the smithy (D) at 1 mk an ingot, one a lift, that trickles into its yard half an ingot an hour, and the ironworks (L) down the channel selling iron at 2 mk, four a lift; the smithy takes four an hour; thirty ingots. Every measure is won a different way. One Schnigge that never leaves B and D, two hexes side by side, is cheapest (par 52 mk, 2 tiles). A Cog parked at the trickle takes four instructions. The fastest plan pairs a Schnigge working the trickle with the Cog hauling dear iron from L (par 35 hours), and no fleet of three or four does better. Level 7, Pewter stamp: the pewterers' stamp takes tin with at most 10% lead, the blend wall; tin (T) lifts three at 2 mk an ingot, and lead (P), up a creek, one a lift, its premium paying 4 mk an ingot; thirty ingots. Pure tin is the easy plan and keeps the hours, water and instructions pars; the cheapest is a Cog carrying exactly one lead and nine tin, by way of the creek (par 72 mk): the premium on lead costs time. Level 8, Heels: two buyers, A pure copper and B brass with at least 30% calamine, twelve ingots each (a buyer can set its own standard, and a plan must meet both). A Schnigge for each grade works (76 mk); a Cog delivering six pure to A, then alloying the four left aboard up with two calamine for B, is cheapest (par 62 mk, 41 hours). The other way round, the calamine left aboard is refused at A. The Sound, mastering timing: Level 9, Roundabout (found by the level lab): a ring round a one-hex island, both berths dead ends off it, the smelter's quay (L) lifting two an hour and the founder's (D) taking four; sixteen ingots. One Schnigge takes 50 hours, its cheapest plan backing out of each berth with a duck on the way (par 20 mk, 10 instructions). Two take 27. The golden plan is a carousel: three Schniggen on one route, five hours apart, each backing out of a berth and swinging round the island the same way, one holding an hour so the gaps fit, a berth coming free just as the next ship comes in (par 23 hours; 196 of the 5.8 million three-ship fleets that fit reach it). Level 10, Toll queue (drawn in the level lab): the ironworks (L) fills a ship an ingot an hour, the toll (D) takes four; one channel between them with room to pass halfway and a little basin at each end; twenty-four ingots. One Schnigge takes 146 hours, two 70. The golden plan is three ships on three routes, queueing in the basins while the slow berth works, ducking aside and passing at the wide spot (par 58 hours; 2 of the 2,164 three-ship fleets that fit reach it); a fourth can't help. Level 11, Passing places (drawn in the level lab): one channel between the smelter (L) and the founder (D), both two ingots an hour, with three wider stretches; twelve ingots. One Schnigge shuttles, astern one way and ahead the other (47 hours); two take 30; three take 25, each in turn ducking aside into a wide stretch and holding four hours while the others go by (par 25 hours; 8 of the 12,110 three-ship fleets that fit reach it). Level 12, Lockstep (drawn in the level lab): one straight channel, the smelter (L) at one end and the founder (D) at the other, a hex to spare past each; twelve ingots. One Schnigge shuttles in 42 hours. Two can't pass anywhere, so they move as a pair: at each end one works the quay while the other waits on the spare hex beside it, then both step together so the second gets its turn (par 35 hours; 8 of the 160 two-ship fleets that fit reach it). A third has nowhere to step.

The harbour is drawn in 3D (`harbour-3d.js`, with three.js kept in `vendor/` like every other file, so it plays offline): hex tiles, the quays' warehouses and yards that fill, a few gabled houses, little cogs under sail carrying their cargo in its metal's colour (set once in `harbour.css`, which the flat map reads too, so the two agree and the themes and night follow), gliding from hour to hour. It wears the suite's theme: Codex a chart in brass and navy, Modern an architect's white model, Kontor an inked drawing office (at night a green trace on black), Office flat light greys and Fluent's blue (at night charcoal and navy), each by day and by night, and at night windows and quay lamps are lit and ships carry a stern lantern. Where a device can't draw in 3D, or when Menu says so, the harbour is drawn flat from above (`harbour-flat.js`); both answer the same calls, so the game doesn't know which it has. Under the map is the fleet: tap a class to make the next ship that class, or, with a ship picked, to swap it to that class if one's left. Tap water to put a ship there, facing east; tap it again to turn it 60° to starboard; drag it to move it, onto land to scrap it. Pick an instruction from the tools and tap a program's hours to paint it (the same instruction again clears an hour). The square at the left of the tools switches to picking: tap an hour, then another, and everything between is picked, across ships too (a ship's number picks its whole row; with a mouse, drag or shift-click). Then Copy and Paste (pasting goes in before the picked hour, shifting the rest later), Insert empty hours, Delete, Loop, Back (copies the picked hours reversed with port and starboard swapped: the way home, once the ship has turned round), and the two arrows, which shift the picked rows an hour earlier or later inside the loop and keep going while held. A whole route pasted into an empty ship's row at hour h becomes the same loop h − 1 hours later, so a second ship on the route is one paste.

A loop plays its hours more than once. Loop makes the picked hours play twice; tap the count (×2) for one more pass, hold it (or right-click) for one fewer. Hold an instruction (right-click, with a mouse) and it becomes a loop of its own, that hour played twice. The tape still shows every hour, the body boxed and its later passes faded, so the timing between ships stays in view; painting a faded hour paints the body, and deleting faded hours takes passes away. Loops don't nest. The edits live in `harbour-tape.js` and are tested in `tests/harbour.mjs`.

A tool can also be dragged onto the programs: onto an hour it takes that hour's place; onto the edge between two hours (an hour's outer quarters and the gap) it goes in there for that ship, the hours after it shifting later; onto the hour numbers between two hours it goes in for every ship, a new column squeezed in. A finger aims a little above itself (2.1rem, about a row of hours), so the mark showing where the tool would go (an outlined hour, or a line between two) sits just over the fingertip rather than under it, and the tool floats a row higher still, clear of the mark: a line between two hours, a cell's width from the next, can only be placed if it can be seen (October 2026). A mouse aims with its tip. Each drop is one edit for Undo, and a tap still just picks the tool. The pen in the tools draws routes: with it on, drag on the map and the picked ship's route goes on from the dot where its program leaves it, an hour a hex (Ahead, Port, Starboard or Astern, whichever reaches it; a hex that would need a 120° turn is skipped, so draw a smoother line, and going back over the last hex takes that step off), added to its program on release, one edit for Undo. While you plan, every ship's route over a loop is drawn on the water from its own program (a ship never decides, so its route doesn't depend on any other ship). Under the tools: Undo and Redo, back to the start, one hour, run or pause, and 1×, 4× or 16× speed. A run keeps every hour it has played: the scrubber above the controls goes back to any of them and forward again as far as you've watched, B steps back an hour, and a crash is marked in both ships' programs at its hour; tap it for the hour before, where the status line says what's coming. Sound is made on the device (a horn leaving a quay, an iron clank for a load, a ship's bell for a delivery, a thud for a refusal, the sea under a run), and Menu turns it off. Any edit goes back to the start. On a computer, Ctrl or Cmd with C, X, V, A and Z copy, cut, paste, pick everything and undo; Delete deletes the picked hours, Escape stops picking; space runs and pauses, S steps, R goes back. Each level keeps its own plan and bests on the device, under `harbour:v3:` keys. Harbour took Survey's place among the first tiles on the games screen.

A game stands still while you're in its menu, its rules or the games screen (`menu.js`: `onPause(pause, resume)`, resume told how long it stood): Crates', Slate's and Delta's board clocks stop, a puzzle rush's clock stops and its start moves on by the pause, Pipes' oil holds where it is, Spot freezes, a running Harbour stops and runs on after, and Cartel's computer waits with its next move. A duo match's shared clock doesn't stop.

Flags are written as emoji (🇮🇪 is two "regional indicator" letters), and some systems, Windows above all, have no flag pictures and show the letters ("IE"). `flags.js` checks on every page whether the system draws a flag; where it doesn't, a font of just the flags (Twemoji Country Flags, MIT, 78 KB in `fonts/`) goes in front of the page's own font for the flag letters only. Systems that draw flags, like the iPhone, keep their own.

Vision, settled (its examples were the fuels trade's until November 2026; its structure and its design rules are as they were). The campaign is a chart of the League's real trade, as far-flung as the Hanse's own, not a list of regions. It starts with micro-puzzles (one harbour, one rhythm, one clever timing) and grows by layers, each built from the one below: a chain (harbours linked by voyages, with a smelter converting ore between them), a network (chains sharing ships, warehouses and contracts), the League (everything running for a season, each solved harbour a lit diorama on the chart). The rules stay the same at every scale (hours in a harbour, days at sea). Each layer brings one idea from real trade, taught first as a micro-puzzle: aggregation of many small origins (osmund iron from Sweden's many small forges, wax and furs gathered at Novgorod), conversion with yields (ore to ingot with its slag, copper and calamine to brass), laycans (date windows to load: the shipping season, opening at Easter and closing with the autumn gales), seasons (ice closing the gulf, the herring fair at Skanör drawing every ship, low water on the Rhine), decay in transit (iron rusting in a wet hold, charcoal turning to dust), production when the wind blows (the water wheel that drives a bellows or a hammer stopping in a dry summer or a hard frost: where seeded randomness comes in), certification (a bar struck with Falun's own mark against metal melted together from many works), a metal's value set by where it's delivered (the staple, a town's right to make goods stop and be sold there, and the tolls on the way: arbitrage as routing), and chokepoints with timing (the Sound's toll at Elsinore, the locks of the Stecknitz canal that joined Lübeck to the Elbe, the ice leaving the gulf). Design rules, settled. Ships never decide: every program plays exactly as written, hour by hour, whatever happens around it, and the only thing that depends on the harbour is whether a transfer happens. That keeps a plan readable as clockwork and keeps the timing puzzles alive (Casting is timing ships to a stockpile). Conditions come later, and only with randomness, when they're needed: as equipment placed in the harbour and paid for, never as a free instruction. Lighthouses, gates, meters and signals that hold ships or smelters until something is so, every hour of holding costing demurrage. Pure plans stay possible beside them, and the measures choose between them. Randomness, when it comes, is seeded: a level runs a fixed set of test runs, so every plan replays identically and every score can be verified.
