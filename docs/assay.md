# Assay
Wordle's genre, by its rules (October 2026). A hidden word; every guess must itself be a word, and each of its letters is marked: verdigris in place, ochre elsewhere in the word, the ink's warm grey not in it. A repeated letter is marked exactly as Wordle marks it: the letters in place first, then elsewhere for as many as the word has left, the rest grey (KEBAB against ABBEY: the middle B in place, the last elsewhere; GEESE against THOSE: one E, in place, the other two grey). Six guesses for five letters. A word not in the list, or of the wrong length, shakes and costs nothing. The keyboard under the board shows what each letter has shown, Enter at its left and Delete at its right as on a phone's own; a key answers as the finger goes down, and a hardware keyboard works too. The rules, the words, the levels and the daily are `assay-engine.js`'s, the computer players `assay-solve.js`'s, the team's table `assay-table.js`'s, all pure; the page is `assay.js`.

The words (`assay-words.js`, English: the engine asks only for a lexicon of that shape, its keyboard, what it accepts and what it keeps as answers, so French or German can follow with a list and a keyboard of its own). Answers are everyday words: the three commonest tiers of `wordlist.js` (SCOWL sizes 10 to 35), a spelling both sides of the Atlantic share, and a word in its own right, never a form of another. Regular forms go by rule: plurals and third persons in -s, -es and -ies, pasts in -ed and -ied (a doubled consonant included), -ing forms, a comparative or superlative with its partner beside it (nicer and nicest), and -ves, -men and Latin plurals; a short list keeps what the rules mistake (seed is not see+d, thing not th+ing, a genus no plural) and nouns that are only ever plural (jeans, pliers). Irregular forms (came, taught, geese) and words that make poor answers (crude ones and neighbours of slurs the word list's own filter missed, jargon, archaic and informal spellings, names in lower case) are on a list. That leaves 1,386 answers of four letters, 1,873 of five, 2,402 of six and 2,294 of seven. Any word of the length in the widest list is a guess. Slate's dictionary, which marks every form of another word, checks the rules: no four- or five-letter answer is only a form there.

How hard a word is. The measure is how many guesses a human-style player needs, not how rare the word is: one who opens with a word people open with (SLATE, CRANE, RAISE…), then guesses only words that could still be the answer, the commoner ones first and those of different letters rather than repeats, played 32 times from a seed made from the word itself (integers only, so it comes out the same on every phone). A word in a large family that differs in one letter takes this player many guesses, a rare word does too (it comes to mind late), and so does a repeated letter; a word that splits the field cleanly takes few. It finds the genre's known traps: HATCH, LATCH, MOUND, JOKER and SHAVE grade among the hardest, RAISE, EARTH, HEART and COURT among the easiest. Grading every word takes a minute, so it's done ahead: `node assay-grades.mjs` writes `assay-grades.js`, and the test fails, saying so, once the word list or the answer rules have changed since (meanwhile the levels go by how common a word is, at once, never a phone grading thousands of words). The levels, quantiles of each length's own grades: Easy, a word of the two commonest tiers no harder than the middle; Medium, neither among the easiest fifth nor the hardest tenth; Hard, the hardest quarter. For five letters that's 634, 1,359 and 475 words, which the human-style player solves in 3.3, 3.8 and 4.4 guesses on average.

Lengths, four to seven letters. The same measure says four is the hard length (whole families differ in one letter: BAKE, CAKE, FAKE, LAKE, MAKE; the human-style player needs 4.8 guesses and runs out of six one game in seven), so it gets a seventh guess; six and seven letters give more away with every guess (3.4 and 3.0 guesses on average) and keep six.

The rules (Settings): Free; Hard, Wordle's hard mode (a letter found in place stays there, and every letter shown to be in the word is used, as often as shown); Strict (every guess could be the word, so a letter shown elsewhere moves and a letter shown out stays out). A guess they forbid is turned away with the reason ("The 2nd letter must be R", "Use the E", "There's no C"). A change of rules takes effect at once on a word not yet guessed at, else on the next.

Today's word: five letters, Medium, one board, six guesses, the same for everyone (the suite's day, the UTC date). It's the next in one fixed shuffle of Medium's words, counted in days from 20 October 2026, so no word comes back for three and a half years. It counts the moment its last guess lands: `assay:daily` keeps each day's guesses (7 for a word not found), `assay:best` the fewest, `assay:streak` the days solved in a row (a day missed or lost ends it). The tile opens on it while it's still to play (`#today`), asking before it leaves a word you've started. Hard or Strict may be on for it; Copy the grid marks that with an asterisk, as Wordle does.

Modes (the menu's Content), each with the length and level beside it: One word; Boards, two, four or eight words at once, every guess played on each, with Dordle's, Quordle's and Octordle's 7, 9 and 13 guesses (several boards always play free, since each board's hints would contradict the others'); Dodge, Absurdle's rule, where no word is chosen: each guess is marked to keep as many words in play as it can (ties to the marks that show least), the strip says how many are left, and it's beaten only by leaving it one word and guessing that, the fewest guesses best; Sprint, as many words as you can in three minutes, six guesses each, a word solved or spent moving on at once. Weighed and left out: lengths past seven (they test recall more than deduction), Fibble's lies and Xordle's twin words (frustration more than reasoning), and sixteen or more boards (no phone holds them).

Levels (November 2026, "levels like dominos": the same ladder as Topple's, Survey's and Bridges'). The menu's Content has Play: Levels or Free play, and in Levels the ladder, three to a row under eight chapters: First words, Long words, The rules (Hard and Strict), Boards, The dodging word, Sprints, Primes and Equations, 41 levels in all (`assay-levels.js`, pure). A level is a recipe, not one word: a game (words, primes or equations), a mode, a length, how hard the answers are and a rule (Boards play free, as everywhere). The first time it is the same word for everyone (the level's own seed, so the first Warm-up is one word on every phone, and no two levels begin with the same word); a new word of the level after that, or from New word, so a level can be played again for its stars. Each level is a miniature of its board (`assay-mini.js`: the tiles' marks are a fixed picture, never its word; the dodging word's last row is dashed, a sprint is a clock), with its number, stars, name and what it plays. Stars are for the guesses: one for solving it, two within a guess of the par, three within the par (the dodging word allows two more for two stars; a sprint counts the words solved instead). A par is the middle score of the Steady rival over 24 words of the recipe (measured, then set by hand in `LEVELS`): never under four on one word, since three guesses is luck, never nearer the guess limit than a guess to spare, and a sprint's about one and a half times what Steady solves in three minutes, since a person types faster than it thinks. Beating a level (one star) opens the next; any open level can be played at any time, as it was left, and the header's Level button (shown while one is being played) opens the menu to change. The level's rules are its own, so the menu's Rules setting is hidden in Levels; the rival and High contrast still apply. A level's game doesn't count in Free play's stats, only in its stars (the best it has had); a finished level offers Next level, Another word and the grid. Someone who has played Assay before carries on in Free play (the daily among it, which the games screen's tile still opens); anyone new begins on the ladder. Kept under `assay:`: `track` (levels or free), `camp` (the level being played and the stars won in each) and `lruns` (each level's game as it was left, so a partner watching and another phone see it).

Numbers (November 2026, "more Wordle clones and variations"). The menu's Game chooses Words, Primes or Equations, each a lexicon of the engine's kind (what can be typed, what is accepted as a guess, what may be the answer, and now also what its pieces are called, how a key is drawn, why a guess is refused and, for numbers, which answers a level means), so every mode, the rules, the rival, the stats and the ladder work on them with no second engine. They are played alone, in Free play and on the ladder: the daily, the team's table and the race stay words. Primes (`assay-numbers.js`): the hidden number is a prime of four or five digits (1,061 and 8,363 of them) and every guess must be a prime too, so each guess is a real question about primes; Easy has no digit repeated, Hard has one (the marks of a repeat are the least obvious), Medium is all; seven guesses for four digits, six for five, as for words of those lengths. Equations, Nerdle's idea: a sum that balances, six to eight characters of digits, + − × ÷ and one =, with a whole number alone on the right ("12+34=46", "6/4*2=3"); a guess must balance too, by the usual order of operations with exact fractions on the way (6÷4×2 is 3, not 2.5 cut short), no number with a leading zero, nothing negative on the right; six guesses. Answers leave out the trivial (a zero added, a one multiplied: "1*1+0=1" is a fair guess but never the answer), 206, 5,749 and 16,548 of them at six, seven and eight characters, in `assay-equations.js` (written by `node assay-equations.mjs`, some 200 kB, fetched by a dynamic import the first time a game of them is begun, with the busy sign saying so; the page never imports it, so word players never download it). Equations' levels are kinds of sum, not rarity (there is no commoner equation): Easy only + and − with at most two signs, Medium has a ×, Hard a ÷ or three signs or more. The keyboard is the digits over two rows (Primes) or the digits and the signs (Equations); a physical keyboard's x or × and ÷ are taken as the signs; signs are drawn as × ÷ − and kept as * / -. What's kept hides a game's answer as it does a word's, a digit moving along 0 to 9 and a sign along + − × ÷ (an = stays), different at every place. A game keeps its lexicon's id (`lang`), so a saved game, a level and its stats (kept apart from words') find the right one; the rival, which has no best opening for these, opens with the guess among a fixed sample that splits a fixed field best. The ladder's chapters Primes (five levels) and Equations (eight) end it, their pars set from Steady's middle score as the others were.

The rival (Settings): the computer plays the same game beside you, its latest marks showing in the strip as it goes (its words are shown at the end), at four levels that solve differently rather than roll dice. Novice forgets what's elsewhere (it plays on what's in place and what's out); Steady is the human-style player that grades the words; Sharp guesses the possible word that splits the rest best (the most information); Master may guess any word that splits them best, one that can't be the answer included, and closes on one when it can. On Medium five-letter words they take 4.8 guesses (Novice loses about one in seven), 3.9, 3.4 and 3.4; on four boards Novice solves about one game in four and the others every one; in a sprint they manage 1.6, 3.4, 5.6 and 8.2 words. Each guess comes after a person's thinking time (a word takes Novice about a minute and a half, Master twenty seconds), so fewer guesses win and equal guesses go to the quicker. The record against each level is kept (`assay:rivals`). A rival's whole game is worked out when yours starts (it doesn't depend on yours), so a reload and a partner watching see the same; its clock is yours, standing still while the menu, the rules or the games screen is open.

Together, two ways. The pair, from the partner sheet, plays as a team: one word for the table, the guesses taken in turns (whoever's turn it is, or anyone while that player's phone is away, so nobody's sleeping phone holds the table up), and the colours split: one of you sees what's in place, the other what's elsewhere, and neither sees what's out, which is only what neither of you has lit, so you have to say what you see. A letter that's your partner's to see is a dashed tile, never a grey one; the roles swap with each word; while someone types, the others see the letters come, with the typist's initial; and each of you can strike letters out on your own keyboard (Notes). Measured: after the same three guesses the pair has 2.5 words left, the one seeing what's in place alone 110, the other 22, and a solver given one colour alone needs about seven guesses where the two together need four, running out of six about half the time. Three or more share the three kinds round the table, the third seeing what's out. Once a word is over everyone sees everything; the pair's record keeps the team's best, the fewest guesses. Or a race (`duel.js`): the same game for everyone at the table, in the dealer's mode, length and level, each on their own phone; the fewest guesses win, then the quicker (a sprint, the most words). A Party plays the race, up to six. Either kind can be swapped for the other from the menu (Race instead, Play as a team instead): the swapping phone deals the other kind into the room's branch and the others' phones follow it there. Your own game, its stats and today's stay as they were. The computer doesn't sit at a table; the rival is for playing alone.

On the screen: the board's tiles size themselves to their box (container units), so nothing changes size while a game is played, and they grow on an upright tablet and held sideways, where the boards take the left column and the strip, the line and the keyboard stand at the right, the keys low. Several boards stand side by side or in rows, whichever gives the larger tile among those whose tiles still fit across at a legible size; where they don't fit, the box scrolls down, never across (four boards on the smallest phone, eight on any phone; held sideways, eight fit). Checked at 390×600, 390×664 and 390×844, on an iPad both ways, in Codex, Modern, Kontor and Office by day and night, played with taps (touch events), a mouse and a keyboard, and a pair and a table of three played through on the stand-in database.

The keyboard (1 November 2026; Korbi: "sometimes when typing quickly the letters don't come through, not sure if hitbox or entry related"). It was the hitbox. Keys answer as the finger goes down, but each key listened for its own touch, and the gaps between keys (a seventh of a key's width across, and about a tenth of its height down) and the strip above the top row heard nothing: a fifth of the taps a thumb makes over the keyboard land there when it goes fast. Chrome moves such a tap to the nearest key itself, which is why a test in Chrome never showed it, but Safari doesn't. One listener on the keyboard now gives a tap in a gap, or within half a rem of a key's edge, to the nearest key (`keyAt` in `assay.js`); the key lights as it is pressed, two fingers at once each press their own, and a key held down does not type again when it is let go (the click that follows a tap is passed over; a click without one is a keyboard's Enter or Space on a focused key). Typing a letter used to redraw every tile of every board, 480 on eight boards; it now redraws just the row being typed in and the line under the board (eight boards, the median letter: 2 ms on a desktop and 10 ms with the processor slowed six times, was 6 and 29), so a run of quick taps never waits on the one before. Crude words may be guessed (Korbi, 1 November 2026: "often used as opener", then "crude words are allowed in original wordle so let's include them here too (guesses not answers is good)"). The shared word list (`wordlist.js`) leaves them out, so a guess of PENIS, FUCK or BOOBS was turned away; 171 of them, four to seven letters, are now in `assay-crude.js` and accepted as guesses, never as answers (answers come from the commonest words, and `tests/assay.mjs` checks that none is ever one). They are sexual, bodily and profane words and insults, picked by hand from two profanity lists (naughty-words and profane-words) and, for five letters, from the list of words the original Wordle accepts (taken from its source: tabatkins/wordle-list), keeping real words and leaving misspellings and brand names out. Slurs against a people, a faith or a sexuality are not in it, though the original accepts them: a team's guesses are shown to the partner, and a slur typed there would show on their screen. Strict play turns a crude guess away like any word that can't be the answer; Hard accepts it where it fits.

The rules (October 2026) are a sheet of their own, from the header's Rules or the menu's The rules, as the other games' are: in plain prose, what the marks mean (TRACE against CRANE worked through, and GEESE against THOSE for a repeated letter), the guesses each length gets, the keys, Hard and Strict, the modes, the rival, today's word and playing together. Its numbers are read from the engine as it opens, so the sheet can't drift from the game, and the clock and the rival stand still while it's open, as for the menu. Looked at on a phone (390×664, 390×844) and an iPad both ways, in Codex, Modern and Kontor by day and night.

What's kept, under `assay:`: `run` (your game as it stands: the guesses, the draft being typed, the rival's game, the time played), `prefs`, `stats` (by mode, length and level: played, solved, the streak, how many guesses each took; a sprint's and the dodging word's best), `daily`, `best`, `streak`, `rivals`, `seen` (answers dealt lately, left for later), `notes` (yours, at a team's table) and `duel` (a race's run, kept apart from yours). A partner watching sees your game as it stands; a watching frame runs no clock. Where answers are kept (a game, a table, a race's match) they're shifted letter by letter, so a glance gives nothing away.

`tests/assay-numbers.mjs` (under a second) checks the number games: the registry, the primes (their counts, easy and hard, what a guess must be), equations by worked examples (the order of operations, a half, a zero divide, leading zeros, a second =, a negative or a sum on the right), the shorter answer lists against what the generator makes now, every length and level having answers, the Steady rival playing every mode of both games legally, the dodging word over the largest pool, the hidden answers, and the page fetching the equation file only when wanted.

`tests/assay-levels.mjs` (a fifth of a second) checks the levels: every recipe a game the engine can deal, the chapters in order, each par leaving a guess to spare, a level's own word the same every time and in its pool (and different from every other level's), the stars to the guess at every boundary (one word, boards, the dodging word, a sprint), the Steady rival playing every level through the real rules, the miniatures, and the page keeping the ladder in `assay:camp`, `assay:lruns` and `assay:track`.

`tests/assay.mjs` (under two seconds) checks the marks, repeated letters included, against worked examples and the solvers' quick marking against 3,000 pairs; the hard and strict rules and their words; the answers (sizes, no forms of others, Slate's dictionary agreeing, nothing crude, every answer a guess); the kept grades against grades worked out now, the levels' order, the traps and the clean splitters, and levels by commonness when the grades are stale; the daily (the same word for a day, worked out again by a fresh process as another phone would, none twice in 400 days, always Medium); every mode played through, a game read back from storage, deals the same from a seed; the openers and the four rivals' order and that the stronger three solve every word; and the team's split (the parts make the whole, neither part alone narrows it as far) at a table through the database's own round trip.

Not done: the team game is one board of one word (its colours split across several boards would be more to hold than to play); a rival can't sit at a table; the share grid is Wordle's squares, plain text.
