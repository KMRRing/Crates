# Party
A party is a room for the evening, each player on their own phone (October 2026): for when three, four or ten of you sit round a table, as on Korbi's trip to Hong Kong. Whoever opens it (Invite players) is its host and shows its QR code; the others scan it with their camera and sit down in their browser with a name: no app to install, no codes to type, no pairing (README, Tables). The host chooses what the party plays and starts it, and every phone follows; the others don't choose. A party plays any game that fits how many are in it: Party's own games (below), played on Party's page, where every phone shows its own player what's theirs (a word, a role, a vote to cast) and the host's phone runs the game and speaks for it, a narrator in the middle of the table, each step said aloud in the phone's own voice and written on every screen in a strip under the header tinted in Party's turquoise; the card, dice and board games (Liar's dice, Big Two, Dou Dizhu, Gin rummy, Cribbage, Banquet, Backgammon, Trumps, Contract bridge, Company, Dominoes, Cartel), each on its own page at a table in the party's room, the computer in the seats nobody has (README, Seats); and every game with a duo match: Crates, Xiangqi and Chess for two; Slate, Delta, Punt, Chart, Quote, Spot, Stow, Origin, Order and Manifest for up to six, everyone on their own phone (October 2026; until then all but Guandan and Mahjong were for exactly two); Guandan and Mahjong for up to four, the computer in the seats nobody has; and the head-to-heads on one deal (Chess's puzzle rush, Survey, Pipes: README, Head to head) for up to six. How each plays at a table of more than two is in its section: in Chart, Quote and Punt everyone answers the same question on their own phone, and after each the reveal shows what everyone did and the standings; Slate, Delta, Origin and Order are one board or line for the team, anyone playing it; Stow takes turns round the table; Spot and Manifest are either, against each other or one team. So four can choose Who am I?, Dominoes, Cartel or Guandan, but not Dou Dizhu (three seats) or Crates (two).

Starting a game on another page writes its table in the party's room first (`tableFrom` in `party-core.js`: everyone in the party seated as they sit in it, two at a four-seat table across from each other as partners, each marked away until their phone sits down, so one who never comes is played for after the grace) or, for a duo match, a request the two have said yes to (the room's `ask`, as the games screen's Play writes it, so the game deals a new table for it: README, Rooms); then the party's choice (`playing`: the game, its page, the time, who was in the party). The two writes go at once, in that order (October 2026): the database applies one connection's writes in the order they were sent, so nobody arrives before the table, and the host waits one round trip rather than two. Every phone in the lobby goes there, the host's at once, and the host's page deals the first game with its own options (Dominoes' variant, Trumps' game) once its table is in. A game's page in a party (`?party=CODE&at=…` in its address, `party-link.js`) follows the party: its menu has the party's part at the top, Back to the party for the host (every phone goes back to the lobby) and Leave the party for everyone, and when the host chooses again it goes there. A game whose help is a rule of the table (Guandan's coach and card counter, Schafkopf's coach in Trumps) takes it from the host: the host's menu sets it for every phone at the table, and the others' menus say what the host chose (`partyHost()` in `party-link.js`). Someone who joins the party while a game is on goes to it if there's a computer's seat for them (a table game), else waits in the lobby: "Korbi and Mei are playing Delta: you're in the next game." A duo match in a party isn't the pair's, so it doesn't count towards the games screen's duo record. The seat games' Invite players opens a party with that game chosen, its QR code showing: it's the one way to play them with others (each used to open a table of its own). The games screen's Party tile opens Party's home: what a party can play, as tiles (a tap says what one is), and Invite players; a phone that left its party's lobby for another page finds Back to party CODE there.

Each of Party's own games also has a tile of its own on the games screen (October 2026): it opens Party on that game, the page wearing the game's name and logo, its rules on the screen and Invite players under them; the party it opens starts on that game. While one of Party's games is played the header wears its name and logo too, and Party's own in the lobby.

The lobby has the QR code and the party's four-letter code (tap it for a larger one, the link to copy or share, and who's in the party, where the host can ask someone to leave), who's in it with how many of Party's games each has won tonight, and the games: the one chosen at the head of the list, with its options for one of Party's own (the other games' options are their own pages'), then every game the party can play now, as the games screen's tiles, in its sections (Party games, with Company; Board & card games; Learning; Puzzles). Only the host's taps choose, and only the host has Start; a guest's tap shows what a game is, and the guests see the host's choice and its options as they change. On a phone held upright that is one column, the chosen game and the tiles scrolling together in their own box (keeping their place as people come and go, back at the top when the choice changes, where its options are), Start below; held sideways, who's here on the left and the game on the right. A game over offers the host Play again (the same game and options, a new deal) or Another game, and the others wait for it. A player can leave the party from the menu; one who leaves mid-game is left out of what's still to do (their vote isn't waited for), and comes back to their own seat after a reload or on another device.

Typing with the keyboard up (October 2026). A phone's keyboard covers the bottom of the page without the page being told: the browser shrinks only the part in sight (the visual viewport) and slides it to the box being typed in, so the top of a game goes out of sight, and the page can stay slid once the keyboard goes. While a box has the keyboard, Party's page is made exactly the height in sight and placed in it (`fitKeyboard` in `party.js`, from the browser's `visualViewport`); the header, the narrator's strip and the game's name give way, each game's parts share what's left (scrolling in the game's own box if it's longer, the box being typed in kept in sight), and a toast shows at the top. When the keyboard goes, the page goes back to the top of the screen. Every game with a box gains, from Sketch's guesses and Gallery's titles to Decoy's lies and Echo's clues. The browser that checks the pages here has no keyboard, so one was stood in for (the part in sight shrunk and slid as a phone's keyboard does) at 390 by 664 with 374 in sight, on a smaller and a larger phone, and both ways round on an iPad: the box, its drawing or question and the clock in sight together, and the page as it was once the keyboard went.

The games are modules (`party-core.js` lists them), each with its rules, options, the steps it goes through and what each phone shows at each step; `party-kit.js` has what they draw with: a secret card that hides your word until you tap it and stays as you left it while others play, a grid of players to vote for, who's still to answer, a clock running down, scores. A game moves on on the host's phone from what the players send (their votes, clues, guesses, each tagged with the step it was for), in one transaction on the table, so the phones can't disagree and a vote sent for a step already closed counts for nothing; what a player does on their own turn ("I've said mine") they write themselves. A game can remember what it has shown the party (`shown` in the table, by game: Who am I's identities, so none is dealt twice): it's given the list as it starts and moves on, and adds what it deals; every phone keeps the lists too (`party:shown`) and brings to the next party it sits down at what that party lacks (`recall` in `party-core.js`), so a table starts out knowing what its people have been shown. Menu: and End this game. `tests/party.mjs` checks, beside the games, that every game a party can play elsewhere has its page, its tile and room enough, which fit how many, the table a party deals and the addresses its phones follow, and seats a party of three at Dominoes on the database in memory, each in their own seat, the host running it, a latecomer in the computer's. Five phones in one browser played it through: Dominoes for three and back, Delta, Crates, Slate and Chess for two, Trumps for two (sitting across) and Company for two with five computer players, a newcomer waiting out a duo match in the lobby, a seat game's Invite players, and the host finding the way back from Party's home.

## Offline party
No internet, as on a plane (October 2026; Korbi: "allow coop on plane"). Every phone joins the same Wi-Fi (on a plane, a pocket travel router: a plane's own network rarely lets passengers' phones reach each other, and an iPhone makes no hotspot without mobile data), then Party › Offline party, where one taps Host and the others Join. Each guest joins with two scans: its camera reads the code on the host's phone, its own code appears, and the host scans that (Add a player, Scan their code). One scan can't do it: each phone needs the other's keys. The phones then talk directly over WebRTC (the browser's own phone-to-phone channel, the one video calls use) across the Wi-Fi, and Party and every game it plays go on as they do online: the lobby, the host choosing, every phone following to the game's page.

How it works. The host's phone keeps the party's database in memory (`memdb.js`, with the Realtime Database's own rules for what it keeps, the same the tests' stand-in uses), and `lan.js` answers every page the same calls `sync.js` answers online, the same way: a transaction is tried on what the page has, then on the host's until the two agree (two taps at once settle as they do online); presence and sessions are undone when a page goes, when a phone's link closes, or when a phone has said nothing for nine seconds (asleep), and said again when it's back; the host's clock is the shared one, measured by pings. `lan.html` holds the links and runs Party in a frame over the whole screen, since a page can't take a link with it to another page: the pages move inside the frame, and `net.js` gives each its database from the page round it. It loads neither the games screen nor `pwa.js`, as an update taking over would reload it and drop every link. A code carries only what differs between two phones (the ICE username and password, which let each answer the other's connectivity checks; the fingerprint of the certificate its encryption uses; the addresses it can be reached at), packed in bytes, about 120 characters, and the other phone writes the rest of the description back round it (`lan-link.js`). Safari hides a page's local address behind a multicast DNS name (a `.local` name the network answers for) unless the page has been allowed the camera, which scanning asks for anyway: the phone that has just scanned shows its real address, and one direction that works is enough for the two to find each other. Safari has no barcode reader, so the camera's frames are read by jsQR (`vendor/jsqr.js`, Apache-2.0) in a worker (`qr-scan.js`, `qr-worker.js`). Only the party's room travels: a page's own syncing (solo saves, where you are for your partner, flagged questions) sees an empty database that keeps nothing, rather than send a phone's history to the host.

While the party lasts every phone keeps its screen on, since a phone that locks stops answering. A guest whose link is lost (its phone slept, or went out of reach) gets a panel over its game: the host opens Add a player, it scans in again, and carries on as it was, in its own seat; the same after its page reloads. The host's page keeps the database through a reload (this tab's own storage), so a reload loses only the links: everyone scans in again and the game goes on. A code answering an earlier one (a phone that scanned twice, the last player's still in view) is passed over and the camera keeps looking. The host ends the party from the menu, asked first, and the others are told. Pictures from Wikipedia need the internet: offline, the cards go without them.

Checked: `tests/lan.mjs` plays it over channels in memory (thirty transactions from two phones at once, presence through a page going and a phone falling silent, a lost link and a new one with what was made meanwhile, a value in pieces, a page kept to its party's room, the clocks agreeing, a table of four through `table.js`) and packs and reads back the codes. In a browser with the network shut, three Chromium phones joined through the screens, each camera showing another's code; they played Order, a guest's link was cut and scanned back in, the host's page and a guest's reloaded mid-game, a page outside the party found its way back, and the host ended it; nothing tried to reach the internet but Wikipedia's pictures. Not yet tried on iPhones: the camera in the home-screen app, Safari's local network permission (allow it when asked: Settings › Privacy & Security › Local Network otherwise), and multicast DNS on a travel router.

## Undercover
谁是卧底 (shéi shì wòdǐ, "who is the undercover"), the party game of Chinese dinners and the TV show of the same name. Everyone gets a word on their phone; most have the same word, the undercover a different word of the same kind (tea and coffee) and doesn't know it; with six or more there can be a blank (白板, báibǎn) with no word at all, who must bluff. In turn each says one thing about their word aloud, vaguely enough not to give it away to the undercover, clearly enough to show the others they have it; then everyone votes on their phone, and whoever has most votes is out, the phone saying what they were. A tie is voted again between those tied; a second tie puts nobody out. The civilians win when every undercover and the blank are out, the undercovers when they are as many as the civilians left; a blank voted out may guess the civilians' word, in either language, and wins outright if right. One undercover up to six players, two up to nine, three from ten.

The words are 125 pairs, each in English and in Chinese (simplified characters, as the mainland game is played), shown in either or both (咖啡 · coffee): the game's classics (牛奶 and 豆浆, 饺子 and 包子, 眉毛 and 胡子) and some from the table Korbi sits at with suppliers (白酒 and 啤酒, 茅台 and 五粮液, 柴油 and 汽油, 油轮 and 集装箱船). Your word shows open while everyone first looks, then hidden until tapped (a neighbour glancing over sees dots); the speaking starts with a different player each round, and the speaker taps "I've said mine" (the host can move it on for anyone). At the end every role and both words are shown, the winners starred.

`tests/party.mjs` plays whole games in Node on the database in memory, the host's phone moving each on as the page does (`tests/party-harness.js`): the right number of undercovers, and a blank only when asked; a game where the table finds the undercover and one where it doesn't; a tie and a second tie; a blank guessing right in either language; a player leaving mid-game without holding the others up; the evening's wins.

## Who am I?
我是谁, the guessing game of sticky notes on foreheads, played with phones: everyone is someone, and every phone shows each player everyone's identity but their own. First the cards are looked over (October 2026, after an evening of identities nobody at the table could have guessed): each card on the others' phones has Reroll, a vote to deal it again, and when most of the others have voted for a card (with two players, the other's one tap; with three, both others; with four, two of the three; with five or six, three) it's dealt again from the same deck, never a card already dealt, and the narrator says whose card it was without naming either. Its owner can't vote on it (they can't see it), and a vote on the card it replaced doesn't count for the new one. The questions begin when everyone at the table has tapped Ready for the cards as they are (a Ready from before a reroll counts no more), or when the host taps Start now; written cards (You write them) aren't rerolled, so that game starts at once. Then, in turn, each asks the table yes-or-no questions about who they are, aloud (Am I alive? Am I European?), and guesses aloud when they think they know; the table says yes or no, so nobody types anything. Every phone shows whose turn it is, how many rounds have passed and Next, which any phone can tap: it moves the turn on once, however many tap it, and the narrator says each turn, and the round as one comes round (Round 3: Mei's turn). A player who leaves is skipped. Menu: a clock for each turn (none, one minute, two), which passes the turn when it runs out. When the table is done, the host taps End: show every card (a second tap to be sure), and every phone shows who everyone was, the narrator saying it; no winner is counted, since the table, not the phone, heard who guessed.

Who you are comes from a deck (the lobby's choice): Famous people, People worth knowing, Gods and monsters, Countries, Cities, Food and drink, Brands, Commodities (for a table of traders), Paintings, A mix (of them all but People worth knowing), or You write them, where each player first writes who the next player at the table will be (someone the whole table knows: a friend, a colleague, a dentist), as the sticky notes go. The decks are built from the knowledge base (`kb/items/whoami.js` picks them: lists of the well known, or sets such as every country), 1,069 identities in all, and under each identity on the others' phones is its crib: what the knowledge base says of it, so the table can answer about a painting or a commodity it half knows (Zeus: a Greek god, the sky and thunder, the thunderbolt and the eagle, Roman name Jupiter; the Mona Lisa: painted in 1503 by Leonardo da Vinci, in the Louvre; Norway: Europe's biggest supplier of natural gas, Johan Sverdrup's barrels, the fjords). The one asking shows at the top of the others' phones with the whole crib, the rest below with their first lines and More; a painting or a person shows its picture when there's a connection. Your own card is a question mark with what you are ("a famous person", as the deck being played calls it) until the end.

The people were gone through in October 2026 so that a party is dealt people a table can guess, and the rest are there to learn: 278 of them, each marked for the party, for learning, or both (`PEOPLE` in `kb/items/whoami.js`). Famous people deals those for the party (194: Taylor Swift, Messi, Freddie Mercury, Mr Bean's Rowan Atkinson, Jackie Chan, Queen Elizabeth II); People worth knowing deals those for learning (192: Howqua, the Canton merchant who may have been the richest man of his day, Hu Xueyan, Lü Buwei, Jakob Fugger, Marc Rich, Wu Zetian, Gérald Genta, the Earl Grey of the tea), the ones few tables would guess but worth knowing; and someone any table could guess who's also worth knowing (Napoleon, Einstein, Shakespeare, Cleopatra, Gandhi) is in both. A mix leaves People worth knowing out, so a party's mix stays guessable, and deals someone in two decks once. Neither deals anyone a table would wince at: no founder of a faith, no modern dictator, no politician in office, no one famous for a crime. A person's crib is written by hand, four to six lines (what they were and where from, when they lived, then what they're known for, the most telling first), because what the knowledge base says of a person is what the other games ask about them, not what a table needs (Neil Armstrong was "a writer" there, of the words he said on the Moon), and their picture is their Wikipedia page's. A person the knowledge base holds keeps its id and its names; the 107 it doesn't (Taylor Swift, Howqua) are Who am I's alone, so Punt and the other games never ask about them.

Nobody comes round twice (October 2026). Once someone or something has been on a card, dealt or rerolled away, it isn't dealt again, at that party or at the next one any of the same phones are at, so nobody at the table has a face from an earlier game at the back of their mind; the game doesn't mention it. The party keeps what it has shown (`shown` in the table, oldest first) and every phone keeps a copy (`party:shown`), bringing it to the next party it sits down at. A deal takes what nobody there has been shown, in a random order; only once a deck has shown every card does it come round again, those shown longest ago first.

`tests/party-whoami.mjs` checks the decks and their cribs (the people dealt by their mark, both in both, their cribs the ones written by hand, those the knowledge base doesn't hold kept out of it; the mix leaving People worth knowing out and dealing each identity once), and plays games in Node on the database in memory: a card rerolled by most of the others and not by fewer, its owner's vote not counting, two players' one tap, five players' three of four, the new card from the same deck and never one already dealt, a vote on the card it replaced not counting, the narrator naming neither card; the questions beginning when all are ready and not before, a Ready from before a reroll not counting, the host's Start now; Next from any phone, two taps on one turn passing it once, the rounds counted as the turn comes round; the clock passing a turn; a player who leaves being skipped; the end telling every card with no win counted; the players writing each other's identities; People worth knowing dealing its own; a mix of the decks; and nobody twice: five games at one party with a card rerolled in each, none of the 25 coming round again, the party keeping them oldest first, a phone bringing what it was shown at other parties and taking home what this one showed, and a deck dealt out (Brands to twelve, five times) coming round again from those shown longest ago. Three phones in one browser (`tests/fake-sync.js` the database) played three games with a reroll, then a new party: nobody twice, and the phones' memory the party's.

## Echo
A word game played together, for the most cards won (Just One's mechanics, October 2026). Each card, one player guesses, in turn round the table, and everyone else sees the word on their phone and writes one clue: one word, without seeing anyone else's. Before the guesser sees them, the same clue twice cancels out, both copies, and so does a clue that is the word itself, of its family or more than one word: the phone finds these itself (by stem, so cat and cats, swim and swimmer, prince and princess are the same; in Chinese, a clue sharing a character with the word is of its family), and marks why. The clue-givers then look the clues over and tap any to cancel (a translation, a word that's the same in other words) or to keep one the phone cancelled, and show the guesser, who sees what's left, large, and has one guess, typed (either language, any case, a letter off in a long word), or passes. Right, and the card is won; wrong, and it's lost with the next card too (on the last card, a card already won); a pass loses only this one. A wrong guess that's right in other words can be counted by any clue-giver while the answer shows. The game is 8, 13 (the classic) or 20 cards, in English, Chinese or both (450 words, from animals and food to the trading desk's tankers and containers); a clock for the clues (none, a minute, ninety seconds) counts a clue not given as none. At the end the score and a verdict (seven cards in ten or better counts as a win for the whole table in the evening's wins), and the cards played, each with its guesser and how it went. A guesser who leaves mid-card loses that card, since the next guesser has seen its word.

`tests/party-echo.mjs` checks the words, the clues that cancel themselves (the same twice, by stem, the word and its family, more than one word, none, and in Chinese), the pile's tally (right, pass, wrong, wrong on the last card), and plays games in Node on the database in memory: clues waiting for everyone, the table keeping and cancelling, a right guess, a wrong one costing the next card and then counted by a clue-giver (never by the guesser), a pass, the end with its verdict, the clock, Chinese words, and a guesser who leaves.

## Decoy
The game of plausible lies (Fibbage's mechanics, October 2026). A question whose answer few at the table know: what a saying means ("The wall of worry", 鹤立鸡群 with its pinyin and "literally, a crane among chickens"), a number (the year OPEC was founded, world oil consumption in million barrels a day, Trafigura's headcount), or an odd fact with a blank (In 1932 the Australian army fought a "war" against ___). Everyone writes a lie that might pass for the truth. Then the truth and every lie are shown together, and each player picks the one they believe, never their own: finding the truth scores 1,000, and each player your lie fools scores you 500. The last question counts double; the most points wins, counted in the evening's wins, and the end names the best lie, the one that fooled the most players at once. A game is 5, 8 or 12 questions of sayings, numbers, odd facts or a mix (each kind in turn, so eight has every kind more than once); a clock (none, a minute, ninety seconds) closes the writing and the picking for whoever's slow.

The phone refuses the truth as a lie, and anything too near it, since a near-truth let through would make a right pick a wrong one: the same words in any case, plurals and articles aside; the whole truth and more ("emus and kangaroos" for emus); for a short truth, most of its words and nothing else ("Minnie" for Minnie Mouse) or its number ("38 mins" for 38 minutes); for a number, the same year exactly or any other within half a percent. A long truth (a saying's meaning) shares words with fair lies, so only the whole of it is refused there. The same lie from two players is one answer, both its authors scoring from it. A number's lie is written as the truth's are (2000 shows as 2,000) and every answer shows in capitals without a closing full stop, so nobody's typing gives a lie away; a saying whose meaning starts "to …" says so in the empty box. When the lies are fewer than one more than the players (four at least), the house adds its own, and a player who's stuck can tap Lie for me for one of the house's, a different one for each of the first three seats. The answers stand for six seconds and a second and a half each (twenty at most), long enough to read who fooled whom and hear it read out; everyone tapping Next moves on sooner.

The questions come from the knowledge base (`party-decoy-bank.js`, built from `kb/`): 432 sayings with their meanings and three wrong meanings each (those Punt's Sayings level asks about), 689 numbers from Quote (its refining questions and units too technical to lie about left out, and none under a tenth), and 63 odd facts in `kb/items/decoy.js`, each with three house lies that are false, never another true answer. A number's three house lies sit round it in steps (years in a span the question's own scale sets, never to come; a magnitude by ratio; the rest by steps of its scale), and how many lie above the truth comes from the question's id, so the truth is as often the least of the four as the greatest: with all its lies below, the latest year was always the truth.

`tests/party-decoy.mjs` checks the questions (a truth and three house lies each, none of them the truth; a number's truth in every place among its lies about as often), what counts as the truth and as the same lie, and plays games in Node on the database in memory: the truth refused, a lie for another question ignored, the same lie twice as one answer, the house making up the numbers, nobody picking their own lie, the scores, Next waiting for everyone, the last question double, the best lie and the winners, the clock closing the writing and the picking, a player who leaves, the house lying for a player, the mix and the numbers' years.

## Gauge
A clue placed on a scale (Wavelength's mechanics, October 2026). Each round one player gives the clue, in turn round the table: they choose one of two scales between opposites (Cold to Hot, Underrated to Overrated, Cheap wine to Fine wine, Bearish to Bullish; 154 written for the game, in English, Chinese or both) and see, on their phone alone, where the target sits on it, a band anywhere along a brass-rimmed dial; then they write a clue that sits there (“lukewarm tea” a little left of the middle of Cold to Hot) and say nothing more. Everyone else turns the dial on their own phone, a finger anywhere on it pointing the needle there (or the arrow keys), and locks it in. The target scores 4 in its bullseye (within 2.5 of it on a dial of 100), 3 beside it (within 7.5) and 2 at its edge (within 12.5); a target is placed where its bullseye is whole on the dial.

Two ways to play (the lobby's choice). Each for themselves: you score what your needle scored, and the clue-giver scores what the guessers scored on average, rounded, so a clue only one of them would get scores little; the most points after everyone has given the clue once (or twice) wins. Together: the table talks it over, every needle showing on every phone with its player's initial as it's moved, and the table's needle, dashed, is the middle of everyone's (the median, so one wild needle doesn't drag it as an average would); the table plays for its score, with a verdict on its points a round at the end (Mind readers, In tune, Close enough, Some static, Different stations), In tune or better counting as a win for everyone in the evening's wins. Two can play together; each for themselves needs three. The clue-giver watches the needles move, and sees the target under them at a tap. On the clue-giver's phone the target is covered as their turn begins and again the moment the clue goes out (October 2026): Show the target under the dial, or a tap on the dial, uncovers it and the next tap covers it, so a glance over their shoulder, or at their phone on the table while the others turn their dials, finds nothing; it stays as they left it through the redraws the others' moves cause. After each round the dial shows the target, every needle and the points for twelve seconds (everyone tapping Next moves on sooner), and the end lists the rounds: each clue, its scale and what it scored. A clock (none, a minute, ninety seconds) passes a round with no clue unplayed and counts a needle moved but not locked in where it stands; a clue-giver who leaves passes the round too. The dial on your phone keeps your needle where you put it through the redraws the others' moves cause, even mid-drag. Held sideways, the clue sits beside the dial, with Lock in and the round's points in the left column.

`tests/party-gauge.mjs` checks the scales, the target's bands, the table's needle and the verdicts, the clue-giver's target covered as each part of their turn begins and shown at a tap, and plays games in Node on the database in memory: the clue-giver's choice of scale and clue, needles moved and locked in, the points and the clue-giver's average, Next, the clue passing round the table, the end; together, the table's needle with a wild needle ignored, its score and verdict, two players; the clock for the clue and the dial, and a clue-giver who leaves.

## Fuse
The word game of a bomb passed round the table (the mechanics of word-bomb games, October 2026). The bomb shows two or three letters; whoever holds it types a word with them in it, together and in order (ING: bringing, kingdom), on their phone, and everyone else watches the word appear on theirs as it's typed. A word counts if it's in the dictionary and nobody has played it this game: it passes the bomb, with new letters, to the next player round the table. The bomb goes off after a time nobody can see (between ten and twenty-five seconds with the normal fuse, seven and fifteen with the short, fifteen and forty with the long), running on as it's passed; whoever holds it then loses a life, and after a moment (the words that would have done are shown, the shortest first) the next player gets a new bomb. Out of lives, you're out; the last player with a life wins, counted in the evening's wins. Use every one of the alphabet's common letters (all but k, w, x, y and z) across the words you play and you win a life back, never more than you started with; your letters show under the bomb, lit as you use them. The lobby sets the letters (easy, medium, hard), the lives (two, three, four) and the fuse. A phone buzzes when the bomb comes to it, where phones can; a word that doesn't count says why (no ING in it, played already, not in the dictionary) and stays said through the redraws; a holder who leaves passes the bomb on. Held sideways, the bomb and the box to type in sit on the left, the table and your letters on the right.

The dictionary is SCOWL's English, British and American word lists (Kevin Atkinson's, used under its licence, whose notice is in the file) to size 60, the size it recommends for a spell checker: 79,205 lower-case words of three letters or more, no names and nothing with an apostrophe, so both colour and color count. It's stored front-coded in `party-fuse-words.js` (each word a character for how many letters it shares with the word before, then the rest: 271 KB instead of 758) and decoded into a set when the game first needs it, in about 50 milliseconds. The letters a turn asks for are the two and three letters held by many of the common words (SCOWL to size 35): easy in 700 or more, medium in 200 or more, hard in 60 or more, so even the hardest are in at least 60 words of the whole dictionary.

`tests/party-fuse.mjs` checks the dictionary and the letters, what makes a word count, and plays games in Node on the database in memory: only the holder's word counting, words that don't, a word for another turn, a word passing the bomb on with new letters while its time runs on, the bomb going off, a life lost and the next bomb, the alphabet's life back, a player out and never handed the bomb again, the last one winning, a holder who leaves, and two players.

## Drawing
Party's drawing games (October 2026: Sketch, Whispers, Forger and Gallery) share a pad and the way a drawing travels (`party-draw.js`). The paper is the same light paper on every phone whatever its theme, the whole of it dimmed at night, so a drawing is the same drawing everywhere and the eraser, which draws in the paper's colour, still rubs out. On it a finger draws in eleven inks and three pens, with an eraser, undo, and clear, which asks for a second tap within a few seconds. A drawing travels as text: each stroke its ink and pen, then its points on a grid of 1,024 a side, the first in four characters and each after it as its step from the one before, in two characters where the step is short (most are) and five where it isn't. When the finger lifts, the stroke keeps only the points that shape it (Ramer–Douglas–Peucker: a point stays only where leaving it out would move the line by more than about half a pixel on a phone), and it's drawn point to point, since those points are the finger's path; smoothing them would round off the corners the finger meant. A minute and a half of drawing is two or three kilobytes. The one drawing sends it as they draw (at the end of each stroke, and every 0.3 seconds during one) to their own place in the table's inputs, which every phone watches. So the others see it drawn, and the game's own state, which every move of the table carries, holds a drawing only once its turn is over.

Party's page draws a game's screen afresh with every change at the table, and while someone draws that is several times a second. A drawing game is marked to keep its screen (`keepsView`), and `party.js` gives it its stage as it left it. Each screen is built once for its step and turn and then updated where it stands, so the pad under a finger, the drawing being watched and the box being typed in are never rebuilt.

## Sketch
The drawing game of guessing (Pictionary's, as phones play it; October 2026). In turn round the table, one player chooses one of three words and draws it on their phone, in a minute, eighty seconds or two minutes. Everyone else watches the drawing appear on their own phone and types guesses, as many as they like. A right guess scores 300 at once, falling evenly to 50 on the bell. The one drawing scores the guessers' average, so a drawing only one of them gets scores little. When everyone has it, the turn ends there.

Half way through the clock one letter of the word shows among its blanks, and three quarters through another (in a word of five letters or more). Wrong guesses show to everyone, newest last. A close guess (two letters off a word of five or more, or most of it) is told only to whoever typed it, in the box itself, and under it while the keyboard is down. The others see it only once they have the word themselves. After each turn the word stands for seven seconds with its drawing, who got it and what each scored.

Guessing with the keyboard up (October 2026). On a phone every guess meant scrolling: the keyboard covered the bottom of the page and the browser slid the page up to show the box, taking the drawing partly out of sight, and Guess took the focus from the box, which closed the keyboard and could leave the page slid. Now, while the keyboard is up, the page is the part of the screen in sight (Party's page does this for every game: README, Party), the blanks and the clock share one line, the drawing takes the height left, and under it the latest guesses and then the box sit on the keyboard; held sideways, the box moves to the bottom of the right-hand column, just above the keyboard. Guess leaves the box its focus, so the keyboard stays up from one guess to the next and nothing moves as you guess. On a phone whose keyboard leaves 374 of its 664 pixels in sight, the drawing is 221 across (331 with the keyboard down).

The lobby sets the words' language (English, Chinese or both), the difficulty (three easy words, three hard ones, or one of each), one, two or three rounds, and the time to draw. The most points wins, counted in the evening's wins. A guess in either language counts. So do other ways of saying it (fries for chips, 的士 for 出租车) and, in English, an article, a plural, or one letter off in a word of six letters or more. Nobody is offered a word twice in a game, and a word the party has drawn before comes round again only once the rest have (as Who am I's identities don't). Held sideways, the paper sits on the left with all the height. On the right are the word or its blanks and the clock at the top, then the box for a guess (high, where a tablet's keyboard leaves it), the guesses, and the pad's tools low where a thumb is.

The words are 720 things a hand can draw (`party-draw-words.js`), in sixteen kinds: animals, food and drink, at home, things to wear, getting about, places, nature, people, things from stories, sport and games, the body, things people do, music, tools and gadgets, festive things, and the trading desk's tankers, refineries and silos. Each is in English and Chinese and marked easy, medium or hard: easy is what a child could draw, hard an idea, a place or something done.

`tests/party-draw.mjs` checks a drawing's text (every point back where it was, steps short and long, a dot, the page's edges, text that can't be read left out), the thinning (a straight line kept as its two ends, a corner kept), the size of a minute and a half of drawing, guesses right and close, and the words: each once, in both languages, enough at every level, none a right guess for another of its kind. `tests/party-sketch.mjs` plays games in Node on the database in memory, checking the words offered at the game's levels, the choice made by the player or, when the clock runs out, by the phone; wrong guesses changing nothing, right ones scored by when they were typed, one too late or for another turn not counting; everyone having it ending the turn early, the drawing kept, the drawer's average, the clock; the rounds, one drawing who leaves, the end; Chinese words with an English guess; and the party's drawn words dealt last. Three phones in one browser played it at the four sizes, in the three themes, day and night.

## Whispers
Chinese whispers with drawings (Telestrations' game, as Gartic Phone plays it on phones; October 2026). Everyone writes something to draw on the first page of a book of their own: a few words, the odder the better, or a line from the game's book (Give me one, three to choose from). Then the books go round the table, all at once. You draw what's written on the page you're passed, without seeing the page before it. The next player sees only your drawing and writes what they make of it, the next draws that, and so on. Every book has every player's hand in it once and ends on someone's words. With an odd number at the table that works out by itself; with an even number each player draws their own first page too, so that every book still ends on words (a table of three makes books of three pages, four of five).

Each page has a clock (Quick, Normal or Relaxed: 30, 45 or 60 seconds for words, 60, 90 or 120 for a drawing). Done says you're happy, and Change takes it back. The next page comes when everyone's done or the clock runs out. It takes what each player had: words are sent as they're typed and a drawing as it's drawn, so nothing is lost to the clock. A first page nobody wrote is the game's own line, so the book still has something to pass on. A player who leaves leaves their pages blank, and the next to draw gets the book's last words instead. Then the books are shown to the whole table, every phone on the same page and the narrator reading each one out ("Korbi wrote: a cat stuck up a tree." "Mei drew that." "Ana took it for: a tent. And that's how it ended."). Anyone can tap Next, and a second tap on the same page moves nothing. There are no points, so nothing is counted in the evening's wins. At the end every book can be looked through again on your own phone. The lobby can deal the first pages from the book instead, and then everyone starts by drawing. The book is 150 lines, odd sights and sayings taken literally (raining cats and dogs; a bull in a china shop; a tanker stuck in a canal), and the party remembers which it has had, so a line comes round again only once the rest have. A book is a page per player, a drawing two or three kilobytes, so even ten players' books sit in the table comfortably.

`tests/party-whispers.mjs` checks how the books go round (at every table from three to ten, each page round every player has exactly one book in hand, every hand is in every book, and at an even table the owner draws their own first page), what each player is passed, and plays games in Node on the database in memory: first pages written, drawn and named, each page by the next player round; a page sent for another step counting for nothing; the books shown a page at a time by anyone's Next, a second tap moving nothing; the pages read out, to the end with no winner; the clock taking words as typed and a drawing as it stands, and the book's line for a first page nobody wrote; a player who leaves; and first pages from the book, none the party has had. Three phones in one browser played it through at the four sizes, in the three themes, day and night.

## Forger
The drawing game of the artist who doesn't know what's being drawn (A Fake Artist Goes to New York's mechanics; October 2026). Everyone sees the kind of thing (an animal), and all but one see the word (a giraffe) on their phone, hidden until tapped. The one without it is the forger. Your card has the same label and looks the same either way, so a glance at a neighbour's phone gives nobody away. In turn, twice round the table, each player adds one line to the drawing every phone shows, in their own colour (ten colours, one for each seat): pen down, one stroke, pen up, then Redo or Done, 25 seconds a line, the others watching it drawn. A dot counts as a line. The forger draws too, as if they knew. The first to draw moves round the table each round, never the forger, who'd have nothing to go on. Then everyone votes for the forger, never themselves. With the most votes alone, the forger is caught but can still win by naming the word, in either language. Otherwise, or on a tie, the forger gets away. The forger winning scores them 2, and the artists winning scores each of them 1. A game is three or five rounds, or once each as forger, each round's forger dealt so that nobody is forger twice before everyone has been once. The most points wins, counted in the evening's wins.

The word is dealt from Sketch's words (`party-draw-words.js`), from the easy and medium ones, of a kind a few lines can begin to show (not things people do, which are drawn as people), and the party remembers which it has had. A line reaches the table as the stroke it is, kept in its player's colour and the medium pen whatever was sent. A forger who leaves mid-round is caught and can't name the word, so the artists win it. The narrator says the kind aloud but never the word, then whose line it is, the vote, and who the forger was. The reveal shows the word, the forger, who voted for whom, and the points for fifteen seconds, or until everyone taps Next. Held sideways, the drawing sits on the left with the order of play, your card, Redo and Done beside it.

`tests/party-forger.mjs` checks the card in the table's language, a line kept as one stroke in its player's colour (a dot one, nothing none), and the vote (the most votes alone, a tie nobody), and plays games in Node on the database in memory: everyone looking at their card; one line each twice round, never starting with the forger; a line out of turn or still being drawn not taken; the clock passing a turn; the forger caught and naming the word in Chinese at an English table; getting away on a tied vote; caught and naming it wrong; the points and the rounds, each with a different forger; the end; a forger who leaves; and once each as forger. Four phones in one browser played it at the four sizes, in the three themes, day and night.

## Gallery
The drawing game of titles (Drawful's mechanics; October 2026). Everyone is given a strange title on their phone (a nervous lighthouse; a dog doing its taxes; a tanker that lost its way) and draws it, in a minute, ninety seconds or two minutes, keeping the title to themselves. Done says you're happy, Change takes it back, and the clock takes what's there. Then the drawings are hung one at a time, in an order nobody knows, each with its artist named; a blank page isn't hung. Everyone but the artist writes a title for it that might pass for the real one. The phone refuses the real title and anything too near it (all of its words, case, articles, apostrophes and plurals aside). A player who's stuck can tap Title for me for a title from the game's own list, a different one each tap. Every title is then shown together, in capitals so nobody's typing gives one away, the same title from two players as one. When there are too few, the house adds titles from the list to make one more than there are writers (four at least). Each player but the artist picks the one they believe is real, never their own. Finding the real title scores 1,000, and its artist 500 for each who found it, so a drawing that shows its title pays. Your title fooling someone scores 500 for each one fooled. A minute to write, 45 seconds to pick, then the titles stand with their authors and whom they fooled, the narrator reading them out, until the clock or everyone's Next. One round or two (new titles for the second); the most points wins, counted in the evening's wins. At the end the wall itself is shown: each drawing with its real title and its artist.

The titles are 140 written for the game (`TITLES` in `party-draw-words.js`), odd enough that a wrong title can pass for any of them, no two alike, and the party remembers which it has had. On a phone the drawing shrinks as far as it needs to so the titles, the clock and Next fit, the list of titles scrolling in what's left, and what you scored sits beside the drawing's name. Held sideways, the drawing is on the left and the titles, the scores and Next on the right.

`tests/party-gallery.mjs` checks titles (the same title, too near the real one, what's wrong with one, the list with no two alike) and plays games in Node on the database in memory: everyone drawing their own title, remembered by the party; a blank page not hung; the real title refused as a title; the same title twice as one; the house making up the numbers; the artist not picking and nobody picking their own; the points; the reveal read out; everyone's Next; the clock running a whole drawing; the end and its winners; a round where nothing was drawn going on to new titles; and the party's titles dealt last. ## Higher or Lower
Higher or Lower (October 2026), on one phone or everyone's. One deck of 52, shuffled, the first card turned. Whoever's go it is says out loud whether the next card will be higher or lower, then taps the card (or Next card): wrong, and they lose; the same rank again, and they lose too. Aces are high, suits don't count, the go passes to the left.

On one phone, nothing to open: its tile on the games screen (and in Party's list before a party's open) opens the deck on that phone, for the phone in the middle of the table; Back goes to Party's list, Menu, Invite players opens a party with it chosen. The deck and how far it has got are kept on the phone (`party:hilo`), so a reload or a locked screen comes back to the same card. At a party it's one of Party's games: the host starts it, every phone shows the same card, and anyone's tap turns the next for everyone (`tap`, a change straight into the game's state: it turns a card only if the one the tapper saw is still showing, so two people tapping at once turn one card, not two). The narrator says each card and who turned it ("Mei: the queen of diamonds. Lower."); the host can shuffle a new deck.

The card turned is drawn as large as the screen allows, with the one before it tilted under it, only its corner showing, and what the new one did against it beneath (higher, lower, or the same: a loss), with the cards left (and at a party who turned it). A turn moves the cards rather than redrawing them: the new card comes in from the right, the one it covers glides left and tilts under it, and the one before that fades away; the cards are kept by card and only their places change, so quick taps, or a card turned on another phone, glide as well. After the last card, a tap shuffles a new deck. `party-hilo.js` is the game; `tests/party-hilo.mjs` checks the deck (every card once, a fair shuffle, the same deck for the same seed, 51 taps to the end and the next shuffling, the verdict by rank alone, aces high, only a deck taken back from what a phone kept), its tile and logos, and a party of three in Node: everyone's phone with the same card, a tap turning it for all, a second tap on the same card turning nothing, the shuffle, the narrator's words, and the state through the database.

No drinking in the games (4 November 2026, Korbi: "remove the drinking references across the games (PG), just say 'loses' or 'loses a life'"): a loss is a loss or a life; `tests/no-drinking.mjs` keeps the words out.

## Cabal
The bluffing game of influence (Rikki Tahta's Coup, 2012; November 2026, Korbi: "add conventional Coup", then "Coup in Party: yes"), two to six players, each on their own phone. Everyone has two secret influence cards, each a Syndic, Saboteur, Privateer, Astronomer or Kontor (three of each in the deck of fifteen), and two coins. On your turn you take one action: Income (a coin), Foreign Aid (two), an Oust (pay seven, a player loses an influence; from ten coins you must), or one of the characters' actions, Tax (the Syndic, three coins), Sabotage (the Saboteur, pay three, a player loses an influence), Steal (the Privateer, two coins from a player) and Exchange (the Astronomer: draw two cards and keep as many as you hold). You may claim any character whether you hold it or not; anyone may challenge the claim, and whoever is wrong loses an influence (a claim shown true costs the challenger, and the claimant swaps the card shown for a fresh one). Foreign Aid can be blocked by anyone claiming the Syndic, a sabotage by its target claiming the Kontor, a steal by its target claiming the Privateer or the Astronomer, and a block can be challenged too. The last player with influence wins, and the win is counted for the evening like any of Party's games.

The rules are the published ones, including the awkward corners: the three coins of a sabotage are paid when it is declared, come back if the Saboteur is challenged and found false, and are lost if the Kontor blocks it; a sabotage whose Saboteur was shown true (or whose Kontor block was caught bluffing) can take both of the target's cards; a player's last card is turned over without asking which; with two players the first to play starts with one coin, as the rules say. Where several challenge or block at once, the one nearest after the player who spoke comes first, since the phones can't tell who tapped first. A player who leaves the table is out with their cards turned up, and a turn or claim that was theirs is dropped.

**Renamed Cabal** on 2 November 2026, when Korbi asked for it to fit the theme. The rules are unchanged and the words are the Codex's own: Syndic for the Duke, Saboteur for the Assassin, Privateer for the Captain, Astronomer for the Ambassador and Kontor for the Contessa (his names), Oust for the seven-coin Coup and Sabotage for the three-coin assassination. Only what a player sees was renamed: the ids, the saved game and the Party room keep the old spelling (`coup`, `duke`, `party:coup`), as Slate kept Glyph's, so nothing in play broke.

Every decision is a step of its own (`challenge#9`: the phase and a counter, `turn`, `challenge`, `block`, `lose`, `exchange`, then `over`), so what a phone sent for one is never read as the answer to the next. Everyone answers a challenge or block window at once, on their own phone, and the window closes when all have answered; the Clock option (off, 20, 30 or 60 seconds, 30 by default) closes it when the time runs out, counting the silent as allowing, and on a turn takes Income (or an Oust on someone, at ten coins), on a choice of card to lose takes one at random and on an exchange keeps what you hold. Like Undercover's roles, the hands are in the table's state and each phone draws only its own: a table of friends, not a casino.

A phone shows the players as tiles (name, coins, two cards face down, or turned up as they are lost, the player whose turn it is and the target marked), what is happening and what you can do about it, your own cards (a tap hides them from a neighbour's glance) and the last two or three lines of what happened; nothing is spoken. It is drawn to fit 390 by 600 with no scrolling for six players, the choice of card and the exchange standing in for your own cards while you choose.

Alone against the computer (1 November 2026, Korbi: "should be possible to play coup by oneself (1-4 players)"). Cabal's tile on the games screen, and its place in Party's list, open a setup on this phone: choose one to four opponents (Quill, Sable, Pip and Wren, in that order), then Deal; "Invite friends instead" goes to the table above. It is the same game: `party-coup-solo.js` does what the host's phone does at a table (it holds the state, calls the game's own `advance` once the step has all its answers, and asks the computer players when it is their turn), so the rules, the log and the drawing are Cabal's own `view`, with no clock (nobody is waiting on you). The game is kept on the phone as `party:coup` and comes back to the same hand after a reload or a locked screen; New game asks twice ("Sure?") so a stray tap doesn't throw one away; the computer players wait for the menu and rules to close (`onPause`) and don't move at all in a frame that is only watching. A challenge or block window is answered by every computer player at once after a moment, as it is at a table; if you are out they play the rest at speed. The one-phone page takes your name from the one the games keep (`crates:name`) and is "Player" until there is one.

The computer player (`party-coup-bot.js`, a pure function of the table and a seat) sees only what a player may: its own cards, the coins, the cards face up and what has been claimed (`tests/party-coup-solo.mjs` deals the other players' hidden cards again in thousands of positions and wants the same answer). It reckons the chance that the claimant holds the character from the copies it can't see (three in the deck, less those face up and in its own hand; with none left, a claim is a lie for certain), and weighs a challenge as a posterior: a holder always makes the claim, a bluffer does at the rate measured in games between these players (actions 37% bluffs, blocks 21%), against what a lost challenge costs. A win for the challenger costs a rival an influence, which helps the other players too, so the bar rises with the table (0.50 for two players, 0.58 for three, 0.64 for four, 0.68 for five); it challenges at once what no copy can make true, a sabotage aimed at its last card (right or wrong it loses the card anyway), and lets through a steal or a sabotage it can block itself. On its turn it prices each action in coins (an influence is worth about 4.6): its own characters at no risk, a bluff when the chance of being challenged times the loss is smaller than the prize, Foreign Aid less when a Syndic is likely to be there to block, a steal from the richest, an Oust at seven and always at ten, and which card to lose or keep by what each is worth (the Syndic most, then the Saboteur, the Privateer, the Kontor, the Astronomer, a second copy of anything less). Each seat has a temper, how readily it bluffs and how readily it believes a claim (`TEMPER`): Quill gambles, Sable is careful, Pip is suspicious, Wren bold and trusting. They were tuned in self-play (many thousands of games, November 2026): a first version that challenged whenever a claim looked somewhat likely to be a bluff lost to one that never did, because most challenges cost more than they won; the posterior and the bar for the size of the table fixed that. At a table of five computer players each seat wins between 15% and 25% of games (20% is fair), a computer player beats players choosing at random from what the rules allow 93% of two-player games (50% is fair) and 88% of four-player games (25%), and a game takes about 13 turns for two players and about 33 for four. Left open: challenges are rare between computer players (about two in a four-player game), so the table is quieter than one of friends; a human who bluffs more than the computer players do will be challenged less than a person would challenge them.

## Pewter
The bidding game Skull (Alex Randolph, 2011; a trademark, so the name, coins and harbour theme are ours), three to six players (4 November 2026, Korbi: "Codenames, Skull: add these two. this allows you to keep party as app as well, even though it's more of a tool thing."). Everyone has four coins, three silver and a pewter; each round all lay one face down, then in turn add a coin to their stack or open the bidding for how many coins they will turn without a pewter (their own stack first, then others' by choice, from the top); the highest bidder turns; all silver wins the round (two win the game), a pewter costs the bidder a coin at random, and a player with none is out. The Computer players option (Fill to 4, 5 or 6) seats Quant, Hedger and the other Cartel tempers (`party-pewter-bot.js`) in the empty seats, so one person alone can start it from its tile.

Rulings I made where the owner's rules were silent: a bid of every coin on the table closes the bidding at once; a bidder who is put out starts the next round with the pewter's owner (their own pewter: the next player); a player who leaves voids the round. Traps: like Cabal's hands, the coins under every stack are in the table's state and each phone draws only its own, so the computer players are given only `knowledge(s, u)` (sizes, turned coins, their own coins; `tests/party-pewter.mjs` swaps the hidden ones and wants the same answer); a computer player's turn waits for `botAt` and the game's `wakeAt` asks to be called then, but they lay at once with no wait (a wait there would only be a timer with nothing to show); `min` is 2 because `tests/party.mjs` wants it, and `need()` is what lets one person start with computer players.

## Beacon
A word-association game of two crews and one board (4 November 2026, Korbi: "Codenames, Skull: add these two. this allows you to keep party as app as well, even though it's more of a tool thing."). Its model is a trademark, so it has an original name and nothing of the original's words, in the UI or here; the games-find keywords are the one place the old name is. Twenty-five words, secretly nine for the crew that goes first, eight for the other, seven open water and one reef; each crew's pilot sees the key and signals one word and a number, the crew uncovers words by tapping one twice, and the reef loses at once. Two to ten players (Korbi's brief said four to ten: the computer lets fewer play), Ochre against Cobalt, crews dealt alternately; the pilot is a player (a team of one gets a computer crew) or, by the Pilots choice, the computer.

The words are Echo's and the drawing games' (`party-beacon-words.js`: each with what it brings to mind, written for this game, and groups such as animal or kitchen); the computer pilot and crew (`party-beacon-bot.js`) read only that, so a clue it has never heard of means one guess at random. A pilot who leaves is replaced by the computer. A clue may not be, hide or be hidden in a word still covered.
