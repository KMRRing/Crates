# Classics
The canonical puzzle genres (October 2026), in a section of their own on the games screen: games you know before you open them, played by their usual rules. Korbi weighed a roster of newspaper and app puzzles and kept every one, with the crossword moved to Learning because it is built on the knowledge base (README, Crossword). What Codex adds is the making and the company. Every puzzle is generated, not dealt from a fixed pack, and proven to have exactly one answer; difficulty is measured by the reasoning a solver needs to finish it, not by how many clues it has; each has a daily, the same for everyone (`today()`), and endless play besides. Each can be played together, mostly by splitting what each player sees (one has the row clues, the other the column clues) so that neither can finish alone, raced on the same puzzle (README, Head to head), and many at a party; the computer players are the games' own solvers, paced like a person. Where a genre has content (a picture, a theme, a final answer) it comes from the knowledge base. Everything works offline.

The fifteen, with a Codex name wherever the genre's best-known name is someone's trademark (Wordle, Spelling Bee, Strands and Connections are the New York Times's, Jumble is Tribune's, Mastermind a board game's):
- Number grids: Sudoku, Calcudoku (the genre KenKen made famous) and Kakuro.
- Logic grids: Hitori, Nonogram and Mines (Minesweeper); Slitherlink (a loop), Masyu (a loop through pearls), Nurikabe (islands and a sea) and Bridges (Hashiwokakero, islands joined by bridges) joined them on 31 October 2026.
- Code-breaking: Bulls (Mastermind, named after Bulls and Cows, the pencil-and-paper game it came from).
- Words: Assay (Wordle's genre: an assay tests what a sample is made of), Yield (Spelling Bee's: how many words seven letters give), Codeword, Ladder (Lewis Carroll's word ladders, his Doublets), Isomer (Jumble's: the same letters rearranged, as an isomer is the same atoms rearranged), Lode (Strands': the theme's words run through the board like ore, the word that spans it the mother lode), Lots (Connections': sixteen words sorted into lots of four by wordplay, where Crates sorts them by what they point to; since 2 November 2026 Crates' Classic, with no tile of its own) and Acrostic.

Fifteen games were built at once, each by its own Claude, so the pieces several of them share are each kept by one game and only used by the others: `wordlist.js`, the English word list every word game draws on (below); the clue bank for ordinary words, which Crossword and Acrostic use; the digit-grid board Sudoku made, which Calcudoku and Kakuro use; the grid filler Codeword made, which Crossword uses.

`wordlist.js` holds every English word of 3 to 15 letters, lower-case a to z only (no names, abbreviations, hyphens or apostrophes), each with how common it is: the SCOWL size it first appears at (10, the commonest few thousand, then 20, 35, 40, 50, 55, 60 and 70, the widest list kept), and whether it is one spelling's only (colour, color). A game picks the commonest tiers for its answers and a wider one for what it accepts. The words themselves are in `wordlist-data.js`, written by `tools/build-wordlist.mjs` from SCOWL (Kevin Atkinson's Spell Checker Oriented Word Lists, used under its permissive licence, whose notice travels in the file); words on a list of offensive terms are left out, with their plurals and tenses (SCOWL files those beside them, and Ladder found them there), but for words the list catches in their everyday senses (an escort, a hooker in rugby, a monger) and words that only look like an offensive word's forms (butter, spiced, a titter, to dicker). `tests/wordlist.mjs` checks the lists are clean, sorted and tiered, and that Slate's own common words are all among the commonest here.

`clues.js` is the clue bank for ordinary English words, which Crossword and Acrostic take their clues from: 21,298 words of 3 to 12 letters with 42,555 clues. It holds every word of the first two tiers of `wordlist.js` (SCOWL sizes 10 and 20), all of their lengths, and every word of 3 to 7 letters in the third (size 35), because short words fill most of a grid; the third tier's longer words are still to come. Inflected forms are in (RAN, RUNS, RUNNING), since a grid needs them, but a word is in only if both spellings share it, so no answer is the other side's typo. Nearly every word has two clues: an easy one, a plain definition (Tot up for ADD), and a harder one, another sense or a fair sideways reading, never cryptic grammar (Say further for ADD, Hundred runs for CENTURY, Night train for SLEEPER). They were written by hand, a few hundred at a time, commonest words first, then read again with fresh eyes. Every clue keeps the rules a solver expects. It is fifty characters at most, in British English and family-friendly, with no proper nouns as answers and no quotations from books, songs or films. It matches its answer's grammar (a plural for a plural, Sprinted for RAN). It never shows its answer, an obvious stem of it, a piece of it or half of a compound it is made of. About 700 words are left out on purpose: oaths, slurs and insults, words for which no family-friendly clue reads naturally, and oddities that read as misspellings (payed, flied, rubier).

A game asks `cluesOf(word)` for a word's clues, each with its difficulty (1 easy, 2 harder), `hasClue(word)` whether it has any, and `cluedWords(n)` for the clued words of a length, commonest first (by `wordlist.js` tier, alphabetical within one; `cluedWords(n, 0)` gives the commonest tier alone). The words are kept a line each in `clues-data.js`, by length and tier, so a clue is mended by editing its line, and a length is unpacked only when a game first asks for it, as `wordlist.js` does. `tests/clues.mjs` checks that every clued word is a common word of `wordlist.js`, filed under its length and tier and shared by both spellings, and that no clue gives its answer away. That counts the stems a solver would spot: a trailing s, es, ed or ing taken off, a doubled consonant undone (RUNNING and RUN), y for ies and ied (CARRIED and CARRY), the common irregular forms (GAVE and GIVE, MICE and MOUSE), a short answer at the start or end of a word (CAT in Category) and the whole answer run across words. It also counts a clue word that is a piece of the answer (HUNK for CHUNK, NOT for CANNOT) and one built on half of a compound answer (Daylight for SUNLIGHT, Paperback for HARDBACK). The test also checks that clues are short plain text starting with a capital, that a word's two clues differ, how the API answers, and that the bank never shrinks below its floor.
