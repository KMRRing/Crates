# Codeword
A crossword with no clues (October 2026). Every white cell holds a number from 1 to 26; the same number is always the same letter, two numbers are never the same letter, and every letter of the alphabet is somewhere in the grid. A few letters are given, and the rest you crack from the shapes of the words: a five-letter word whose second and fifth cells share a number, the only three-letter word that fits two letters you know. Tap a cell and type a letter, and every cell of that number takes it. A letter already on another number moves to this one (that number goes blank), unless it is fixed there; Undo takes back the last change, moves too. Hint shows one letter for good, fixed like a given and marked with a corner, and costs 30 seconds (it can't be undone, nor anything before it); Check strikes through in oxblood every letter written in wrong until it changes, and costs 20. The score is the time on the clock plus those, lower better, and the clock shows it as it stands (the penalties were once shown beside it, which wrapped the tools onto a second row the first time a hint was taken and shrank the grid). Bests are kept for each level and size in `codeword:times`, not `codeword:best`, which the games screen reads as highest-is-best (Survey does the same); the tile shows today's time.

The levels differ in how many letters are given and in how much a solver must weigh, measured, not guessed. Each grade is a rung of a human-style solver (`crack` in `codeword-solve.js`) that does what a person does: brings to mind the words that fit an entry's known letters, sifts them by the cells that repeat a number, the cells that can't be the same letter and the letters used elsewhere, and writes one in when only one is left and the list was short enough to sift; failing a word, it pencils in a cross-check (a number whose entries' short lists leave it one letter, whichever word each turns out to be), and the last number takes the last letter. Its vocabulary is the commonest four SCOWL sizes (10 to 40). The rungs let it sift 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 words, pencilling lists of up to 3, 4, 6 and 8 from the fourth on; a grid's grade is the lowest rung that cracks it from its givens without a guess. Easy gives four letters and never asks for more than four words weighed at once (rungs 0 to 2); Medium three, up to eight or a cross-check between short lists (rung 3); Hard two, sixteen or thirty-two (rungs 4 and 5); Expert one, thirty-two or sixty-four (rungs 5 and 6). The givens are drawn at random until a set falls in the level's window; failing sixteen draws they are built a letter at a time, each the one of eight tried that takes a solver at the window's top furthest, and a grid given that way may land a rung under its window. The menu holds New grid; Grid (Random or Today's), Level, Size (Small 11×11, Medium 13×13, Large 15×15) and Mystery word for the next one; how a pair plays; Race the computer; and the best for the grid's level and size.

Every grid has exactly one answer. A dense grid (`gridfill.js`, below) is filled with the commonest words (SCOWL 10 to 35, one spelling only, with a short list of little words left out that read as mistakes in a grid: mys, ors, ems), every letter in it, and numbered by a shuffled cipher. An exact solver then counts its answers from the givens against every word in `wordlist.js`, rare ones and both spellings included: each number's possible letters a bitset, each entry's words grouped by the pattern of its repeated numbers (letter is 0.1.2.2.1.3), narrowed until nothing more follows (a number down to one letter takes it from the rest, a letter only one number can still be goes to that number, which holds because all 26 are in the grid), then a search that stops at a second answer. A grid that has two is dealt again. In practice a filled dense grid has one answer even with no letters given, the shapes of its fifty-odd words pinning the 26 letters, so the givens are about the way in, not about uniqueness. A grid is made in a worker in about half a second here (Easy and Expert a second or two, as their givens are more often built one at a time); a second worker makes the next grid ahead with the same choices, so New grid is at once and a hint never waits behind it. Where a worker can't start, the page does the same under the rete.

Today's grid is Medium, 13×13, dealt from `today()`'s seed: the same grid on every phone, since nothing in a deal uses the clock or `Math.random` (the tests deal today's grid in a second process and compare). It counts the moment its last letter is right (`noteDayTime`, the first solve), and the games screen's tile opens it while it's still to play, asking before it leaves a grid you've started. The menu's Grid chooses Random or Today's; Copy a link to this grid gives an address that deals the same grid on another phone.

A mystery word, the menu's twist (Country, Commodity or City): a row of boxes under the grid, each with a number, spells a name of that kind from the knowledge base, its letters filling in as you crack them, as a puzzle magazine's codeword hides a word in shaded squares. Knowing the kind is a way in ("a country of six letters ending in E"). Countries come from Punt's flags bank, only the well known on Easy and Medium; commodities from `kb-index.js`'s that are also words (copper, cocoa, naphtha, never HVO or UCOME); cities from its cities. Names in the grid itself were tried first: a fill forced to take three or four of them came off a third to a half of the time and took one to two seconds, too slow for a phone, and a softer try placed none to four; the row gives the same moment of recognition at no cost to the grid. A hexagonal grid was considered and left: it reads poorly at 13 cells a side on a phone.

Race the computer (Steady, Sharp, Ace): the human-style solver at rungs 2, 4 and 6, paced as a person. Before each step it thinks (18, 9 and 4 seconds, plus 8, 4.5 and 2.2 for each doubling of the words that fitted what it knew), then writes each letter in (2.5, 1.5 and 0.9 s); stuck, it pauses (45, 30, 15 s) and thinks a rung harder, and at the top rung takes a letter as a hint would. Its solve is worked out once from the grid's seed and plays out on the grid's own clock, so it stops for the menu and goes on after a reload; on Medium grids it takes about ten minutes, five and two and a half. Its letters found show on a row under the clock, there from the grid's start so the grid doesn't shrink when its solve arrives, and the result says by how much you beat it or it beat you (your score, hints and checks in, against its time). Not on today's grid.

Playing together. A pair plays the split key (the menu's A pair plays: Split key, or Race). The key is split in two: one holds the numbers 1 to 13, the other 14 to 26. Each sees only their own numbers in the grid (the partner's cells are shaded and show no number, only a letter once it's in), writes letters in only for their own, and can suggest one for a partner's cell, which shows on the partner's grid small in the corner in the suggester's colour, with its key ringed: tap it to write it in, or Delete to wave it away. A letter on the partner's number is theirs to move. Hints and checks are the team's; Undo takes back only your own. The time runs from the deal to the last right letter on the database's clock, the same on both phones, and the match is recorded for the pair as a team, lower better. The cipher for a split grid is chosen so that each holds 42 to 58% of the cells, and so that the solver cracks it with the two views together, a rung above the level's top, while neither alone finishes their own numbers (what one works out of the partner's cells they know cell by cell, never seeing where else those numbers are). It is played at a table on `together.js` (`codeword-room.js`: numbers keyed n1 to n26, which the database would otherwise hand back as a list with holes, and nothing empty relied on). A race is the same grid for everyone (`duel.js`): the others' letters found show under the clock, the lowest score wins, and your own grid, bests and today's stay as they were; a party of up to six races too. A pair's request opens whichever the first of the two to arrive prefers, written for that request in the split key's room branch, so the second goes to the same; a game started from Party always races, as Party offers it, however many are at the table.

The page is phone first and never scrolls. The grid is a square as large as the room under the strip allows (container units, nothing sized in script), 27 px cells at 390×664 and 22 at 390×600, where a number is never smaller than half a rem (8 px); the keys are a phone keyboard's rows, each letter in use dimmed with the number it's on, so the keyboard is the key as you've filled it (a fixed one in brass, the cursor's framed); ↔ turns the cursor across or down (so does tapping its cell again), ⌫ takes out its number's letter. The cursor's word is shaded and every cell of its number tinted, and the line under the grid says how many cells the number fills. A physical keyboard types too: letters, Backspace, the arrows, Space to turn. When the grid is cracked the result and the way on take the keys' room, so nothing moves. A tablet draws it larger; held sideways the grid takes the left with all the height, and the side column has the strip, the key table a printed codeword has (1 to 26, each with its letter; a tap takes the cursor to the number), the line and the keys at the bottom. A tall phone (half as wide as tall or narrower, as the app on the home screen is on most phones) has the key table too, between the grid and the line, in room the grid can't use: it is as wide as the screen already. A hint's letter was first marked by a dotted underline, which read as "!" under an I; it carries a corner now, as a revealed square does in a newspaper's app.

The rules (October 2026) are a sheet of their own, from the header's Rules or the menu's The rules, as Dandan's are: in plain prose, what a codeword is, a step worked through (12 is T and 7 is E, so 12, 3, 7 is THE, TIE or TOE, never TEE, since 3 isn't 7), how letters go in and move, what Hint and Check do and cost, the score, each level's givens and the sizes, the mystery word, the computer, today's grid and the split key. Its numbers come from the rules' own constants as it opens, so the sheet can't drift from the game, and a grid of your own and its rival stand still while it's open, as for the menu. Looked at on a phone (390×664, 390×844) and an iPad both ways, in Codex, Modern and Kontor by day and night.

Saved state: `codeword:run` (the grid packed, a 13×13 in about 300 characters, and what's written in it, the hints, the undo log, the marks, the time, the cursor and a rival's solve), written at every letter and every five seconds the clock runs, so a partner watching sees your grid; `codeword:times`, `codeword:opts` (the menu's choices) and `codeword:today`. A race's run is the match's (`duel.js`'s `keep`), the split key's the table's. The logo is a brass key whose bow is a cipher dial (a numeral 7 in a cobalt ring) and whose bit is cut as two grid cells.

`tests/codeword.mjs` deals thirteen grids at every level and size and checks each is a good grid, uses the whole alphabet, holds only common words once each, gives the level's letters, has exactly one answer against the widest list (and it is the key), is graded at the lowest rung that cracks it and within its level's window, and that the levels get harder; that a seed deals the same grid and `Math.random` has no part in it, and today's grid is the same from another process; the solvers (counting stops at two, the cracker needs a letter, takes only right steps and never sifts more than its skill); the rules of a run (writing in, moving, undo, check, hint, score) and a run through JSON; hints and the three rivals (every letter once, right, in time, the stronger sooner, at a person's pace, the same from a seed); the mystery word's kinds and spelling; and the split key: halves of the cells, together cracked, neither alone, one answer, and a match at a table through the database's round trip (writing in, a partner's number refused, suggestions, undo of only your own, check, hint, the end and its record, a new grid). The page was played through in a browser at 390×600, 390×664, 390×844, 820×1180 and 1180×820 in all three themes by day and night, solo, against a rival, with a mystery word, today's, and with two pages on the stand-in database as a split key and as a race.

Not done: names in the grid itself and a hexagonal grid (above); the split key for three or more (a party races instead); a computer rival in a race or the split key; stats beyond the bests. For Korbi to judge: whether Medium, the daily's level, feels like a newspaper's codeword (ten to fifteen minutes), whether a hint at 30 seconds and a check at 20 are priced right, and whether the 43 px keys and the smallest screen's 22 px cells sit well under a thumb.

The grid filler, `gridfill.js`, fills a crossword grid with real words; Codeword made it and Crossword uses it, without changing it (its use is documented at the top of the file). It makes grids too, symmetric under a half turn as printed ones are: dense ones for a codeword, where most letters cross (about a quarter black, never four blacks in a square or three in a row, words of three to eight letters, nine from 13 across), and British blocked ones for a crossword, the lattice of black squares every blocked grid grows from, with more blacks added to cut its long runs, every word at least half crossed and room asked for answers of given lengths. It fills depth first, the open slot with fewest words left first; each slot's words are a bitset over its length's list, so a crossing letter narrows them with one AND per 32 words, and after each word every slot it crosses must still have a word and a letter it can share with each of its own crossings. The budget is counted in search steps, never in time, so the same seed fills the same way on every phone (a daily grid depends on it), and split over many fresh starts, since a search that runs long was usually lost in its first few words. Words to place first (Crossword's knowledge-base answers) each go where they leave the slots they cross the most words; words for given slots and letters already in the grid are kept. A cover (Codeword's: every letter of the alphabet in the grid) is met in two goes: the fill tries the rare letters it lacks on cells no other word crosses, where they cost nothing, then each letter still missing is brought in by emptying a six-by-six window of the grid and filling it again with that letter required and none lost, window after window. Measured here: a dense 13×13 grid with all 26 letters about three times in four, a third of a second each, and British 13×13 grids with four or five answers placed nine times in ten. `tests/gridfill.mjs` checks slots and clue numbers, the grids it refuses, both styles at three sizes (symmetric, valid, the same for the same seed, the dense rules and the lattice), fills of distinct words from the source, fixed and placed words, letters already in, the cover, and the same seed filling the same way.
