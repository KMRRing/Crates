# Timegrapher

A watchmaker's timegrapher on the phone (`timegrapher.html`, `.js`, `.css`): a mechanical watch's case back pressed to the microphone, or a recording of one opened, and it measures what a Witschi or a Weishi does: the rate in seconds a day (fast is positive), the beat error in milliseconds and, roughly, the amplitude in degrees, with the classic dot trace. It answers Korbi's question whether a video of a watch's small seconds could tell if it runs fast or slow: not from the frames (5 s/d is 3.5 ms of drift a minute, a tenth of a frame) but from the sound, since every beat of the escapement is a sharp click and the phone's audio clock times clicks to microseconds.

The signal chain (`timegrapher-dsp.js`, `timegrapher-measure.js`, pure, run in Node by `tests/timegrapher.mjs`). The live microphone comes through an AudioWorklet (`timegrapher-worklet.js`, which only copies blocks to the page with the audio clock's own frame count, so a lost block shows as a gap and restarts the reading) at the context's own rate, with echo cancellation, noise suppression and level control all off (WebKit stopped using its voice processing when echo cancellation is off in 2023; the page says so if a phone keeps it on). A recording (a Voice Memo, a .wav, an .mp3, an .mp4's sound) goes through `decodeAudioData`, the first 150 seconds of it. Then: a band-pass, a 4th-order Butterworth high-pass at 3 kHz and a low-pass at 12 kHz (the quote started from 2–8 kHz; on synthetic watches in pink noise, hiss and voices, 3 kHz gained about 2 dB of beat over room against 2 kHz, and 12 kHz about 1 dB against 8); the band's magnitude smoothed at 1.5 kHz into an envelope, kept at a quarter of the sample rate (12 kHz from a 48 kHz microphone). Knocks (far louder than the beats) and loud stretches (a voice: 50 ms whose floor stands well above the room's) are set to the room's floor before anything is measured.

The beat rate: the envelope's autocorrelation at each standard rate's beat period and at twice it (tick to tick): 14,400, 18,000, 19,800, 21,600, 25,200, 28,800, 36,000 and Frederique Constant's 288,000. A rate whose beat period is a multiple of the true one shows too, so of rates in that relation the fastest that shows wins: a 28,800 with a tock a third as loud as its tick is still 28,800, and only a tock that can't be heard at all makes it look like 14,400 (the menu sets the vph by hand then, from 12,000 to 288,000). A lamp's buzz or a voice's pitch repeats too, but folded at its period it's a ripple, not a few sharp clicks, so it never wins. A step a second is a quartz watch, which the page says has nothing for a timegrapher to hear.

Each beat timed by a matched filter, which is far more precise than picking peaks: the ticks alone first, a full oscillation apart, tracked against a template from a short fold, pin the period down; the whole stretch folded at that period then shows the tock clearly, and every beat is found where it best correlates with its own template (tick and tock sound different, so they have one each), between samples by a parabola through the peak, each beat's place predicted from a fit of those already found. The templates are rebuilt from the aligned beats (a trimmed mean, so a knock can't spoil one) and everything is timed again. A robust fit (Tukey's biweight) of the beat times, t = c + k·P + d·[k odd], gives the period P and the tock's offset d. The figures are defined as Witschi's "Testing methods for mechanical watches" (2025) defines them: rate is 86,400·(P_nominal / P − 1) s/d, the tick's and the tock's rates averaged (the common P does that); beat error is half the difference between the tick-to-tock and tock-to-tick intervals, |d|, which is also what the open-source tg computes (`be = period/2 − tic_to_toc`); both are timed from the onset of the first noise (the unlocking), as Witschi times them. Amplitude is from the time t between the first noise and the third (the drop) in each template, A = λ / (2 sin(π t / T)), λ the lift angle and T the full oscillation (two beats), Witschi's formula and tg's; it shows only when both noises stand clear and tick and tock agree within 40°, marked ≈ and "rough": a phone's microphone isn't a contact pickup, and the lift angle is often uncertain.

How well, on synthetic watches (`tests/timegrapher.mjs`, at 44.1 and 48 kHz, every standard rate, 20 s each): the vph right every time, rate within 0.08 s/d, beat error within 0.03 ms, amplitude within 3°; in a noisy room with knocks and voices (about 14 dB of beat over room) within 0.1 s/d and 0.07 ms; a watch put on the microphone ten seconds into a reading, every one of its beats found and the room's chance resemblances before it dropped (a beat matching far worse than the reading's typical beat doesn't count); a magnetised watch at +240 s/d and 1.2 ms out of beat read as such; a quiet room, a busy one, a loud one and a buzz read as nothing. End to end in Chromium, a synthesised watch at +4.0 s/d, 0.4 ms and 280° as the fake microphone read +4.0 to +4.1, 0.4 ms and 278–281° within 30 s; the same as an uploaded WAV at −7.5 s/d, 0.6 ms and 295° read −7.5, 0.6 and 294°. The fit's own uncertainty is shown with the rate (±0.1 s/d after half a minute of a clear signal); under it all is the phone's own clock, a crystal good to about ±20 ppm, which is ±1.7 s/d on every absolute rate the phone measures, and the page says so beside the number.

The screen: the watch on the microphone (a dropdown of the watches, what its calibre says to listen for beside it) and a signal meter (before a beat is found, how far the band's peaks stand over its floor; after, how well the beats match and how many do), the trace (a tap turns it into the beat: tick and tock averaged, the first and last noises marked), the three figures, a line of what's happening, Open (a recording) and Listen, the plate. The trace is a dot for every beat over the last 20 seconds, its offset from a perfect watch at the nominal rate up (early, gaining, rises): ticks and tocks are two parallel lines, the slope the rate and the gap the beat error. The window is centred on its own dots, as a timegrapher's screen is, so a watch within a few s/d draws two clean sloped lines; one far off wraps round the height into slanted stripes, as a paper trace wraps its drum. The height (±1 to ±20 ms) is chosen to hold the beat error and the scatter. The screen stays on while listening (iOS honours that in a home-screen app from 18.4); a page put away stops, keeping the reading if it heard enough. A reading stopped after 8 seconds or more of the watch is kept with its watch, its words saying how it stands (inside COSC's limits, inside Witschi's service tolerance, or fast or slow in that position; far out, possibly magnetised).

The watches and their calibres (`timegrapher-calibres.js`). Each watch has a name, a calibre and, if wanted, its own vph or lift angle set in the menu (the calibre's, by ear, the rates and angles a timegrapher offers, or Other… for one typed in: 3,600 to 360,000 vph, 20 to 90°); the first time, Korbi's are there: the Grande Reverso Ultra Thin (JLC 822, hand-wound, 21,600 vph: watchbase), the 1998 Reverso Géographique (JLC 858, hand-wound, 22 jewels: Europa Star and Phillips; its vph isn't published anywhere I found, so 21,600 as its 8xx siblings', which the trace confirms by ear), the Slimline Monolithic (FC-810: 40 Hz, 288,000 vph, a monocrystalline silicon oscillator with its anchor in the flexures swinging 6°, so no balance and no lift angle: rate only, which is how the page treats it), the green Rado True Square (R27077312: Rado's R763, which is the ETA C07.611 of the Powermatic 80, 21,600 vph, Nivachron), the Heart Beat (FC-310, a Sellita SW200-1, 28,800 vph; the Manufacture Heart Beats' FC-910 family is a preset too), and another watch, by ear. The Reverso Lady is quartz, and choosing quartz says a timegrapher has nothing to hear in it; a watch that steps once a second says the same by ear. Then the movements most met: ETA 2824-2, 2836-2, 2892-A2, 2000-1 and the Powermatic 80, Sellita SW200-1 and SW300-1, Unitas 6497/6498 in both beats (18,000 and 21,600), Peseux 7001, Valjoux 7750, Rolex 3135 and 3235, Omega's co-axial 8500 family and 2500 (25,200 vph) and the 1861, Seiko 6R, 4R/NH and 7S26, Miyota 9015 and 8215, Zenith's El Primero (36,000). Every lift angle names its source and how sure it is: checked (a maker's figure, or a watchmaker's working table: watchguy.co.uk's, built from the movements on its bench), family (carried from a sibling), differs (sources a degree or two apart: the 2892-A2 at 52 or 51, the 7750 at 49 to 52, Seiko's at 53 or 52) or assumed (none published: JLC's, Rolex's 3235, Zenith's, where 52°, the timegraphers' default, stands). The 2824-2 is 50° by ETA's own technical communication, though most timegraphers default to 52. A co-axial's noises aren't a lever's (Omega asks for a timegrapher with a co-axial mode), so it gets rate and beat error, no amplitude. The vph matters more than the lift angle: rate and beat error need only the vph; a calibre's vph that nothing beats while another rate shows by ear gets "another watch?" on the line.

The positions session (Menu: Positions, fully wound or after 24 hours). The six positions as Witschi names them, dial up and down (CH, CB), crown down, up, left and right (9H, 3H, 6H, 12H): each placed by hand and started, settled for 15 seconds (Witschi: 20 s after a change; 10, 15 or 20 in the menu) and measured for 40 (20, 40 or 60), any one skipped, the session ended early with two or more. Its six chips show each position's rate as it comes; a reload picks the session up where it was, and the menu's pause holds a step that falls due while it's open. The result (`timegrapher-records.js`): the mean rate (Witschi's X), the spread from the fastest to the slowest (D), the largest of a position from the mean, H − V (the flat positions' mean less the hanging ones', COSC's sign), the amplitude flat and hanging, the worst beat error, each against its limit: COSC's chronometer criteria for a balance-spring wristwatch (cosc.swiss, ISO 3159: mean −4/+6 s/d, H − V −6/+8, no rate more than 10 from the mean, for a movement over 20 mm; −5/+8, −8/+10 and 15 under), and Witschi's service tolerances for a watch fully wound (rate −5/+15 s/d for a man's watch, amplitude 260–320° flat and 240–280° hanging, beat error under 0.5 ms, about 3 ms needing correction, over 330° a risk of overbanking). The verdict in plain words with the numbers: chronometer-grade (inside COSC's limits on every count, five positions or more, flat and hanging: fewer can't be held to them), well tuned (inside Witschi's tolerance, the positions close), worth regulating (the positions close, the mean off: the regulator's job), or worth a service (the positions far apart, the beat far out, or the swing low). A session is minutes on a bench at one temperature, not COSC's fifteen days at three, so it's a yardstick, and the page says what it is. Two sessions a day apart, fully wound and after 24 hours, give the isochronism (Witschi's Ie), shown with the result: the latest full-wind session's mean less the latest 24-hour one's. The differences between positions (the spread, H − V) are free of the phone's own clock error, which is the same in every position; only the mean carries its ±1.7 s/d.

The rate as worn (Menu: Worn rate). The quote's method, in the page: the phone's clock to a tenth of a second, and a tap the moment the watch's seconds hand reaches 12 (on the touch itself, not when the page hears it). The watch shows that whole minute, so its offset is the nearest whole minute less the phone's time; a watch more than half a minute out is put right with −1 or +1 minute. Tap again a day or a week later: a straight line through the offsets since the watch was last set is the rate as worn, with no microphone and no crystal under it, since the phone's clock is kept to network time. A tap's error (a person's reaction and the hand's step, a sixth of a second at 21,600) is about a tenth of a second and much the same each time, so over a week the rate is good to about ±0.03 s/d. "I set the watch" starts the line afresh at the next tap.

Before you listen (November 2026; Korbi's note that the page should prompt for a 3 to 5 mm foam pad and a compass check of the watch). The first time a device listens, the page stops at a sheet of two things to do first, and says "Now press Listen, with the case back on the pad" once it is closed; the menu has the sheet again under About ("Before you listen"). One: check the watch for magnetism with the phone's Compass app, since a needle that swings means a magnetised watch, which reads fast by hundreds of seconds a day (the reading's own words already say a far-off rate is often magnetism) until it is demagnetised. Two: lay 3 to 5 mm of foam or cork over the microphone and rest the case back on it rather than on bare glass or metal. The wording and the reasons given for the pad (a hard contact rings with every beat, and the pad takes a table's knocks out) are mine, not measured on a bench; the thickness is Korbi's, and the first real watch on a pad is the test. `listen()` is the one place the microphone starts, so the Which? and Positions routes ask too; the sheet is remembered in `timegrapher:primer`, and a partner's watching frame never asks.

The library (October 2026; Menu: Your watches, or the round photo beside the watch's name in the strip). Korbi asked for the page to be a full library of his watches: each has a photo (taken or chosen on the phone, made 480 pixels on its long side, a JPEG of 30 to 60 kB, kept under a key of its own so the list stays light and a photo travels to his other devices only when it changes), a name, a reference, a description, its calibre and the date of its last service. The list shows each with its photo (or its initial on a plate until it has one), its reference and calibre, and how it's keeping. His were given their references and words as he'd given them (the Grande Reverso Ultra Thin Q2788520, 277.8.62; the Géographique 270.2.58; the Rado R27077312), and his second Grande Reverso Ultra Thin added beside the first, the same calibre, to be told apart by its photo, its reference and its sound.

Which watch (Which?, beside Open). It listens for twenty seconds by ear, whatever watch is chosen, and says which of the library it is. The beat rate parts most at once (the Monolithic beats 288,000, the Heart Beat 28,800, the Reversos and the Rado 21,600); between watches that beat alike it weighs what stays a watch's own between services: its beat error most (set by where the hairspring is pinned, and seldom alike in two watches), then the shape of its beat (when its three noises come and how loud each is), and its rate and amplitude least, since those move with position and wind. Every reading kept for a watch, Which? or not, teaches its print (`timegrapher-library.js`: running means and spreads, and its tick's and tock's shapes, weighing its last 20 readings, so a watch regulated or serviced is learnt again). It names the likeliest with how sure it is; tap the one it was and the reading is measured again as that watch (with its lift angle) and kept with it, which teaches it too. Until the watches that beat alike have each been heard, it says it can't be sure and asks; two of the same calibre regulated alike can't be told apart at all, and it says so rather than guess. On synthetic watches (four at 21,600, beat errors 0.2 to 1.1 ms, each read three times to learn it, then with a reading's usual scatter of ±3 s/d, ±10° and ±0.03 ms): every reading named right, sure of nearly all, never sure of a wrong one; two regulated alike: 50% each.

How it's keeping (each watch's sheet). Its rate, amplitude and beat error over time, every reading and every session's mean, against its baseline: the median of its first readings after its last service (or of its first readings at all), a third of them, three to ten. Then, from its latest five: an amplitude 30° or more below the baseline is the usual first sign of oils drying (50° or more, or under 220° at all, a service: Witschi has 260° flat, fully wound, as a healthy watch's floor); a beat error grown past 0.8 ms wants putting in beat (3 ms, now); a reading that jumps 20 s/d or more from the ones before is most often magnetism, or a knock; a rate drifted 10 s/d from its baseline is worth regulating; a service more than six years ago is due (JLC asks for one every six to eight years). It says "Keeping well", "Keep an eye on it" or "Service due", with what tells and the numbers; under four readings since its service, it waits for more.

Kept under `timegrapher:`: `timegrapher:watches` (each watch with its calibre, its library entry, its print, its readings, the last 100, its sessions, the last 30, and its worn-rate log, the last 200 taps), `timegrapher:photo:<id>` (a watch's photo), `timegrapher:lib` (the library given its first entries, once), `timegrapher:session` (a session in progress) and `timegrapher:opts` (the session's settling and measuring times). The history of a watch (Menu: History) lists its sessions (a tap shows the result again), its readings and its rate as worn, each deletable. The first time, readings kept before watches had names (`timegrapher:readings`) go to Another watch. `tests/timegrapher.mjs` also checks which watch a reading is (four synthetic watches beating alike and one apart, named right and never surely wrong; nothing learnt, all offered alike; two regulated alike, not sure; quartz and other beats left out), how a watch is keeping (well; its swing falling; a swing too low; a rate that jumps; a beat error grown; a service long ago; the baseline starting again at a service; nothing yet), the library's fields through JSON, the calibres (every one with a source, a vph the menu offers, a lift angle 30–70° or none for the Monolithic and quartz, Korbi's first), the session's figures and every kind of verdict, the isochronism, the worn-rate log's maths (the offset of a tap, the rate over a week from three taps, a setting starting afresh), and the watches through JSON, garbage dropped.

How long to listen. The fit's uncertainty falls as the time to the power one and a half: with a clear signal, ±0.4 s/d after 4 seconds, ±0.15 after 10, ±0.1 after 15 and ±0.05 after 30 (the fake microphone's figures above), so half a minute puts the fit well under the phone's own ±1.7, and a noisy room needs longer. The page shows a number only when it trusts it: half the beats or more matched, a dozen at least, the fit's uncertainty under 10 s/d; otherwise it says why (no beat yet, not enough signal, a quartz watch, or another watch than the calibre's). The phone's clock can't be calibrated against anything better from inside a page (its audio clock and its system timer most likely run off the same crystal, and the network time a page can see is good to tens of milliseconds, too coarse over a minute), so the absolute rate keeps its ±1.7 s/d; the positions' differences and the worn rate don't carry it.

On the iPhone. Listen asks for the microphone on the tap, as iOS requires, and starts the audio there. The constraints turn off echo cancellation, noise suppression and level control; since 2023 WebKit drops its voice processing whenever echo cancellation is off (WebKit bug 192932), so the raw microphone comes through, and the page checks what it got and says if processing stayed on. iOS may hand a mono microphone over as a stereo track with only its left side live (WebKit bug 281978): the page keeps whichever channel hears the watch better. The audio runs at the phone's own rate (48 kHz on an iPhone; the analysis takes whatever the context runs at). A headset's microphone (Bluetooth earphones at 16 or 24 kHz, filtered for speech) is no use, and the page says so. AudioWorklet needs iOS 14.5; the screen kept on in a home-screen app needs 18.4. When the page is put away iOS stops the microphone, so the reading stops there, kept if it heard enough. Recordings: a Voice Memo is the thing to open (Voice Memos: Share, Save to Files; set its quality to Lossless in Settings, since compression smears the three noises); Safari can't decode the sound of an iPhone video (.mov, WebKit bug 228971), which the page retries once as an .mp4 (the sound inside is the same AAC) before saying so. For the best signal: a quiet room, the phone on something soft, the watch's case back pressed to the microphone's holes on the phone's bottom edge (a 3D-printed or wooden cradle holding watch and phone together does better still); for the positions, hold the two together and turn them as one.

What isn't done. No temperatures, so no thermal coefficient (COSC's C) and no 15-day test: a session is a snapshot. No calibration of the phone's crystal (a quartz watch's one step a second could serve, but its own rate is only ±0.5 s/d or so and unknown, so it's left out). Amplitude stays rough: a contact microphone would resolve the noises better than a phone's air microphone, and a co-axial's amplitude needs the special mode Omega asks for. The vph by ear can't tell a 28,800 whose tock is silent from a 14,400 (the calibre or the menu settles it), and doesn't try 12,000, 16,200 or 43,200 by ear (the menu has them). An iPhone video's sound may not open. Nothing is synced to a partner: a watching frame shows only the kept readings and never opens the microphone.
