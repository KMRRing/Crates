# Manifest
A stack of the League's packages (columns of varying height) comes down onto one isometric quay in a Hanseatic harbour, package by package with a bounce, holds, and is shipped; then you're asked about it. A run is twelve rounds, or until three lives (coins of the League) are gone.

The setting (2 November 2026, Korbi: a re-skin for Hanseatic trade; the rules, the engine, the prices, the change rounds and what is asked are as they were, only the skin, the wording and the drawing changed): the picture is a quay of oak planks on piles, with water lapping at its foot, and across the harbour a town of crow-stepped brick gables with hoisting beams, a church with two spires, a gate between round towers and two cogs at the wall. The town is one silhouette, `manifest-town.svg`, used as a mask, so every theme and each day and night colours it from its own tokens (brass-toned in Codex, green in Kontor, ink-grey in Modern) and it stays behind the stack, never the thing you read. The five wares each travel in their own package: cloth in a blue bale lashed with crossed cord and a lead seal, salt in a pale stitched sack with the merchant's mark stencilled on it, amber in an iron-strapped chest with a lozenge on it, iron in a dark bundle of bars held by two straps (the bars' square ends show on the end), herring in a brown hooped cask with a fish chalked on it. A package is the box the engine always drew, so what shows through the stack is exactly what the engine says shows: the packing is drawn on its three faces and never changes a silhouette. A stack under tarpaulin (the turned spot's grey stack) is grey with ropes and says nothing of what lies under it. `manifest-art.js` draws the quay, the packages and the small package beside an answer; `manifest.css` makes the harbour frame.

Telling the wares apart: the first version's five colours (red, blue, yellow, green, orange) were its weakest point for colour-blind players: yellow and orange sat about 1 apart (on a scale where 2 is barely a difference) for a green-blind eye when they met on neighbouring faces. A ware now differs from the others in hue, in lightness (five steps, from near-black iron to pale salt) and in its packing, so none rests on colour alone, and an answer is a small drawing of the package with the ware's name, never a bare swatch. `tests/manifest.mjs` measures it (CIEDE2000, for a normal eye and for protan, deutan and tritan eyes): the nearest two wares are 19 to 23 apart on one face whichever the eye, against 8 to 19 for the old colours, and 15 to 18 on any two faces against 1 to 17; it fails if any pair comes nearer than the first version's did. The questions say "ware", never "colour", so the question and its answer mean one thing.

The words, old to new: container, package (a bale, sack, chest, bundle or cask by its ware); colour, ware (cloth, salt, amber, iron, herring); order book, the tally, and an order an entry on it, marked with tally strokes (one, two, three); stage, the quay (the card the quay and the questions sit on is still the stage in the code); "Bring it in", "Land the cargo" (Ship it stays); a recoloured package, a package changed to another ware. Round, run, stack, tier, life and pass stay. Nothing underneath was renamed except `colour` to `ware` in the engine and its test: the page, the files, every id and every saved key (`manifest:run2` and the rest) are as they were, and a saved run holds only a ware's number, so one begun before the re-skin loads as it was.

The tally: each round opens with the entries for its stack, the questions to come posted by kind and not by detail, each with a price. How many of one ware (which ware isn't said), how many in all, how many on tier 2, the ware at a spot on tier 2, the top of a column, the most common ware, a spot with the stack seen from the side or from behind (from round 3), and which of four stacks is yours, turned (from round 5). You choose what to watch. The stack comes in by the round's view (tier by tier; all at once, inner packages hidden; slice by slice; or two tiers and then the rest) and holds. Ship it ends the hold early: the share of the hold you didn't use pays up to half as much again on the round's answers. Then the questions come one at a time, in the order that gives least away first: the counts, then wares, then the turned spot (whose grey stack shows the shape), and which-stack last (its four stacks show nearly everything). Each card has its answers in view, so one tap answers it, or Pass this one moves on; the questions still to come show only their kind and price, and nothing is judged until the round's review, so neither a later question's options nor an earlier verdict helps. A question about a place on the stack carries its own picture beside its answers (the floor with the spot marked, or the stack turned and grey with one package lit). A right answer pays its price, a wrong one costs a life, a pass costs nothing, all settled at the review. Number answers are five evenly spaced values with the truth at a place drawn evenly, so the middle one is no safer a guess.

Prices grow 12% a round and follow your record for each kind (a hit rate that weights recent answers): a kind you usually get right pays down to 0.6 of its price, marked ↓; one you usually miss up to 1.4, marked ↑. The strategy that pays keeps moving.

Turning: a quarter turn puts the packages' long side the other way, and the stack is drawn so. The turned spot shows the stack turned and grey, one package lit: which ware is it? The which-stack question shows four stacks turned the same way: yours, its mirror image (left out when the stack is its own mirror under some turn), and yours with a package changed to another ware or two swapped.

Change rounds, every fourth: the stack holds, fades to an empty quay for a second and comes back all at once with something different, nothing marking it. Round 4: a package changed to another ware. Round 8: the stack turned a quarter, with a package changed to another ware. Round 12: two packages of different wares swapped, so counts can't give it away (tap both).

Stacks grow from 3×2×2 with three wares holding for 9 s to 4×4×3 with five wares for 5.7 s; three orders a round, four from round 6. The review after each round shows every answer and the truth; tap a question to light what it asked about. Random runs or today's run; bests kept; a run resumes where it was (a showing starts again from the beginning). `manifest-engine.js` makes the rounds and prices, `tests/manifest.mjs` checks 720 rounds: every order fair, turning consistent, four different stacks with one yours, and change rounds changing what they say among packages you could see.

The page fits the screen. The quay is there for the stack coming in, for a change round (where the stack itself is the answer: tap it) and for the review; while answering an ordinary round the questions have the room. They scroll inside themselves only in the busiest rounds on a small phone (four questions, two with pictures, and the four stacks).

Held sideways (README, Bigger screens), the card's two halves stand side by side: the packages (or the round's tiers) on the left, large, and the questions with the buttons on the right, the buttons at the bottom.

Together (October 2026): a table of two to six, the pair from the partner sheet or a Party, plays the rounds in step, in one of two modes, Against or Together, chosen in the menu's together part for the next game (before round 1, for this one too). The room keeps the round and the moment its tally opened, on the database's clock, and every phone runs the tally (six seconds, its bar running down), the drop and the hold from that, so everyone sees the stack at the same moment; a phone that reloads or comes in late joins at that moment, the packages already down drawn landed. A lobby lists who's at the table, and round 1 opens once everyone there has said Ready (two at least); after each round's results, the next opens once everyone at the table has pressed Next. Nobody away holds the table up (a phone asleep or closed isn't at the table), and once all the others are done, anyone may go on without the last one 30 seconds later (in a round, 30 seconds after the questions opened at the soonest: their answers stand, the rest are passed). Prices are plain for everyone: your record moves your own prices, not a table's, though it still learns from the answers you give. A table's clock doesn't stop for the menu, and the screen stays on. The line under the round says who else is at the table and how they stand (✓ done or ready, … answering, away). Your solo run, bests and today's are never touched.

Against: everyone sees the whole stack and answers their own questions, one at a time as alone, and Ship it ends only your own hold, for your own bonus. Once everyone at the table has finished, the round's results: the standings (standings.js: each player's answers this round as ✓ ✗ –, their lives, what the round paid them, the score), the questions with the truth, and, on a tap, the stack lit and what everyone gave. A player out of lives watches the rest, the stack and then the others answering. The game ends after the twelfth round, or once nobody has a life left: the final standings, Play again (dealt by whoever asks first; a later ask is a Ready for it) and Back to solo. For the pair's record, a win is first place alone.

Together: one run for the table, its score and three lives. Each player sees only their part of the stack, so the table has to talk. The stack is shared out in whole columns of the floor, a stretch of it each, the columns taken slice by slice in a snake and cut where the parts come out most even (never further apart than the tallest column, three packages): a part stands on the floor as it was stacked and reads the same in every view. Not by tier, though a stack comes in tier by tier: the bottom tier has every column and the top one a third of them, so one player would get three times another's, and a tier above the floor would hang in the air. The parts go round the table a step each round, and one who sits down mid-round has a part from the next. All at once, each part is drawn alone, so the table sees more than the whole stack shows (nobody else's packages stand in front of yours), and "you could see" means any of you. A change round shows each their part and brings the whole stack back to everyone: its owner can tell what changed. The questions are the team's, one at a time: anyone answers, the first answer counts (one a moment later does nothing, and says who was first), anyone passes. Ship it is everyone's: the hold ends, and the bonus is set, once everyone with a part has shipped. The review shows everyone the whole stack, and who answered what. For the pair's record, the team's score, won if it came through the twelve rounds.

The table's rules are pure, in `manifest-engine.js` (Ready and Next, the round's clock, replies, settling a round for everyone, who the table waits for, the split), each change made in the room's transaction, keyed s<slot> and q<i>; `tests/manifest.mjs` splits 3,600 stacks among two to six, checks teams' rounds as fair as alone, and plays tables of three in Node, every change read back as the database gives it. (Before this, a match was a race on `duel.js`: the same twelve rounds for everyone at their own pace.)
