# Yield
Spelling Bee's genre, by its rules (October 2026). Seven letters in a hive; make words of four letters or more from them, every word with the centre letter in it, any letter as often as you like. Four letters score a point, a longer word a point a letter, and a pangram (a word using all seven) seven more; every puzzle has at least one. The name is the refinery's: yield is how much of each product one crude gives, here how many words seven letters give. The ranks are a refinery's run, each a share of the puzzle's points as Spelling Bee's are: Crude, Warming up (2%), On stream (5%), On spec (8%), Steady (15%), Full rate (25%), Cracking (40%), Record run (50%), Master refiner (70%, the one to aim for, Spelling Bee's Genius) and Full yield (every word, its Queen Bee). They're a gauge across the top, an instrument's scale with a tick for each rank and the index at your points; tap it for the points each rank needs. The rules, the puzzles, the levels, the daily and the computer player are `yield-engine.js`'s, pure and tested in Node; the page is `yield.js`.

The words. Spelling Bee's commonest complaints are obscure words counted and ordinary ones refused, so what counts is chosen with care and kept in `yield-words.js`: 16,791 words. SCOWL (the word list behind `wordlist.js`) files every form of a word under its base word, so its common lists hold "gonging", "tenanting" and "queening" because gong, tenant and queen are common. A word counts when its own form is used, by its Zipf frequency (the base-10 logarithm of its uses in a billion words of film subtitles, books, Wikipedia, the web and social media, from wordfreq, CC BY-SA 4.0, credited in the file): SCOWL's sizes 10 to 40 at a Zipf of 2 or more (once in ten million words; 1.5 for the commonest sizes), and sizes 50 and 55 at 3 or more, for the ordinary modern words SCOWL files as rare (online, timeline, texting, pandemic), less the names, brands and slang whose frequency flatters them (tony, japan, google). Never: an S (Yield's letters never include one, as Spelling Bee's don't, or every plural would count), a spelling only one side of the Atlantic uses (colour and color both stay out, so neither side misses the other's), and a list of slurs, vulgarities, crude words and words that sound like slurs. A word you type that doesn't count is still told apart: "a word, but rarer than Yield counts", "one side of the Atlantic's spelling only", or "not in Yield's list" (a slur is never called a word), and the first two are kept beside your words, without points. `yield-words.js` is written by a builder from `wordlist.js` and wordfreq's English list (with the coordinator to file in `tools/`), which also keeps the frequency of each word to a tenth: the levels and the computer player are made of it.

The yield is exact: every word the letters make, found by trying every subset of the seven letters against the words that count (128 lookups), so nothing is missed and nothing invented. Spelling Bee has no single solution to prove; what every puzzle must have is a pangram, and a deal without one is never made (2,526 letter sets have one, each with seven centres). A puzzle is never mostly one ending (-ing words 30% of it at most, -ed words 45%), since a yield of one suffix is one trick, not a puzzle.

The Wheel (the menu's Board) is the genre's other half, the newspapers' Target and Polygon: nine tiles from one nine-letter word, the centre tile in every word, each tile used once (a letter on two tiles can be used twice), and the nine-letter word scores as a pangram. It asks something else of you, finding words inside a word rather than building them up, and a tile in use greys out as you type. 2,107 nine-letter words make wheels.

Levels are crude grades, Light, Medium and Heavy, and they measure depth, how far down the vocabulary the coveted rank needs you to reach: finding the words in order of ease (the commoner and the shorter first, as people tend to, each letter past four counting as a fifth of a Zipf), depth is the ease of the word that takes your points past Master refiner's 70%. Light reaches it on everyday words (a depth of 2.75 or more), Medium needs a few less common ones (2.35 to 2.75), Heavy words people rarely use (under 2.35); the size is only a range for each (a hive's Light 20 to 42 words, Medium 26 to 55, Heavy 30 to 75; a wheel's 16 to 36, 20 to 45 and 22 to 55), and Light's and Medium's pangrams are common ones. Measured on generated puzzles (`tests/yield.mjs`): median depths 2.9, 2.6 and 2.2; median sizes 30, 40 and 49 words in a hive, 24, 34 and 27 in a wheel; and the computer player at Steady takes a median 17 minutes to Master refiner on Light, 35 on Medium, and on Heavy mostly never gets there, which is the grading checked against how a person plays. Whether those are the right bands is for Korbi's thumb. A deal takes a millisecond or two.

Today's puzzle (the menu's Puzzle, which also picks a random puzzle's level) is a Medium hive, the same for everyone (`today()`). Deciding which letter sets can make a Medium hive takes seconds, too long for a phone to work out as it opens, so `yield-words.js` keeps the walk: every one of the 1,039 sets that can, in a fixed shuffled order from 20 October 2026, a set a day, so none comes back for nearly three years; each day takes the first of its set's centres that makes a Medium puzzle, in an order its date sets. `tests/yield.mjs` fails if the walk no longer matches what the engine decides (rebuild it then). Today's points go to the games screen as each word is found (`yield:daily`, by the day: the figure is today's points), so leaving costs nothing, and the tile opens today's puzzle while you have none. Today's and a random puzzle are kept apart, so switching between them loses neither; the daily before today's is kept too, and the menu shows its words, what you missed faint (Yesterday's words). Show the words (the menu) ends a puzzle there and lists them all.

Playing: tap the letters (a letter goes in as the finger lands, so quick tapping keeps up), Enter, Delete (hold it to clear the word), and Shuffle, which turns the outer letters round; on a keyboard, type, Enter, Backspace, Space to shuffle, Escape to clear. The word shows over the hive, its centre letters in the game's honey ochre and any letter not on the board faint; what a word got is said under it ("Fine +6", "Pangram! +14", the new rank when it changes one, or why it doesn't count, the word shaking). Under the gauge, your words on one line, newest first (tap for them all, in columns), and the Chart: Spelling Bee's hints grid, what's left by first letter and length, and by first two letters. It's free, as Spelling Bee's is. A word tapped in any list says what it means, the clue bank's plain clue (`clues.js`, Crossword's and Acrostic's), so the words you missed teach you something; the bank covers the commonest words so far, and says when it has none.

Sprints and the rival (Settings). Clock: a random puzzle untimed, or a sprint of three or five minutes, the most points before time's up (best kept for each clock, board and level); a sprint stands still while the menu or the games screen is open, and its clock runs only while the page is on screen. Rival: the computer plays the same puzzle beside you, at Novice, Steady, Sharp or Master, its index under the gauge and its words and rank in a line, its words shown at the end. It plays as a person does: it knows a word with a chance that falls as the word's ease goes below its vocabulary, finds what it knows commoner and shorter words first with some luck in the order, takes longer over rarer words and much longer as the easy ones run out, and a moment to tap each in. Its whole game is planned from the puzzle's seed when the puzzle starts, so a reload or a partner watching sees the same, and it runs on your clock. Fitted to how people play, on a Medium hive: in a three-minute sprint a median 34, 45, 61 and 69 points; Master refiner in about 35 minutes for Steady, 15 for Sharp and 10 for Master, never for Novice, who finds about half the points in half an hour. A rival joins the puzzle in front of you, as far on as it would have got by now.

Together (duel.js, README Head to head: from the partner sheet, or a party of up to six), in the dealer's way (Together in the menu, switched at the table for the next match): a race or a team; until you choose, a pair plays as a team and a party races. A race: the same letters for everyone and a clock of three or five minutes, the others' points as small indexes under your gauge and in a line; the most points wins, equal points going to whoever reached them first, and the result stars the words nobody else found and lists the best you missed. A team: the same letters, and a centre letter each, so every player's words are their own: the deal picks the two letters whose words only one of you can enter are the most even (each about a third of the team's yield), so the less practised player has words that only they can play, and the other can only tell them; a word typed with only your partner's letter says so ("That one's Sarah's: it has their C, not your B"). Your hive has your letter in the middle and your partner's ringed in their colour with their initial; each word shows in the colour of whoever found it; the ranks are the team's, over every word any of you can make. The chart is split: your phone holds your partner's (what's left that they can enter, by first letter and length) and theirs holds yours, so each of you guides the other. At a party, every player gets a letter of their own, each holding the next player's chart. A team's puzzle ends when every word is found or someone calls it a day (the menu), and the pair's record keeps it as a team's: the share of the yield found, won at Master refiner. Each phone writes only its own words to the table, so two finds at once never collide; the team's yield is everyone's together (`teamFound`). The computer doesn't sit at a table: the rival is for playing alone.

What's kept, under `yield:`: `day` (today's puzzle as you've played it: the words, the word being typed, the order of the outer letters, the time), `run` (the random one), `on` (which of them is on screen), `last` (the daily before today's), `prefs`, `stats` (random puzzles started, Master refiner and Full yield reached, sprint bests), `daily` (today's points by the day, for the games screen) and `duel` (your run of a match). Everything on the screen is in them, so a partner watching sees the same board, letters in the same places.

The screen never scrolls: the gauge, your words, the word being typed and the board share the height, the hive taking what's left (390×600 and up). On an upright tablet it's drawn larger; held sideways, the board, the word and the keys stand on the left with all the height, and the gauge and every word found, alphabetically, in a box beside them with the Chart under it. Every theme by day and night: the hive's centre is honey ochre in all of them, Codex's ornaments (the hairline inside each hex, the plate's inner line) left out of the others, which `yield.css` undoes itself.

The rules (October 2026) are a sheet of their own, from the header's Rules or the menu's The rules, as the other games' are: in plain prose, what makes a word, the points (a six-letter word 6, a seven-letter pangram 14), every rank's share, which words count and how one that doesn't is told apart, the taps and keys, the Wheel, the levels, the sprint and the rival, today's puzzle, and the race and the team. The points, the ranks and the clocks are read from the engine as it opens, so the sheet can't drift from the game, and a sprint and the rival stand still while it's open, as for the menu. It shares the game's sheet class (`yd-sheet`) with the words, the chart and the ranks; the menu-like sheets keep the menu sheet's own head. Looked at on a phone (390×664, 390×844) and an iPad both ways, in Codex, Modern and Kontor by day and night.

`tests/yield.mjs` checks the words (every one a word, none with an S or one side's spelling, the blocked ones never, ordinary ones counted and SCOWL's odd forms not), every kind of word typed, points and ranks, that a puzzle's yield is exactly what a brute-force pass over every word finds and has a pangram (72 puzzles of both boards and every level), that a seed always deals the same puzzle and the levels go deeper and slower in order, today's puzzle the same everywhere and 120 days of different sets, the walk against the engine, the rival (the same game from a seed, only the puzzle's words, its levels in order), the chart's counts, a team's split (the two players' words make the team's yield and neither alone does, each with a fair share only they can enter, the same deal on every phone, through the database's round trip) and a run through JSON. Two pages on the stand-in database played a team match (a word with the partner's letter turned away and found by the partner, both phones showing it, the chart split, called a day on both) and a race to its result, with real taps; a sprint ran out with a rival; every size in every theme by day and night was looked at.

Not done: meanings for the rarer words (the clue bank has the commonest; Slate's dictionary has more, but it's Slate's to share); bonus points for the rarer words typed (they're acknowledged, not scored, so a race can't be won by trying letter strings); Boggle's rule for a race, where a word anyone else found scores nothing (the stars show those words, without the scoring); a wheel daily. For Korbi to decide: the ranks' names, the levels' bands, and whether today's figure on the games screen should be the rank rather than the points.
