# Acrostic
A hidden text in a grid of squares, and clues whose answers hold exactly its letters: every letter of every answer is numbered to a square of the text, so a letter written on either side appears on the other, and the answers' first letters, read down the clues, spell where the text comes from. It is the double-crostic Elizabeth Kingsley made for the Saturday Review in 1934, played by its usual rules: you work in from both sides, a clue giving letters to the text and a half-read word of the text giving letters back to the clues, and the source down the first letters, guessed early, gives every answer its first letter.

**The texts** are the knowledge base's own words, read from kb/ as Origin and Nexus read it, never edited: a line of literature (Punt's quotations, works before 1929, in English or a translation's own words), whose first letters spell its author and work, or what of them fits; a saying (Punt's sayings, the pop culture left out), hidden as what it says word for word and what it means, its first letters spelling the saying itself in its own language (CARPE DIEM down the clues of "Pluck the day: make the most of today. Horace, Odes I.11."), or "German saying", or the language; a place's note from Chart and Origin, spelling its name, or its name and country; a painting's note, spelling the painting or its painter; and the words of architecture and watchmaking, defined without naming themselves, spelling the word (a chronograph, a pediment). A long note may be cut where a reader pauses (a sentence's end, a semicolon, a comma in a first clause, never inside brackets) to suit a level, and the reveal gives it whole with the rest of the entry. A spine is never one whose every word the text itself contains (For Franz Marc blue meant… can't spell FRANZ MARC). The source's letters must all be in the text, since the answers' letters are the text's, and that is what keeps most short lines out: a newspaper's acrostic runs to two hundred letters, a phone's to sixty or a hundred, so a quotation of thirty letters seldom holds HAMLET. With the clue bank as it stands, about 110 texts make Easy puzzles, 190 Medium and 210 Hard, more as the bank and the knowledge base grow. The menu's Sources tick which kinds come up (an empty choice deals them all), and literature and the sayings fold out by their stages, as in Punt (German sayings only, say).

**Making one** (acrostic-make.js, in a worker so the page never loads the knowledge base or the word lists itself). First the answers: one for each letter of the spine, starting with it, that between them take exactly the text's letters. A depth-first search on the answer with the fewest words left, its words tried by how well they house the letters hardest to place (running prose has far more H, T and W than a dictionary does), keeping the vowels in step and the lengths even, with a word a clue can only define flatly (THE, HIM) taken only when the letters need it; the last two answers are looked up by what's left. Every answer is a word of the clue bank (clues.js), so each comes with its clues. Then the squares: each answer letter is given a square holding that letter, by a local search over swaps that spreads an answer across the text's words, keeps two of its letters from sitting side by side in its order (that would spell a piece of it: 1 pair in 1,225 in the tests), and above all leaves no square whose letter could change with both its answer and its word still words. Then the proof (acrostic-solve.js, fills): a search that counts the ways the letters fit, stopping at two, where every answer may be any clued word with its letter of the spine and every word of the text any word of the wider list (wordlist.js to SCOWL 40) or the text's own word (its names included); the clues are not read at all. Only a puzzle that fills one way is kept, so no square is ever a coin toss between two readings that both make words: the clues are how you get in. When there is a second way, the squares it differs in are moved and the letters laid again. Every search is bounded by its count of steps, never the clock, and every choice comes from the seed, so a seed makes the same puzzle on every phone (today's, a race's). A puzzle takes about a fifth of a second on a computer, a few times that on a phone, and the next random one is made while you solve this one.

**Difficulty** is the skill a person needs, as a sudoku's is the hardest technique it needs (acrostic-solve.js, solve and grade). A model solver works the puzzle the cheapest step first: a word of the text read off its pattern (when the commonest words leave it one way only), an answer read off its pattern, the source guessed once three fifths of a word of it are in, a famous line recognised once three fifths of its squares are, a clue cracked with the letters it has, or cold. A clue's level is its difficulty in the bank (the easy plain definition, or the harder other sense) plus one for a rarer word; letters already in its answer bring it down, a third of them one step, two thirds two. Skill 1 cracks cold only easy clues for common words, 2 a harder clue or a rarer word, 3 both, and a puzzle's grade is the least skill that finishes it. Easy: 28 to 72 letters, five to eleven answers, the commonest words wherever the letters allow, plain clues, grade 1. Medium: 42 to 92 letters, seven to fourteen answers, grade 2: the generator turns clues harder one at a time until the plain clues alone no longer finish it. Hard: 60 to 125 letters, ten to eighteen answers, rarer words and the harder clues, grade 3, somewhere a clue that must be cracked with little help. On the tests' samples the model takes 167 seconds over Easy, 221 over Medium and 334 over Hard, and every puzzle grades its level, or (on Hard, now and then) one step easier, which the generator accepts rather than fail.

**Playing** (a phone upright is the design). At the top the text's squares, each with its clue's letter in the corner as on paper and the punctuation at its edges (a digit is shown, not a square), sized once for the puzzle so nothing moves as you play, a long text scrolling inside its own box; under it the spine's line ("First letters: the work"), with its squares filling on a tall phone; the clues, each on one line with its answer's squares at the right, in a box that scrolls, the one you're on brought to the top of it; Undo, Check and Hint; and the keyboard. Tapping a square types along the text, tapping a clue's square along its answer, and tapping the square again (or the key at the keyboard's corner, which says Text or Clue) switches; the next square along is the next to type, so a word typed whole lands letter by letter. Undo takes back your letters one at a time. Check marks the wrong letters, 10 seconds for each it finds; Hint shows the letter under the cursor, 20 seconds. The keys answer to the press, not the click, so a thumb's quick taps land on the key they started on, and the reveal waits a moment after the last letter so that tap can't close it. A tablet upright draws it all larger; held sideways, the text has the left column and the clock, the spine, the clues and the keys stand beside it, the keys at the bottom.

**The rules** (October 2026) are a sheet of their own, from the header's Rules or the menu's The rules, as the other games' are: in plain prose, how the text, the answers and the spine fit together, with CARPE DIEM down the clues of "Pluck the day: make the most of today" as the example, working in from both sides, the taps and keys, Undo, Check and Hint and what they cost, the levels with their sizes, the twists and sources, today's, and the two ways together. Its numbers come from the engine as it opens, so the sheet can't drift from the game, and a solo puzzle stands still while it's open, as for the menu. Looked at on a phone (390×664, 390×844) and an iPad both ways, in Codex, Modern and Kontor by day and night.

**Twists**, from the genre's own variants: the Classic; the Telestich, the old form whose source runs down the answers' last letters, so the spine tells you how each answer ends rather than how it begins (a weaker help, and a different way in); and Half clued, where half the clues are blank and those words come from the text alone, chosen so the puzzle still finishes at the level's skill with only the rest: it moves the weight from vocabulary to reading. Left out: the double acrostic (first and last letters both spelling something), which needs two names of the same length that the knowledge base seldom gives one text; and the cryptic acrostic, which needs cryptic clues, which the clue bank deliberately hasn't.

**Together.** For the pair, a team match (the partner sheet's request; together.js): one puzzle, each of you holding half the clues, the other half showing as "Sarah's clue", so you read yours aloud. The split is the generator's, chosen so that neither half alone finishes it: the model solver at the highest skill, reading only one half, gets stuck, which the tests check for every puzzle; the halves are as even as the clues allow. Either of you types anywhere, your partner's letters in their colour; the clock is the table's and doesn't stop; if your partner is away, the menu lets you read their clues too; Another together deals a new one for both. The pair's record keeps your best time together. At a party (Party, up to six), a race on duel.js: the same puzzle for everyone at the table, the others' letters right shown in the strip as they go, the fastest solve (hints and checks added) wins, and the text's reveal sits under the result. The page tells a party from the pair by its address (a party's carries ?party=), so the pair's request is always the team match and Party's always the race. Solo, there is no computer to race (3 November 2026, Korbi: "'against the computer' makes little sense"; "probably drop from all").

**Today's** is Medium, Classic, from every source, made from the day (today() in suite.js) alike on every phone. The pool is in a fixed order (each text by its hash, so a growing knowledge base moves it little) and each day enters it a golden-ratio step of its length on from the day before: drawn independently, a text would come back within weeks; this way 120 days gave 105 different texts, none back within 29 days. The games screen's figure is today's time, counted the moment its last letter is right. Random puzzles pass over the sixty texts you've had last.

**Kept** under acrostic: run (the puzzle whole, your letters, the clock, hints and checks), prefs (level, twist, sources and their stages), times (bests by level and twist, lowest first, kept apart from acrostic:best, which the games screen reads highest first), seen, and duel for a race's run; a team's table lives in the pair's room. A partner watching sees your puzzle from acrostic:run, saved with every letter and every five seconds of the clock.

**Tests** (tests/acrostic.mjs): the text laid out as squares (digits shown, punctuation at the edges, a word split where a mark falls inside it); the hidden texts (every source has them, no pop-culture saying, no spine the text gives away); 23 puzzles across the levels and twists: answers holding exactly the text's letters, each letter a square of its own that holds it, the spine down the first (or last) letters, every answer a clued word with one of its own clues, each level's size, the proof finding one fill for every puzzle and catching a text that fills two ways (CAT and DOG laid into "Cat dog": CANDID fills it too), a seed making the same puzzle; the grading (every puzzle finishes at its grade's skill and not at the skill below, longer for a person the higher the level, Hard with clues only a rarer word answers); the rules of filling and moving; the twists; the team's split (half each, neither half alone finishes, both together do); today's the same on every phone, tomorrow's another, a month of days entering the pool far apart; a run through JSON, and a team's table and a race's match through the database's round trip. In a browser, phones played it through with real taps on the keys, a team of two tabs and a race of two on the stand-in database (README, Together), today's from the tile, the rival, the pause, a reload and a partner's watching frame.

Not done: answers of more than one word (the bank is single words), so a text's letters must go into single words; and the knowledge base's notes are long and its lines short, so Places make up most of the pool and literature, which suits the genre best, comes up least.
