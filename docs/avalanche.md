# Avalanche
The sixth idle game (October 2026; README, Idle): `avalanche.html`, `avalanche.js` and `avalanche.css`, its rules in `avalanche-engine.js`. A slope seven squares across and eight down, above a road. Each square holds up to three layers of snow (a tick for each, and a full square cracked, so depths read without their shade); a fourth sets it sliding: four layers go, one onto each neighbour, which can slide in turn, round and back, so a square can slide twice in one avalanche. This is the sandpile model (Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld's, 1987), the standard example of self-organised criticality: a pile fed one grain at a time settles where the next grain can set off an avalanche of any size. Snow sliding off the top or the sides is gone; off the foot it lands on the road. Each day you lay up to three charges (23 October 2026; it was one), a layer of your own each on any squares, and then fire. Nothing slides while you lay them: press a square and the avalanche everything laid so far would set off with one more there is shown (every square that would slide ringed, the bridges dotted, the points above it, and in the line under the slope the slides, the bridges, what goes onto the road and the chalets in the way), and let go on it to lay it. A laid charge is a diamond in the square's corner; tapping one lifts it (once the day's three are laid, a tap on a laid square shows what lifting it would do), and Undo takes back the last. Fire sets everything over its limit sliding at once, as one avalanche, and the order the charges were laid in makes no difference (the sandpile settles the same whatever order the squares are taken in; a test lays three charges in three orders and gets the same slope and score). A slide you set off is controlled, the road closed for it, and scores ten times the square of what it counts for: its size, the number of slides in it, and the bridges. A bridge is a square that slid having started the avalanche short of full: it counts one extra for each layer it was short of three, so a bare square the chain had to fill itself counts three. The extra is the point of laying three: a charge that sets nothing off by itself, a layer on a bare square, can be the link between two loaded stretches, and the avalanche that then runs through both counts for far more than either; and a square one layer short that is simply tipped over is no bridge (the dots on the slope show where they are, one for each layer filled). Ten slides with no bridges are 1,000, twenty 4,000; ten with three bridges are thirteen, 1,690. Then night falls (a charge laid is not kept to the next day: Fire clears them) and snow lands on squares at random, layer by layer: two a night at first, three after 20 days, four after 50 and five after 100. Whatever it sets off runs where it runs and scores nothing. The plough clears a little off the road each morning, but a night whose slides send three layers or more onto it buries it, a life lost, and the third ends the game. Two chalets stand on the slope (never on the foot): a slide through a chalet's square carries it away, to be built again elsewhere, and each chalet a slide of yours carries away halves what that slide scores (the press shows it ringed in red, and the halving), so where your own avalanche runs matters as well as how big it is: the biggest slide on the slope may be worth less than a smaller one that misses the chalets, and a loaded stretch round a chalet is one you'd rather the night didn't set off either. And each night's wind is forecast by day, streaks on the side it blows from: from the west it loads the east side of the slope (each column's share of the snow rising across it, seven to one), from the east the west side, and a still night spreads it evenly; two nights in five blow each way and one is still. The line under the slope says how much snow is coming tonight, on what wind, and how many squares above the road are full. The score and your best are in the strip, the best kept as `avalanche:best` the moment it's beaten, so the games screen has it; the game in play is kept (`avalanche:run`, with the charges laid so far, so a partner watching sees them and a game opened again has them as they were) and opens again where it was.

Terrain (menu, Slope: Open or Rocky, chosen for the next game and kept in `avalanche:opts`). A rocky slope has four squares of bare rock, none on the top row or the foot, drawn as stones: a rock holds no snow, the night's snow never falls on one (the draw is made again), a charge can't be laid on one, and what slides onto one is turned aside and gone, as off the side. So a rock stops a chain where it would have run on, and shelters whatever is behind it from the night. It makes a game much easier to last in (the careful player below goes from about 120 days to 320 and hits the 400-day limit of the harness on some seeds), so a rocky game keeps its own best, `avalanche:rocky-best`, and never touches `avalanche:best`, the one the games screen shows. The open slope is the same game as before, to the same seeds: a game saved without rocks has none. Held sideways the slope is on the left with all the height, the score, the lives and the line beside it.

Why three layers: with the road buried by any snow at all, careful play hardly mattered (about 40 days against 26 for charging anywhere), because every full square along the foot was a burial waiting for one flake and one charge a day can't empty seven. Needing three, a night has to send a real avalanche down, so the danger is a loaded stretch of slope that reaches the foot, and a charge can take that down first, on your terms and for points.

Why these numbers: played out by simple players (`options` in the engine gives what one more charge on each square would do, `foresee` what a set of three would; `node tests/avalanche.mjs measure` plays them, 8 games each on seeds 100 up, chalets and wind and all): three squares anywhere have the road buried three times in about 46 days (30 to 90) and score about 54,000; each charge laid in turn where it makes the biggest avalanche with those before it lasts about 101 days and scores about 400,000; the same weighing the full squares it leaves near the foot lasts about 121 (68 to 161) for about 490,000; and trying every set of three squares and taking the best lasts about 105 for about 495,000. Bridges are about half of what a good player scores (53% of the careful player's), so they are the game rather than a sideline: a player who chooses by slides alone (a bridge-blind one) is not worse off for it: it lasts longer (about 146 days) and scores about 570,000, because it hasn't built big chains for the night to find. So the bridges are not yet a reason to play differently, only a bonus on the way, and that is left to see how it plays before the weights are changed. Setting up pays: on positions met in play, the best three charges beat laying each where it does most by more than a tenth on 73% of the days. The scores are in the hundreds of thousands now, where one charge a day made tens of thousands, because an avalanche's worth goes with the square of its size and three charges build far bigger ones. The earlier scores on the games screen are lower than a game now makes and stay as they are. Every random thing (the snow dealt and where each night's falls) comes from the game's seed and the number of the draw, so a game opened again goes on as it would have.

Why the foot is left as it is (reviewed October 2026, when a day had one charge; the numbers below are from then): a player who fires a full square on the bottom row whenever there is one, and otherwise the biggest slide, outlasts that careful player (about 139 days against 99) but scores two-thirds as much (about 46,000), and one charging the fullest square nearest the road lasts 118 days for 25,000. Keeping the foot clear is the safe way to play and big slides the rewarding one; the best score comes from weighing the two, which is the game. Burying the road only under a night avalanche of ten slides or more was tried and left: the foot habit fell to about 74 days, but firing the biggest slide each day became about as good as careful play (85 days and 54,000 against 92 and 62,000), so every day's choice came down to the biggest slide. Needing five or six slides kept careful play ahead but shortened every game by a third.

Danger shading (October 2026). Players said they were mostly guessing, and the one thing hidden was which squares would bury the road at night. The slope now hatches (oblique lines in the danger enamel, so it reads without the colour in all three themes) every square where a single layer of snow tonight would, by itself, bury the road: `danger` in the engine adds one layer to each square of the slope as the day's charges would leave it, lets it settle with the same rules as the night (rocks included) and asks whether `BURY` layers reach the road. It is exact for one flake and says nothing about two landing together, so a clear slope is safer, not safe; wind is not part of it, since where the snow falls is the one thing it doesn't know. It moves with the charges: laying or lifting one, or just pressing a square to see it, redraws the hatching for what the slope would be after the day's avalanche. A line under the slope counts it ("3 squares would bury the road tonight", or that none would), in a row that is always the same height. Menu, Settings, Danger shading turns it off (`avalanche:opts`, on by default). Nothing else changes: the score, the seeds and old saves are untouched.

The lesson (October 2026). A first game opens on three days on slopes laid out by hand (`avalanche-lesson.js`, pure, the same for everyone), each step in plain words under the slope and done by doing it (Fire waits for the step): day 1, three full squares touching, one charge sets off all three and scores the square of the size (90, against 10 for one alone); day 2, two loaded stretches with one square a layer short between them, a charge on each and the gap is carried along as a bridge, so the reach is 6 and the points 360 against 40 for a stretch alone; day 3, loaded squares at the foot hatched as the danger, charge the block, and the scripted night's snow falls on the hatched squares without burying the road. After each fire the line says what happened and why in a sentence or two, and Next goes on. The nights are scripted (`fire(game, script)` takes the squares the layers land on, so the lesson plays the same for everyone), nothing in the lesson counts for the best, and its three days are kept in the run itself (`lesson`, the day; `told`, what the day came to) so a reload or a partner's screen finds it where it was. After day 3 (or Skip lesson, always in the strip, or in the menu) a normal game starts fresh from a seed, and `avalanche:taught` is set. A visit with no game and no earlier play opens on it; a game already in play is left alone. Menu, Settings, Replay the lesson keeps the game you were in under `avalanche:held` and gives it back afterwards.

`tests/avalanche-lesson.mjs` checks the shading against a brute-force flake on every square of a few hundred slopes (rocky ones too), that it follows laid and lifted charges, the scripted night, that each lesson day can be done and that doing it scores, never buries the road and tells whole words, that day 3's night buries it if the block is left, and that a game saved before the lesson, or in the middle of it, opens as it was.

`tests/avalanche.mjs` (rebuilt for three charges, and a rocky slope: the rocks from the seed and clear of the top and foot, snow turned aside by a rock and none made or lost, no charge or snow on a rock, a rocky game saved and opened again, the open slope unchanged) checks the deal from a seed (nothing full yet, two chalets off the foot, a wind forecast), a slide (four layers to the neighbours; at the foot onto the road, at the side gone; nothing set off below the limit), a row sliding one after another both ways, a square sliding twice in one avalanche, no snow made or lost, the day's charge and its points, the game charged from left as it was, the night's snow layer by layer, the day's slide onto the road not counting, a night sending three layers or more burying it, a bare slope where nothing slides, the points, the pace, chalets (the day's three charges as one avalanche whatever their order, and the bridges counted: one square short by a layer, by three, a square merely tipped over being none; a slide through one carrying it away for half the score, built again elsewhere, two quartering it), the wind (from the west loading the east side, from the east the west, still spreading it; the next night's forecast), the end at the third burial, a game saved and opened again, and whole games: charging anywhere buried three times in under 60 days, careful charging lasting half as long again or more.
