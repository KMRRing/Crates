# Texas Hold'em

Texas Hold'em (德州撲克) as Macau's regulation has it (October 2026), at a table of up to six: `holdem.html`, `holdem.js` and `holdem.css`; the rules in `holdem-rules.js` (a seats.js game: `deal`, `act`, `wake`, `over`), the hands' evaluator and the chances in `holdem-eval.js`, the computer players in `holdem-bot.js`, the rules on one page in `holdem-sheet.js`, and the course in `holdem-course.js` on the stage `holdem-stage.js`. Hold'em is the one casino game here where a decision changes the odds, so it is played against people (or the computer players) for chips, not against the house; the house only takes a commission. Its tile and logos are in `apps.js`, its entry in `rooms.js` (`GAMES`) and its place at a party's table in `party-core.js` (`ELSEWHERE`).

The game, by the regulation (Despacho 11/2008; the article numbers are its): one pack of 52, two hole cards face down for each player, five community cards face up in three stages, the flop (3), the turn (1) and the river (1), and a round of betting before the flop and after each stage. The button (指示器, Article 1) moves one seat clockwise every hand; the player left of it posts the small blind (小盲注) and the next the big blind (大盲注, Article 5); before the flop the player left of the big blind acts first and the big blind last (Article 6), and from the flop the first player left of the button acts first and the button last (Article 7). A pack is dealt from the small blind a card at a time with a card burned before each stage. A move is fold (蓋牌, Article 13), check, call or raise, a raise being at least the last bet or raise (Article 12), and all in (全押) a bet of every chip you have; there is no limit on a bet but your chips (Article 11). At the showdown the best five of a player's seven cards win (Article 8): suits never rank (Article 14), the other cards count as the regulation has it (Article 15: one kicker for four of a kind, two for three of a kind, one for two pair, three for a pair), equal hands split the pot and the odd chips go to the first of them left of the button (Article 10). The house takes 3 to 5% of the pot, never more than five big blinds (Article 16).

What this table decides, because the regulation does not say (the rules page says so too): the blinds are HK$50 and HK$100 and everyone starts with HK$10,000, a hundred big blinds; a game is 10, 20 or 40 hands (the menu), and a player who goes broke buys in again for the same, so the result is chips less every buy-in and the most ahead wins. Heads-up the button is the small blind, acting first before the flop and last after it, as casinos play it. A player all in can win only what the others matched: the pot is split in layers (the main pot and side pots) and the chips nobody matched go back to their owner and are neither contested nor raked (a last layer of its own, `back`); the odd chip of a layer goes to the first winner left of the button. An all-in bet short of a full raise can be called but does not let players who had already acted raise again. The commission is 5%, 3% or none (the menu; 5% by default), taken from the contested chips only, never more than five big blinds (HK$500) and rounded down to the dollar. After the last bet, when everyone left is all in, the board is turned up a stage at a time (the flop, then the turn, then the river, a beat each) with each hand's chance of winning beside it, counted over the boards still to come, and then the hands are shown with the five cards that win lit. The regulation's terms for check and call (過牌, 跟注) were not found in it, and neither were side pots, a numeric limit on a bet, the heads-up blinds, the short all-in rule or buying in again, so none of those is claimed to be its.

The page: the strip has the hand, the pot and the street. The other seats sit round the top and flank yours at the foot, in order clockwise from you, each with its name, chips, the bet this round (a chip beside the stack), the button, SB, BB or REBUY, its two cards face down (face up at a showdown) and what it last did; in the middle the pot, the five places of the board and what the narrator says (the same words are spoken, unless the voice is off). Under the table your cards, with the hand they make once there is a flop (or "Pair of kings", "Ace-king suited" before it) and whose turn it is; the size of a raise (the sizes worth a tap in the spot, below, each saying what it's for, the one the raise is at lit, and a slider that is finer for small raises and snaps to HK$50; a decision starts at the textbook size); and the buttons, Fold (off when there is nothing to call, because a free fold is never right), Check or Call and Raise to (a label too long for a phone's width, "Raise to 1,200" or a call all in, drops the wide lettering rather than be cut short). Held sideways the table is on the left and your cards, the size and the buttons beside it. The sizes (October 2026). The raise buttons were the least, half the pot, three quarters, the pot and all in, the same in every spot; Korbi asked instead for the sizes a strong player would weigh, worked out for the spot, after the coach seemed to favour big bets (around HK$500). They are now what bet-sizing theory has a player consider (`sizes` in `holdem-rules.js`), each with a word for what it's for. Before the flop: an open of 2, 2½ or 3 big blinds, one more for each player who limped in (2½ the textbook one; PokerCoaching's preflop guide calls 2 to 2½ a solid default); a 3-bet of three times the raise in position (acting after the raiser on the later streets) and four out of it (Upswing's and PokerCoaching's rule), one more for each player who called the raise (the usual squeeze); a 4-bet of 2.2 times in position and 2.5 out of it (the usual range); with fifteen big blinds or fewer, all in is the textbook raise. After it: a third of the pot (small: solvers' usual size on a dry flop, with much of the range, for thin value and to protect middling hands), three quarters (big: a polarized range on a wet board, the draws charged), the geometric size, 1¼ pots (an overbet, for the side holding the strongest hands; offered only while the geometric size is under the pot, since past it the geometric size is the overbet) and all in; facing a bet, three times it, the geometric raise (the pot on the river) and all in. The geometric size is the share f of the pot that, bet on each street to come, puts the stacks in on the river: (1 + 2f)ⁿ = 1 + 2·stack/pot over n streets, the stack what the others can match (110% of the pot on the flop of a single-raised pot at a hundred big blinds; GTO Wizard's "Pot Geometry": it gets the most money from a calling range, for a range that is strong or nothing). A size that would leave less than a third of the pot behind is all in instead (there would be nothing left to play for), and of two sizes within a tenth of the pot only one is offered, the geometric over a plain fraction. One size in each spot is the textbook one (the open of 2½, the 3-bet that fits the position, a third on the flop, three quarters on the turn and river: ranges narrow and the bets left polarize them); a decision starts with the slider there. Why the coach seemed to like big bets (a scratch run: the coach in one seat of five against Hard players, 791 of its decisions, 160 deals each): its pick among the old sizes was within about two standard errors of every other size in nine raises in ten, and before the flop the computer players folded as often to a raise to 200 as to one to 350 (51 to 53%), so which size came top was mostly the deals' luck, a big one as often as a small one (after the flop it bet the least more often than any other size). Now, between sizes its deals can't tell apart, it plays the textbook one, as long as raising still beats not raising, and it goes all in only when that is clearly better than the next best move (of several noisy figures the noisiest tops the list by luck most often, and none is noisier than a shove's): in a fresh run of 767 decisions, 357 of its 463 raises were the textbook size (87% of its opens, 80% of its 3-bets, about half its bets after the flop, where its figures tell the sizes apart more often: the geometric size with a strong hand, a third for thin value), and it never moved all in first before the flop, where the old coach had 21 times in its 791. Against the coach as it was, on the same 2,000 hands (the coach in one seat of four against three Hard players, the same decks and the computer players' same luck, 160 deals a decision), it made 191 ± 36 chips a hand against 228 ± 32: 37 ± 31 less, within the run's noise, and what there is of it before the flop, where the old coach's cheapest re-raise (to 400 over an open to 250) took the pot as often as a 3-bet three times the size, because the computer players fold to a re-raise whatever it costs. The new sizes are the ones that hold up against people; these computer players don't price a raise by its size before the flop. The rules page says what each size does and asks (a call needs bet ÷ (pot + 2 × bet): 20% against a third, 30% against three quarters).

The computer players (Easy, Medium and Hard; Hard by default) use only what their seat may see, the same as you: their cards and the board turned up so far. Before the flop they count the hand as Bill Chen did (the higher card A 10, K 8, Q 7, J 6, otherwise half its number; a pair double, five at least; 2 more if suited; less 1, 2, 4 or 5 for a gap of one, two, three or more; 1 more if the cards are close and both below a queen; rounded up) and open the betting with a hand worth 9 or more from an early seat, 6 or more from the button and between them in between, with fewer than fifteen big blinds going all in or out. After the flop they work out their chance of winning against hands dealt at random by drawing boards, trim it when the bet against them is large (whoever bets holds more than a random hand), and call when it beats the price of the call (the pot odds), raise a strong hand and, the harder levels, bluff and slow-play now and then. Easy plays looser, bluffs never and calls too much. In the tests Hard beats Easy and Medium beats Easy over 24 games of 20 hands, and heads-up Hard is no worse than Medium.

The coach (Menu → Coach, October 2026) speaks in one of four ways, as Big Two's, Hong's, Guan Dan's and Schafkopf's do. Hints, the default: nothing unless you ask; Hint, in the coach's box, gives its move for the decision you are at ("Raise to 250 (2½ BB) · everyone folds 54% of the time · worth +HK$120 on average", a raise named by its size among the buttons; facing a raise, "Call 250 · about 73% to win, a call needs 36% · worth +HK$1,650 on average"), with Why? after it: every move it weighed, its own first, each with the chips it comes to beyond folding (and ±, the standard error: how far an average over that many deals may stray by chance), how often it wins the pot and how often everyone folds. Check: Hint too, and after each move of yours it would not have made, its word in oxblood so it isn't taken for advice: how many chips worse it reckons yours and what tells most against it ("Your fold: about HK$9,300 worse than all in for 9,900. You win about 100% against the hands that bet; the call needs 43%."; or that a smaller raise takes the pot as often for less, that a bigger one risks more than its extra folds are worth, that the hands that stay in beat yours more often than the folds pay). Always: the same, and at each of your decisions its move without your asking, ringed on the button that plays it, the raise slider set to its size. Off: nothing, and no Hint. A move counts as agreeing when it is the coach's or as good as near as its deals can tell: no more than a big blind (HK$100) worse, and not two standard errors clear of it, so a close decision is never called a mistake. The word stays until a move of yours it agrees with; a fold when there is nothing to call is not offered, because it is never right. The box keeps three lines' room whatever is said, a longer word is cut there, and a tap on the line opens Why? all the same. Under Check and Always a hand ends with how often the coach agreed with your moves, and Where it differed lists the three that cost most. A partner's frame, watched from the games screen, coaches nobody; at a table with friends each phone's coach is its own, and it sees only what its seat does.

How it weighs a decision (`holdem-think.js`; the words are `holdem-coach.js`'s, the work runs in `holdem-worker.js` so the table never stops, with 80 deals on the page's own thread where a browser has no worker). Hold'em has hidden cards, so the coach cannot search a tree of moves; it samples. It plays every move the seat could make (fold, call or check, each size of the raise buttons) out to the end of the hand, in the same deals of the cards it cannot see, and sets them side by side by the chips each comes to beyond folding, the commission taken. Between sizes of a raise its deals can't tell apart (by the check's own rule: no more than a big blind behind, or not two standard errors), it plays the textbook size, as long as that still comes to more than not raising. A deal is the others' two cards and the rest of the board, drawn from the pack less what the seat knows; everyone plays on by Medium's rules, and the seat too after its first move, but more steadily than Medium (it believes a bet a little more, raises only with a monster and never bluffs: Medium's own habit of bluff-raising and stacking off made every call look about 3,000 chips worse than it is). Every move is played in the same deals, so the moves are set against each other on the same footing, and a computer player's chances are counted once for a deal and shared by every move tried in it. A move goes once it is clearly behind the best (three and a half standard errors in the first hundred deals, two and a half after, and a twentieth of a big blind), so a decision takes about half a second on this machine, four seconds at most when it is close and all 320 deals are played; a backstop of six seconds (seven for a check) is for a slow phone. The seed comes from the position, so the same spot gives the same advice on any phone, and a Hint, Always's move and a check all agree. It knows only what the seat may: `known()` strips the others' cards and the board still to come before it starts, and `tests/holdem-coach.mjs` checks that changing either changes nothing.

What a seat has done says what it holds. The hand's `log` (`holdem-rules.js` keeps it in the state, and it survives the database's dropping of empty lists: every check, call and raise, with the pot, the bet to meet and the street) gives each other seat a weight for every two cards it could hold: a hand that bet is drawn from the stronger end of what it could be, the more so the bigger the bet; one that called from the middle upward (the price sets how far up from), without the very top, which would have raised; one that checked from the weaker end. The weights multiply over the hand, so a seat that raised before the flop and bet the flop is drawn from the top of the top. A hand is ranked among all that were possible on the board of that moment (before the flop by Chen's count, after it by the made hand with a bonus for a draw). The weights were set against what Medium's players really held when they acted, and the test keeps them there: for each kind of action the average rank of the hands that took it is within a tenth of the real one. This is a model of the computer players, not of people: the coach finds the computer players' habits (they fold to a re-raise; they pay a small bet with too little), which is the point at this table and nothing to rely on at another. One change to the computer players came with it: a seat facing an all-in it cannot raise now calls with a hand worth 12 or more by Chen's count before the flop (they used to fold aces to a 100-big-blind shove, so the coach found that shoving any hand was best), and `botMove` takes `samples`, `equity` and `tune` so the coach can play the others faster and itself more steadily.

How well it plays, measured (a scratch run, not kept in the repository): the coach in one seat of four against three Hard players, 1,600 hands from fixed decks, the same decks and seat for a Hard player and a Medium one in its place. The coach made 202 chips a hand (two big blinds), Hard −3 and Medium 13: ahead of Hard by 205 ± 46 chips a hand (162 over the first 800 hands, 248 over the second; ± is the standard error, how far an average over that many hands may stray by chance) and ahead of Medium by 189 ± 43. It ends 77% of its hands with chips won, Hard 25%: most of the gain is the computer players folding to a raise more often than a person would, which the coach has learned from their rules. So the figure says it reads these players well, not that it would beat people. On the sizes of October 2026 (above) the same kind of run, 2,000 hands, gave it 191 ± 36 chips a hand.
