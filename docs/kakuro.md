# Kakuro
A crossword of sums (October 2026). The grid is black cells and white ones; every run of white cells, across or down, holds different digits 1 to 9 that add up to the clue at its head: the black cell just left of an across run, or just above a down run, split by a diagonal, the across sum in its upper right and the down sum in its lower left. No run is shorter than two cells, and every grid has exactly one solution. Two rules, chosen in the menu (Rules): **Sums**, the classic, and **Products**, where each run's digits multiply to its clue. Three sizes (Size), counted as the whole grid with its row and column of clues: 8×8 for a few minutes, 10×10, and 12×12, the most a phone shows legibly. The grid is drawn on the digit grid Sudoku made (README, Sudoku), its clue cells and black cells as that board draws them; Kakuro uses its files as they are. There is nothing to choose from the knowledge base: the genre's content is its arithmetic, and a picture or a theme would only decorate it.

**How a grid is made, with one solution.** `kakuro-solve.js` is an exact solver: candidates as bit masks, each run kept to the ways it can still be filled (for each combination of different digits that makes its clue, which cells can take which of its digits, worked out in layers over the run's cells), each change passed to the runs that cross it, then a search on the cell with the fewest candidates, counting solutions up to two. `kakuro-engine.js` makes a grid in four steps. A pattern: the first row and column black, the inside symmetric under a half turn (a printed Kakuro's convention), black cells added a mirrored pair at a time, never one that leaves a run of one or splits the white cells apart, weighted toward the ones that cut a run too long, the ones touching a black cell corner to corner (which draws the staircases printed grids have) and the ones that leave runs of three to five, since a grid of pairs is a lattice, not a Kakuro; about a third of the inside black, runs of two to seven (Products: four, and a little more black). A fill: a digit in every white cell, none twice in a run, found by a search on the cell with the fewest digits left, which never closes a rectangle of a b over b a in two across runs and the same two down runs, since those four can be swapped without changing any clue and no grid with one has one solution. A lean: a cell at a time takes the digit that moves its two runs' clues toward the level's doubt, the number of ways a clue can be made (16 in two is only 7 9; 10 in two is four ways), measured in bits. And the clues worked out from the fill; while the solver finds two solutions, a cell where they differ (one in the most doubtful runs first) takes another digit, the one that leaves its runs the fewest ways, and the clues are worked out again. When that goes round in circles a fresh fill is quicker than more tries. The lean is what makes the large grids possible at all: a 12×12 filled at random took 23 seconds a grid, and eight fills in nine never came to one solution; leaned, it takes a third to two thirds of a second. Every number comes from the seed by integer arithmetic (the doubt's logarithm too, worked out by squaring, since a browser may round its own `Math.log2`), so a seed makes the same grid on every phone, which is how today's grid and a match's are the same everywhere, and how a link (`#s=SEED&r=RULES&z=SIZE&l=LEVEL`, or `#d=DAY`) brings one back.

**How hard a grid is, and the levels.** Difficulty is how much a person has to work out, never the number of clues. `kakuro-logic.js` is a human-style solver that always takes the easiest step it can find, each technique a function that finds one step and says why. It takes as written what a solver pencils in from the sums (a cell keeps the digits some combination of each of its runs still allows, less the digits already in either run), and its steps are, rank 1: the last cell of a run (the rest of its clue); a cell whose two runs leave it one digit (across 3 in two allows 1 2, down 4 in two 1 3: where they cross, 1); a digit every combination of a run holds with one cell that can take it. Rank 2: the ways that fit, a run's combinations narrowed to those its cells can hold, every digit placed where it can go and each a different one (pairs and triples in a run follow from it). Rank 3: a block's sums, the cousin of Killer's rule of 45: inside a rectangle of the grid, the across runs it holds whole cover every cell but those whose across run leaves it, the down runs every cell but those whose down run does, so the difference of their clues is what the one or two leftover cells come to. Rank 4: a chain, a cell of two candidates where one of them, followed through the first-rank steps it forces for up to a dozen cells, leaves a run that can't be made. Nearly every grid needs the ways that fit somewhere, so the hardest technique alone hardly tells grids apart; what does is how often a person must stop and work something out. So a grid's level is its work, the steps beyond rank 1, for each white cell, with its hardest technique: **Easy** needs the ways that fit at most once for every twelve cells, **Medium** up to once for every four or five, **Hard** up to once for every two or three, or a block's sums, **Expert** a chain, or working out at nearly every other cell. Expert is offered for Sums at 10×10 and 12×12; an 8×8 has too few cells for it, and Products, whose clues give more away (a product with a 5 or a 7 in it shows it, and a product has fewer ways than a sum of the same length), comes out Easy to Hard, so its menu stops there. Each level's fill starts at a doubt of its own (half a bit a run for Easy, 1.3 for Hard and Expert); a grid that comes out easier than its level leans the next try's fill to more doubt, a harder one to less, within the level's range (past about 1.6 bits most fills can't be brought to one solution, and chains come from a grid's shape more than from its doubt). About one grid in thirty on a 10×10, and one in ten or so on a 12×12, needs more than every technique here, and is never dealt; a grid not found at its level in forty tries is dealt as the nearest level under it, called by the level it is (it hasn't happened in testing). On the machine here a grid takes a tenth to two thirds of a second in the median, the worst seen a second and a half (a 12×12 Hard); a phone is slower, so grids are made in a worker (`kakuro-worker.js`) and one spare of each kind is kept on the device, made in the background, so New grid is at once; the rete shows only when there's none.

**Today's grid** is Sums, 10×10, at Medium, the same for everyone (`today()`'s date its seed), counted the moment its last digit goes in: the games screen shows today's time (`noteDayTime`, kept in `kakuro:today`) and opens on it while it's still to play. It's always played alone, with the computer off, so the times compare.

**Hints teach.** Hint costs 30 seconds. A wrong digit comes first, struck through; then pencil marks that leave out the digit their cell needs; then the easiest step from the grid as it stands, counting your pencil marks where you've made them, named in the line under the grid ("Down 14 in two has 9 in it: the last cell is 5"; "Across 12 in three can only be 1 2 9 or 1 3 8 now: the other ways need a digit its cells can't hold"), its cells lit and its target picked out. A digit's cell is selected for you to write it yourself, and candidates a step strikes out go from your pencil marks there. Fill in every pencil mark (the menu) pencils every empty cell with what its two sums allow, the same marks the solver takes as written.

**The helper**, a setting (Show the ways a run can be, off by default): with a cell selected, the line under the grid lists the ways each of its two runs can add up, counting the digits already in each (a way must hold them): "Across 23 in 3: 6 8 9", "Down 20 in 4: 1 2 8 9 · 1 3 7 9 · 1 4 6 9 …", and the keys of the digits neither run leaves room for are dimmed. It's the combination table a solver keeps beside a printed Kakuro, and it costs nothing; whether it should cost something (or count against a best) is Korbi's to say. Mark mistakes (on by default) marks in oxblood what can be seen to be wrong without the solution: a digit twice in a run, and a run whose digits can no longer make its clue (all in and not adding up, or no way left for its empty cells). A run that's full and adds up has its clue fade, as a solver ticks a clue off.

**The computer** (3 November 2026, Korbi: "'against the computer' makes little sense"; "probably drop from all": no rival that races you, on any puzzle) is the same solver at a person's pace (`kakuro-bot.js`): each step takes the time a person needs for its technique (a crossing or a run's last cell a few seconds to ten, quicker with many on offer and slower with one; the ways a run of three can fit half a minute and of five three quarters of a minute; a block's sums a minute and a half; a chain a minute and a half to three), with a little luck either way; at three paces, Steady (a newspaper solver), Keen (a regular) and Sharp (a competition solver). Over today's kind of grid (10×10 Medium) they take a median of about 17, 10 and 5 minutes; Keen takes 3 minutes over an 8×8 Easy, 14 over a 10×10 Hard, 27 over a 10×10 Expert and 29 over a 12×12 Expert. In the menu (Computer: Off, With it): **With it** splits the clues between you as a pair at a table splits them (below): you hold the across clues and it the down ones, a few shared, its clues a "?" on your grid, its digits arriving in copper as it finds them. It works from the clues it holds and your right digits (a wrong one of yours it leaves alone, as a partner who checks your sums would), and waits for you when that isn't enough. A grid solved with it isn't your best: it's a team's time.

**Playing together.** A duo match (asked for from the partner sheet) or a Party's table of up to six (a race first; Together is in the menu), on the head-to-head table (`duel.js`: the lobby, Ready, the countdown to a start everyone shares), in either of two ways, switched from the menu (Race instead, Together instead), which deals a new match for everyone at the rules, size and level of whoever asks. **A race**: the same grid for everyone, each on their own phone, the others' digits to go in the line over the grid; the lowest time with hints wins; the pair's record counts the wins. **Together** (a pair's match starts this way): one grid for the table, and each player sees only their share of the clues, the others' a "?". What anyone writes is everyone's at once, in that player's colour, and anyone may write anywhere; the cell each of the others is on is framed on your grid in their colour with their initial (as in Sudoku), so two don't set off for the same one; pencil marks stay your own; hints are the team's (30 seconds each, the hint shown to whoever asked); the team's time runs from the start to the last right digit, recorded as the pair's best. For two, one holds the across clues and the other the down ones. That is Kakuro's own seam: every white cell is where an across run meets a down run, so every digit needs both players, and the less able one can't watch, since the other can't place a digit without what they hold. On its own, though, it can't be finished without talking: at the start neither can place a single digit. So clues are then shown to both, a few at a time, until a relay finishes it: each player works out all they can from what they see (with the techniques up to the grid's own rank), what they find goes to the other, and round again. The clues shown are the ones worth most: those whose cells the other player already sees the crossing clues of, so they'd hold both sums there; among clues worth as much, those of whoever holds the most. As few are shared as that takes, about a third (a fifth to three fifths), so on a 10×10 each player still holds five to sixteen clues the other can't see (ten or more, usually), and each finds a fair share of the digits (in the relay, the one who finds fewer still finds a third to a half of them). Shared at random instead, as first tried, half to four fifths had to be shown, and a player could be left holding one clue. A table of three to six gets its clues dealt round it a clue cell at a time (a clue cell's across and down sums to one player, the next one's to the next) and the same relay. The tests check that neither share alone has one solution (each has many, by the exact solver), that between them the players see every clue, and that the relay finishes, so the table can finish without a word (saying "what's your down 16?" is quicker). The players who began at the start hold the shares; one who sits down later shares one of theirs; a share whose player has been away for 20 seconds is lent to everyone else until they're back, so a phone that sleeps never leaves the table stuck. The split is worked out from the grid and the table's size on each phone, in the worker, the same everywhere. Computer players don't sit at a table, as in Sudoku: a seat nobody has is no share at all, and a share that goes unheld is lent, which serves the table better than a computer filling it in.
**Co-op** (3 November 2026, `docs/coop.md`, `kakuro-coop.js`, `digits-coop.js`): a paired partner who watches a solo grid can ask to join it, and both then write on one grid with every clue shown to both: not Together above, which splits the clues on `duel.js` and is never this. Digits and pencil marks are shared (the layers are over every cell, black ones included, which nobody writes in); selection is each player's own and your partner's cell is framed as in Together; undo leaves what your partner changed since; a hint is the team's. No computer partner, no Show the solution, and a shared solve earns no best, today's time or Random round.

**Twists**, weighed. Kept: Products, a variant puzzle setters use, because it changes the reasoning rather than the dressing: you factor instead of adding, a 5 or a 7 in a product shows itself, a 1 is invisible to it (it can stand anywhere in its run), and 24 in two is 3 8 or 4 6 where 24 as a sum isn't possible at all. Its runs are two to four cells and its clues at most 999, since three digits are what fit a clue's corner, and every rule above (the doubt, the levels, the block's sums, which divide where sums subtract) works for it unchanged. Left out: clues left out on purpose (a competition variant, but Together already plays with clues you can't see, and better, since there someone holds them); Kakuro on triangles or hexagons, three directions a cell (it doesn't fit the square board, and a phone's cells would be too small); digits 1 to 6 or 0 to 9 (a different table to learn, the same reasoning); digits given in the grid (they make it easier and duller, which the levels already do); Sudoku hybrids (clutter: Sudoku's Killer is already the sum-cage puzzle); and runs that may repeat a digit (it takes the puzzle's logic away).

**Controls.** As Sudoku's, on the same pad: tap a cell, then a digit; Notes writes in pencil; a digit tapped with no cell selected lights where it stands; a selected cell lights its two runs. Undo, Redo, Erase, Notes and Hint are under the keys; Fill in every pencil mark and Show the solution are in the menu. The keys don't count digits left, as Sudoku's do, since a Kakuro has no fixed number of each. The line under the grid is three lines high whatever it says (a longer hint scrolls inside it), so nothing changes size mid-game; at 390 by 600 nothing scrolls, though a 12×12's clues are small there (8 pixels), and from 390 by 664 up they read well. On a tall phone the grid is as wide as the screen already, so the room left over is shared above and below it. A sum's clue is set a little larger than the board's default, a product's (up to three digits) at it. On a keyboard: the arrows move, past black cells, a digit's key writes it (with Shift, in pencil), Backspace or Delete erases, N switches Notes, Ctrl+Z undoes and Ctrl+Y redoes. On an upright tablet the phone's layout is drawn larger; held sideways, the grid takes the left with all the height and the clock, the line and a three-by-three keypad stand beside it, the keys at the bottom. The menu has the rules, size and level (picking a level deals a grid of the rules and size chosen), the computer and its pace, Show the ways a run can be, Mark mistakes, and Clear pencil marks as you write.

**The rules sheet** (October 2026, as in every classic): Rules in the header, or The rules in the menu, opens the rules in short plain prose: the aim, how a clue cell reads, Sums and Products and the sizes, a worked example (16 and 17 in two cells, and an across 3 crossing a down 4), how to play here (the taps and keys, Notes, Mark mistakes, the helper, the hint), the levels by the working out they take, the time and today's grid. It scrolls inside itself under its pinned head, opens at its top each time, and the clock stands while it's open.

**Scoring and what's kept.** A grid's time is the time it was on screen: the clock stops while the menu, the rules or the games screen is open, and while the phone is elsewhere, and the grid hides behind a plate meanwhile, so the stopped clock buys no thinking. A hint adds 30 seconds. Bests are by rules, size and level, lowest first, in `kakuro:times` (not `kakuro:best`, which the games screen reads as highest-wins); the games screen's figure is today's time. The run in play is `kakuro:run`: the grid itself (its pattern and solution as strings, the clues worked out again from them, so it opens at once and a partner watching sees it without making it), your entries with their undo, the selection, the Notes switch, the time and hints, and with the computer its pace, the split of the clues and who placed each digit; the settings `kakuro:settings`, the spares `kakuro:spares`, the way a table plays `kakuro:together`, and a match's own run `kakuro:duel` (duel.js keeps it), so a reload anywhere comes back as it was.

`tests/kakuro.mjs` checks the runs and peers of a known grid, its clues as sums and products, the combination tables (3 in two, 23 in three, 45 in nine, 24× in two) and the ways a run can still be, the ways that fit, the doubt's integer logarithm, the exact solver (one solution, two for a swappable rectangle, none, many without clues), sixteen grids of every rules, size and level (one solution each, symmetric, no run of one or too long, no digit twice in a run, no product too long to print, at their level, every step of the human-style solver agreeing with the solution, named, and finishing the grid), two Expert grids that need chains (every chain sound and named), the same grid from the same seed, grading and hints on a known grid, a grid beyond every technique, the computer (every digit right and in order, the paces in order, the same race every time, a partner's next digit right from its own clues), grids through JSON and the database and tampered ones refused, the splits for a pair (across against down, each holding clues the other lacks, neither able to finish alone by the exact solver, the relay finishing with every digit right, every step the solver takes from each player's view halfway through sound, the same split twice) and a table of three, the table through the database's round trip (a digit in for this match only and never in a black cell, who placed it, the last right digit solving it with its time, the team's hints, a share nobody holds, a latecomer's share), and today's grid the same each time it's made, with nothing in the engine but its seed (no `Math.random`, no clock, no logarithm or power a browser may round its own way).

Not done: there's no mistake limit or check-as-you-go mode beyond Mark mistakes (a hint finds a wrong digit, at 30 seconds); computer players don't take seats at a table (above); an Expert or Hard 12×12 can take a phone a few seconds to make the first time (the next is already made); the patterns are generated, so they're tidy rather than a setter's pictures. Whether Medium is the right daily, whether Keen's pace matches a regular, and whether the helper should be free, are for Korbi's thumb to say.
