# Punt
Trivia from the Crates bank and the subject banks, with a stake on every answer. Each question shows a clue, a country or a commodity, and four options, filling the 2×2 grid; sometimes more than one is right ("China" goes with many commodities): then the question says how many, and you must pick all of them to win. You pick and stake a share of your pot (slider or 0/5/10/25/50%/all-in), or pass. Every bet pays 2×: a right pick wins the stake again, a wrong one loses it. You start with 1,000. Runs are a standard length (12 or 15 questions by level), 100 questions, or endless (dealt 20 at a time; end it from the menu). The score that compares runs is the average return a question, compounded: the steady rate that would have turned 1,000 into the pot (a pass is 0%, a bust −100%). The best per level and length is kept.

Runs are dealt in balanced batches so they're comparable: a fixed-length run is one batch, an endless run a batch of 20 at a time, and within a batch the mix of question kinds and clue difficulties is exactly the level's. Runs differ in which questions they get, nothing else.
- Easy: 12 questions, easy clues.
- Medium: 15 questions, easy to hard clues.
- Hard: 15 questions, hard clues, more questions with several right answers.

Questions come in two kinds: a clue with answers to choose from (any answer carrying the clue is right; wrong options come mostly from the same region or sector), and an answer with clues to choose from. A clue that names one of the options is never used. The same seed always deals the same session, so a link (#s=…&d=…) lets two people play the same questions. `tests/punt.mjs` checks every level.

Maths: a fourth entry in the level dropdown deals from `maths-bank.js` instead of the clue bank: 1,108 multiple-choice questions from GCSE to first-year university with a finance tilt, each with a stage (GCSE, IB SL, IB HL, Y1 Uni), a difficulty from 1 (routine GCSE) to 10 (hardest first-year university), four options, one or two right answers and an explanation. Choosing Maths shows two pickers under the header, stages and difficulties, each a sheet of toggles that counts how many questions match; a new choice starts a new run. The bank loads only when Maths is chosen. Questions come in the order the seed shuffles the filtered pool (round again once it runs dry). Links carry the choice (`&st=` stages, `&df=` difficulties), stats and bests keep Maths as its own level, and flags work on maths questions too.

Refining: a fifth entry, on `refining-bank.js`, the same machinery as Maths: 139 questions on how a refinery works (basics, units, specs, renewables, economics) and, in two stages of their own, the chemistry of gasoline and diesel blending: molecular classes and octane, reforming and alkylation reactions, RVP and distillation, EN 228's limits, oxygenates, stability, cetane and cold flow, EN 590, what each component brings and costs, difficulties 1–10, with explanations, and the same two pickers. It pairs with the Refinery game: the game teaches by routing, the questions check what stuck. Quote has a Refining category too (two dozen refinery numbers), and Chart's trade places include refineries and hubs.

Reasoning: critical reasoning on `reasoning-bank.js`, 41 questions in trade and market settings: which statement weakens or strengthens an argument, what it assumes, what would help evaluate it, the flaw by name (false dilemma, argument from ignorance, equivocation, affirming the consequent), what follows and what doesn't, correlation against causation, base rates, selection and Simpson's paradox, regression to the mean, overfitting, sunk costs and expected value. Five stages for the picker.

Words: a word, pick its synonym among four, 236 of them from advanced to obscure with a gloss each, with a trade and law tilt (contango, demurrage, estoppel, subrogation). Cities: every capital (197), which country a notable city is in (about 200 cities: ports, refinery towns, industrial and financial centres), and what a city is known for (115 questions: BASF's Ludwigshafen, the ECB, Cushing, the Canton Fair, Rosario's crushers), stages by region with the weight on Germany, the UK, China, the rest of Europe and Latin America. Flags: 197 countries (the UN's members, Vatican City, Palestine, Kosovo and Taiwan), the flag as its emoji, distractors from the same region at a similar obscurity, in three stages from well known to the Pacific micro-states. Patterns: 61 what-comes-next questions, each standing on its own and most with a drawing: number series and the odd one out as boxes, functions as pairs, letter and word series, and figures (a painted cube cut 3×3×3 in isometric, a die, points on a circle and a grid, a clock, a folded sheet, shape rows, a chessboard). A bank question may carry an `svg`, which Punt shows under the prompt. All four use the Maths machinery and pickers.

The textbook banks, each on the same machinery, with explanations on every question: Economics (`eco-bank.js`, 73: markets and firms with drawn supply-and-demand diagrams, macro and money with the yield curves and the Phillips and Laffer curves, trade and finance, the history of ideas); Physics (`phy-bank.js`, 62: motion and energy, electricity and waves, heat, gases and nuclei, the modern picture, formulas in LaTeX); Chemistry (`chm-bank.js`, 58: atoms and bonds, reactions, organic chemistry and fuels, industry and the environment); Code (`cs-bank.js`, 68: data and algorithms, code to read in Python, JavaScript, SQL, regex and JSON shown as a snippet, object-oriented and functional paradigms, systems and the web); Philosophy (`phil-bank.js`, 60: the ancients, the moderns, ethics and politics, knowledge and mind); Religion (`rel-bank.js`, 59: Islam, Judaism and Christianity, India and East Asia, and what to know at the table and in the calendar). Switzerland (`swiss-bank.js`, 44, written October 2026 for the move to Geneva): Geneva (the Escalade and Mère Royaume's soup, Calvin's Protestant Rome, the Reformation Wall, the Jet d'Eau, Dunant and the Red Cross, international Geneva, CERN, the Geneva Seal, the private banks, the trading houses), the Swiss state (the Federal Council and the magic formula, initiatives and referendums, the double majority, the Sonderbund War, neutrality, the cantons, the UN and the EU) and Swiss life and money (the languages, the Gotthard, Swiss made, chocolate, banking secrecy, the franc shock, Credit Suisse, the militia, fondue); wrong options are real alternatives where they can be (Tell's apple and Winkelried's spears beside Mère Royaume's soup; the Paul Scherrer Institute beside CERN). Art (`art-bank.js`, 797) shows the painting: who painted it, where it hangs, which movement, over 201 works from Giotto's Lamentation to Hockney's A Bigger Splash (the build writes these from `kb/`: The knowledge base, below), and Stories, 68 of the art world's without a picture: what hides in a painting (the skull across The Ambassadors, the king and queen in Las Meninas' mirror, 'Jan van Eyck was here' over the Arnolfini mirror), thefts, forgeries and rescues (the Mona Lisa in 1911, the Just Judges never found, van Meegeren's Vermeers, Beltracchi's titanium white, the salt mine at Altaussee), pigments and restorations (ultramarine, Prussian blue, Klein's blue, the Sistine ceiling, Il Braghettone), and paintings in novels, films, music and on album covers (Ferris Bueller, Titanic, Bean, Doctor Who, The Thomas Crown Affair, Woman in Gold, Pictures at an Exhibition, Viva la Vida). Wine (`wine-bank.js`, 162) is Brut's cards; Brut itself was retired on 3 November 2026, and what it had put on the pile is still asked in Deck as Wine. Movements as ideas (written October 2026): knowing which pictures belong to a movement isn't knowing the movement, so each of the 34 movements has a card in kb/entities.js (where and when, what it rejected, what it sought, how to spot it, what lay behind it, what came next) and the build asks four questions from it: which movement was reacting against this, which was seeking that, how to spot it, what lay behind it. Wrong options are other movements and their own lines, nearest in time, so every wrong answer is true of something; on 'against', never one of the same family (the Renaissances, the abstractions), whose reasons overlap; never a movement the question names, and no question echoes its answer's name. A miss shows the card.

Rich questions: any prompt, option or note may carry maths between `$…$`, rendered to MathML by Temml (`vendor/temml.min.js`, MIT, loaded on first use; `rich.js`); a question may carry a drawn chart (`svg`), a code snippet (`code`, shown in a monospace block) or a picture (`pic`, the English Wikipedia page title of a painting or landmark, whose lead image `pics.js` fetches from Wikipedia at display time and the service worker keeps). Deck shows all three on review. Quote dates the same 201 paintings from their picture, with the painting's story in the note once it's settled, and Chart shows a museum's famous painting on its card. Maths is told from money as Pandoc does: an inline $ opens maths only before a non-space and closes it only after one, never before a digit, and \$ is always a dollar, so 'spent $3 million … needs $2 million' stays two prices (`mathParts` in `rich.js`; `tests/rich.mjs` also checks that no maths span in any bank reads like a sentence).

Pictures (paintings in Punt, Quote and Chart, and Deck when it brings them back) are named by their English Wikipedia page (or, where the page won't do, a file on Commons) and fetched at display time by `pics.js`: Wikipedia's own API gives the page's lead image (`prop=pageimages`, fair-use images included, since most 20th-century paintings are) at a standard thumbnail width, the asked width rounded up to Wikimedia's ladder (120, 250, 330, 500, 960, 1280, 1920 px). Since 2026 Wikimedia refuses thumbnails at any other width, which is why every picture once went blank: the old code built 640-pixel URLs by hand. If a thumbnail is refused anyway, the original file is shown instead; only a page without an image is remembered as such (a failed fetch is tried again next time), and the old cache, which had remembered the refusals, is dropped. The service worker keeps fetched pictures, including thumbnails from thumb.wikimedia.org, for offline play.

Keep pictures offline (Settings on the games screen, October 2026) downloads every picture any game can show, once, at 500 pixels: the 707 in `pics-list.js`, which the build writes from every bank's `pic` (paintings, buildings, places, watches, Who am I's people), about 50 MB, into the cache the service worker keeps shown pictures in, which app updates and the Update button leave alone. It's off by default, since that's 50 MB of a phone for pictures you may never open, and every picture you've seen is kept anyway. They aren't shipped with the app because many are Wikipedia's fair-use copies (most 20th-century paintings), which aren't ours to hand out; this way each phone fetches them from Wikipedia, as it would by showing them. The addresses come fifty to a request, the images four at a time; a download cut short (the connection gone, the app closed) carries on where it stopped the next time the app is open online, turning the switch off stops it, and Settings says how far it has got. Offline, a game asking for a bigger picture (Punt and Quote ask for 960 pixels) gets the kept copy. The switch belongs to the device (`suite:pics`, not synced). `tests/pics.mjs` runs it against a stand-in for Wikipedia and the browser's caches.

A question about "this" picture is no longer asked beside an empty space (October 2026). Quote would ask for the year "this" was painted with no painting, every picture downloaded, for three reasons. Some titles gave Wikipedia's API no picture: Nighthawks named a disambiguation page, Composition VIII, Travelers Among Mountains and Streams and the Unité d'Habitation named pages that don't exist, and a museum or a building whose page puts its logo before its photo showed the logo, since PageImages (the API's choice of a page's picture: the lead section's first image unless it's tiny or wider than 3:1) takes it; Settings counted a page with no image as kept, so it said all were kept with them missing. A weak connection was waited on: Punt and Quote ask for 960 pixels, and a lookup or image that hung left the space empty while the 500-pixel copy sat on the device. And an earlier question's picture, still on its way or failing late, could hide the next one's or put itself back. Now every title was checked against its page (that it exists, that it's the subject, what it leads with), and 44 point elsewhere: the right page (Nighthawks (painting)) or, where the page won't do, a file on Commons named outright (`File:…`, which gives exactly that image: Composition VIII, the Sydney Opera House, the Centre Pompidou, the Reverso, and museums whose logo came first). Water Lilies (its page leads with a 6:1 panorama of the Orangerie's) and Lavender Mist (its page has no image) still have none, and a dozen museums whose page may still lead with a logo (the Louvre Abu Dhabi, Te Papa) are as they were: Chart only shows them, and no photo of them could be confirmed. The copy kept on the device shows once the bigger picture's address hasn't come in 2.5 seconds or its image in 4, and a picture that isn't kept is given up after 10; only the latest picture asked of a box touches it. When a picture can't be shown, its question names the thing in place of "this" ("The year Water Lilies was painted", "Which style is the Seagram Building?"), from the name the build writes beside every picture (`title`: a painting's as titled, a building's with a small "the"), and says "this" again once it shows; not when the name is one of the options, which would give the answer away (`askedWith` in `pics.js`; Punt, Quote and Deck). Settings says how many pages have no picture instead of counting them kept, and the service worker finds a kept picture by its address alone (`ignoreVary`), whatever headers the image's request carries. `tests/pics.mjs` covers the waits, the takeovers and the naming.

Flags: after any question, "Disagree with this answer? Flag it" lets you say what's wrong (a right answer marked wrong, a wrong one marked right, an unclear clue, something else) with an optional note. A flag is kept on the device first, so it works offline, and sent to the database (crates/flags/punt) as soon as there's a connection (`question-flags.js`). Menu → Flagged questions lists this device's flags or everyone's, with a button that copies them as text to hand over for review. The database only accepts flags once the rules in `firebase-rules.json` are published (Firebase console → Realtime Database → Rules → paste the file → Publish); until then they wait on the device, nothing lost.

Stats (Menu → Your stats, also from the end of a run; this run, or all runs at the level on this device) score how well you judge what you know, two ways. Expected outcome, the view for a fixed run: how often your bets were right against how often their prices needed you to be, the edge a bet at equal stakes (what each chip earns, ignoring compounding), and the return on the chips you actually staked. Kelly, the view for the long run (Kelly is only optimal as the number of bets grows without limit): your bets grouped by stake size (a bigger stake says you're surer), how often each group came off, what Kelly would stake at that hit rate and those prices, your stakes as a multiple of Kelly's (over- or under-betting), and the growth a question you got against Kelly on your own hit rates (with hindsight). If you pick an option and then pass, the pick is kept, so the stats also show how your passes would have gone. Every settled question is kept on the device (the latest 5,000).

At a table (October 2026): a pair's match from the partner sheet, or a party of up to six, everyone on their own phone with the same question. Against, the default, each has a pot of their own, 1,000 to start, and stakes on it as alone, all in included; Together, the table shares one pot, each staking up to an equal share of it (half for two, a third for three, a sixth for six), so the stakes can never come to more than the pot. The menu's Together part chooses (Pots: Against or Together) at a table of any size; the other kind of pot is a new run, asked first while one is under way. A question settles once everyone at the table has staked or passed, and until then the Bet button says who it waits for; once everyone else has staked and half a minute has passed since the last of them, it reads Go on without Tom, and anyone may: the slow one's stake is nothing. Nobody away is waited for (their phone asleep, or on another page), nor anyone bust (Against, a pot at nothing: they watch); until the first question settles the table also waits, with the same half minute, for seats not yet sat in, since a party's phones arrive one by one. The reveal shows the right answer, a dot in each player's colour on the option they picked, your own result, then everyone's line, ranked by pot: their pick (ticked or crossed), their stake, what it won or lost and their pot now (Together, ranked by what each has added to the pot over the run). Next waits for everyone at the table the same way. At the end of the set (its length as dealt) come the final standings: Against, who won, what each gained or lost, their bets won and their average a question; Together, the team's pot and what each added. Play again deals a new run for the table, everyone's pot back to 1,000; Back to solo returns to your own run as you left it. The line under the header shows the others in their colours, with their pots and a tick once they've staked (at the reveal, once they've pressed Next). A table's run touches no best, no daily and no solo run; your stats and the review pile still learn from your own bets. The pair's record: Against is head to head, won by the sole first; Together is the team's pot, as before. Opened in a room, the page shows the rete, Joining your partner (at a party, Joining the table), until the shared run is drawn, and starts your own run only if the join doesn't come off (October 2026): it used to draw your own run first, so for the first seconds, ten on a slow connection, each of you was on a question of your own. `punt-table.js` is the table's rules, pure, and `tests/punt.mjs` plays them through the database's round trip: a round settled for three, who's waited for, the half minute, Next, the bust, the reveal's order, the shared pot's share, a seat taken over on another device.

The next question no longer shows the last one's pick and stake (October 2026): the choice was cleared only after the new question had been drawn, so Bet straight away would have staked on an option you never chose.

The page fits the screen: the slip takes the room left and scrolls inside itself when a question is long (its torn edge stays put), and at the reveal the result sits under the slip with its notes scrolling inside, so Next stays in reach while the slip keeps the right answer in sight.

The level dropdown in the header is the suite's own, as in Crates (`dropdown.js`): a button in Punt's logo colours, and the levels on a card with a tick on the current one, scrolling inside itself on a phone. A hidden select still holds the level, so links and rooms work as before.

The slip never scrolls. A painting takes only the room the question and its answers leave (contained, with a shadow on its own edges), down to a small picture on the shortest screens; the stages and difficulties pickers of a bank and the running average are in the menu (Content and About), bank questions carry no stage-and-level marker, and there's no "choose a stake, or pass" line. Pass is as narrow as its word, and the Bet button carries the rest: the stake and what comes back if right ("Bet 100 · pays 200"; "Pays double" before a stake; "Waiting for Sarah" in a duo). Checked over full Art runs at 360×640, 375×600, 390×664 and 390×844: nothing scrolls inside the slip.

Name it turns a clue round: its own hint, with the country or commodity it belongs to and its topics ("Name it · France · Culture & arts: A masked genius haunts the Paris Opéra"), and the clue to name, typed: recall rather than recognition, nothing to choose from, the strictest test of having learned something. Three modes (Menu, Content, Name it; a change deals a new run): Off; Known, the default, where two in every seven questions may be name-it, but only for clues this device has already recognised the other way round (a crate solved and named in Crates, or a right answer you staked on in Punt's clue and answer questions: `known.js` keeps that record, keyed by the knowledge base's entities), clues not named yet coming first, and until there are some those slots are ordinary questions; and All, every question name-it, any clue. Being able to pick something out doesn't mean being able to produce it, so recognising comes first and naming is the test after. A typed answer pays 2× like any other bet: it is only harder. Case, accents, punctuation, a leading article and a part in brackets don't matter, and a slip of a letter in a name of five letters or more (two from nine) still counts, the reveal showing the right spelling (`typing.js`, shared with Deck and Parley, tested in `tests/typing.mjs`). A name you miss goes to the review pile as a name-it card, and Deck asks it the same way: the hint, a box to type in, Check. Naming one right marks it named in the record; missing it unmarks it. In a match the questions are dealt from the starter's record, and a device with name-it off chooses from four of the answer's clues instead of typing.

A subject bank's options (Maths, Art and the rest) come in a new order every run, with "all of the above" and its kind kept last; they used to keep the bank's order, which gave answers away.

A pass is a bet of 0%: Pass waits for an answer like Bet does, the answer is kept, and every count treats it as a bet (the stats' hit rate and Kelly view, with a "passed (0%)" band; the Ledger, where a pass believed no more than an even chance, what a bet at 2× needs, and shows as "Passed (0%)").

A run deals from topics, not a level: Countries and Commodities (Crates' clue bank, each its own topic) beside the subject banks (Maths, Refining, Reasoning, Words, Cities, Flags, Patterns, Wine, Art, Economics, Physics, Chemistry, Code, Philosophy, Religion, Watches, Skiing, China, Britain, Bavaria, the Art market, Etiquette, Literature, Sayings, Law, Mercantilism, Mythology, Architecture, Switzerland). One difficulty range (1–10) applies to every topic, and each subject bank keeps its own stages. Every question is on the same 1–10 scale (a clue question's from how obscure its clue is: 3, 6 or 8; `mixQuestions` in punt-gen.js), and every one pays 2×. Easy, Medium and Hard are gone as choices (their runs still play to the end). A run's link carries its topics (`&t=countries.2,maths.2&df=…`); in the pile a question keeps its own topic's key.

Which topics a run deals is no longer chosen in Punt (2 November 2026; Korbi: "the topics should be set in settings in the main menu, not for each knowledge game independently… remove the more/less thing, on/off is the only needed"). Punt had a control of its own, each topic tapped round out, in and more, with three presets (Balanced, Trader, Culture night); that and the "more" are gone, and the topics are Settings' one set for every game (README, The knowledge base: Topics, set once for every game), each subject on or off. Punt's menu has a line saying where they stand ("Topics: STEM (5 of 23 on)") and a Change topics link, which opens the same sheet over the game as Settings does, under Content; nothing else about topics is in the menu. A change reaches the next run: the subjects are read when a run is dealt, a run already dealt keeps the mix it was dealt (an endless run's later blocks too), and a table deals from the topics of whoever deals it, carried in the table's run as before (Play again keeps the table's).

Each of Punt's topics belongs to a subject (`TOPIC_OF.punt` in `topics.js`). Countries, Flags and the four regional banks (China, Britain, Bavaria, Switzerland) are Geography, and Cities their own; Commodities and Mercantilism are Trade & shipping, Economics Money & economy, Law Policy & law, Refining Energy; Maths, Reasoning and Patterns are Maths & logic, and Physics, Chemistry and Code Science & tech; Words and Sayings are Language & words, Philosophy and Religion Religion & philosophy, Literature and Mythology Literature, music & myth, Etiquette History; Art, the Art market and Architecture are Art & architecture, Wine Food & drink, Watches Craft & style, Skiing Sport. A topic is in when its subject is on and out when it isn't, at the weight Balanced always dealt it at (`topicsOf` in punt-gen.js): every topic is dealt twice as often as one at weight 1, and the four specialist banks (Watches, Skiing, the Art market, Etiquette) are the ones at weight 1. The weights are Punt's own and not a choice. So with every subject on a Topics run is the old Balanced run, seed for seed (`tests/punt-topics.mjs` holds it; it was also checked once against the old engine over 180 runs of 15, 20 and 100 questions), STEM leaves Maths, Refining, Reasoning, Patterns, Physics, Chemistry and Code (no Art), and Culture leaves the arts, Literature, Mythology, Wine, Words, Sayings, Philosophy, Religion and the specialist banks. Seven subjects (Nature, Finance, Companies, Agriculture, Metals & mining, People, Film, TV & games) have no Punt topic; a mix of only those, or of nothing (all off), deals every topic, as the sheet says every game does, and Punt's menu says so.

Your best counts a Topics run when anyone could have played it: Settings' topics are one of its presets (All on, Culture, STEM, Trade, All off) and every difficulty is in, at any length; your own mix of subjects doesn't count, and a run from a link counts only if its mix is what a preset deals (`isPresetMix`). Today's run is as before: every topic, every difficulty, 15 questions, nothing of yours in it. A fault came out in passing: a run's mix never carried the name of the preset it was dealt on, so the presets had never counted, only today's run. The levels stay as they were: Easy, Medium, Hard and each bank (Maths, Wine, Art…) are levels in the header's dropdown whatever is on, and a subject that's off only takes its banks out of a Topics run. The old key `punt:topics` is neither written nor read now (what is in it stays where it is), and a saved run keeps its own mix, whatever name it was dealt under.


Wrong options are real alternatives, never padding. In Philosophy each wrong answer is another thinker's or school's actual idea, stated correctly (Plato's Forms beside Aristotle's form-in-matter, Democritus' atoms and Heraclitus' flux; Zeno's Achilles beside his own arrow, the sorites and the liar), so a question asks which idea is whose; in Reasoning the wrong answers are plausible considerations that don't do the job (most strengthen an argument the question asks you to weaken). In Refining each wrong answer is something real that belongs to another unit, stream or spec: what HVO brings to a diesel pool sits beside FAME's, LCO's and coker gas oil's own profiles; cloud point beside pour point, CFPP and flash point; gasoline's induction period beside existent gum, copper strip and the Rancimat (FAME's test). None is secretly true of what's asked: LCO's density, which really is too high for EN 590, isn't offered against it. Options are of matching length: once the right answer was the longest every time (four times the wrong ones'), which gave it away. `tests/fair-options.mjs` holds those banks (Philosophy, Reasoning, Refining) to it (the right option the longest in at most 40% of questions, on average at most 1.2 times the wrong ones' length); a bank joins it once its options are evened out. Still to do: religion, chemistry, economics, code and physics have the same tell; wine a milder one.

Every bet pays 2× (October 2026), and every question has four options. The odds used to run from 1.1× on the easiest question to 3× on the hardest (4× for a typed answer), set by how hard the house reckoned a question was for a typical player, so a bet weighed the price as well as what you knew; now a bet is only about how sure you are, and the stake is the whole skill: at 2× a bet pays on average once you're right more than half the time, and Kelly's stake (the share of the pot that grows it fastest) is twice your chance less one, 60% sure staking a fifth. A clue question used to have two options when its clue was easy; four for every question makes a pure guess one in four, a known answer the only way to an edge. Two things went with the house's prices: its noise and its scale (`priceBatch`, `scaleOdds`), and the odds row under the slip (hidden since the Bet button carried the odds). And a fault in the deal: a block's kinds and difficulties were dealt as the first of the level's list, so a topic with one question in a block of a mixed run (most of them, with every topic in) always dealt the easiest clue, as a clue question, never a name-it or a harder one; a block now draws them from the whole list. Bests from before keep their scores: the old odds averaged about 2× on a long run, but easy questions paid less than they do now.

No subject question gives its answer away. Every bank was audited (442 of 3,067 questions did: the right option clearly the longest, or the only one with a formula or a number) and those questions rewritten, every option as long, specific and plausible as the answer: often a neighbouring idea (Veblen for Giffen, contango for backwardation, Bellman-Ford for Dijkstra, the expected shortfall for VaR, jihad as a false fifth pillar) or the answer with one thing changed; detail that only the answer carried moved to the explanation. `tests/banks.mjs` holds every bank to it.

Held sideways (README, Bigger screens), the ticket stands on the left with its picture as large as the column lets it be; where you stand (the question, the pot) is beside it at the top, and the stake or the result at the bottom, where a thumb is. The ticket's torn edge is drawn in rem, so its teeth grow with an upright tablet's page.
