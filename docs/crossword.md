# Crossword
A crossword on the knowledge base (October 2026), in Learning: a British blocked grid, some of its entries the knowledge base's clue links and the rest ordinary words, played by a crossword's usual rules (or, chosen in the menu, ordinary words alone, or the knowledge base's alone, a Lexicon grid: Sizes and kinds, and Lexicon, below). Type the answers; the crossings settle what a clue alone can't; the grid is done when every letter is right.

The entries. Every clue link of Crates' bank (about 6,000) makes two. Forward, the clue and its reason name the country or commodity: "Troll · One of the largest offshore gas fields ever found (6)" is NORWAY. Reverse, the reason and the country name the clue, Punt's name-it with the length and the crossings to help: "Norway · One of the largest offshore gas fields ever found (5)" is TROLL. A tag on the clue says what's asked (Country, Commodity, Name it), and a knowledge entry's number in the grid is printed in brass. A name goes in as its letters with its enumeration, the word lengths a printed clue gives (Oil fund 3,4; A-ha 1-2; Lloyd's 6); a name with a number or a sign in it (B40, Tate & Lyle) can't go in, and the UK goes in as Britain, the United States as USA. An entry that would give itself away is never made: a forward clue that says its answer, as a word or a word's start (Brazilian real for Brazil, Swiss franc for Switzerland), or a reverse one whose reason has the answer's own word in it (Brown cheese: "a sweet caramelised whey cheese"). That leaves about 5,900 forward entries and 5,000 reverse (`crossword-kb.js`). A forward clue may fit more than one answer: Vikings fits Sweden as well as Norway, and the knowledge base says so; one crossing letter decides, and the generator makes sure one can.

The grid. British style: a square grid that's the same turned half round, grown from the lattice of black squares at every odd row and column, so about every other letter of an entry is crossed and every entry has at least half its letters crossed (checked, in a setter's word). That's what lets names like SVALBARD in, which an American grid, every letter crossed, can't take. Each level has a size of its own, Easy 11 by 11, Medium and Hard 13 by 13, and a random grid can be any of 9, 11, 13 or 15 squares across (Sizes and kinds, below). The grids are `gridfill.js`'s British ones (Codeword made it), kept to those with no entry longer than 9 letters (9 and 11 by 11), 11 (13 by 13) or 12 (15 by 15: the clue bank's longest words), at most three entries in ten of three letters, and hardly a two-by-two block of black squares: a printed grid's blacks stand apart.

Making a puzzle (`crossword-engine.js`). First the knowledge entries, as many as the size and kind ask (five, six or seven on the levels' own sizes, mixed): in slots that don't cross each other (two rare words sharing a letter is what fills fail on), across and down in turn so both directions get some, each drawn by its weight and kept only if the letters it puts in the slots crossing it leave each of them ten words or more of the clue bank to choose from. Then `gridfill.js` fills the rest with the clue bank's words (`clues.js`: ordinary English words, a plain clue and a harder one each), the commonest first, never one a knowledge clue says. Then the clues: a knowledge entry its link's, an ordinary word one of its two, the harder at the level's share. Then two checks. No clue may say another entry's answer (an ordinary word's other clue is tried). And exactly one fill may fit the clues: a solver (`crossword-solve.js`) counts the fills that give every entry a word its clue allows, up to two; a forward clue allows its answer and the knowledge base's other answers of its length, an ordinary clue every word the bank gives that clue (Limb: arm and leg), a theme's keystone any answer of its kind and length. A puzzle is dealt only when the count is one; where an ordinary clue is shared, its other clue is tried first. A puzzle takes about a fifth of a second here, rarely more than two thirds; it's made in a worker (`crossword-worker.js`), so the page keeps drawing on a slower phone, and everything is seeded and counted in steps, never in time, so a seed makes the same puzzle on every phone. Today's grid takes its ordinary words from the clue bank's two commonest tiers alone (about 10,400 words, every one clued): the bank is still growing, and a phone that updated at noon would otherwise make another grid from the one made that morning. A random grid takes the whole bank.

Difficulty. What makes a crossword hard is how many entries you can't get from their clue, and whether the crossings rescue them, so each puzzle is graded by solving it as a person does. A model solver knows an entry from its clue with a chance by its kind and difficulty: an ordinary word's plain clue 0.9, its harder one 0.6; a country or commodity from one of its clues 0.85, 0.62 or 0.4 by the link's difficulty (there are only so many, and the length narrows them); a clue's own name from its reason 0.55, 0.3 or 0.12 (recall, not recognition). Then it goes round again with the letters the crossings give: an entry nothing else fits is certain once half its letters are in (no other word of the language to SCOWL size 35, or no other country or commodity of its kind), and any other gets likelier with each letter, a pattern jogging the memory, round after round until nothing more comes. Twenty-four such solvers try each puzzle; its grade is the share who finish it unaided and how much of it the average one fills. The levels differ in what goes in (Easy: easy links, a tenth named the other way round, every ordinary word its plain clue; Medium: a quarter the other way, a fifth of the ordinary clues the harder one; Hard: half the other way, the harder links, three ordinary clues in five the harder one) and a puzzle is held to its level's band of both figures. Measured on generated puzzles: a typical solver fills 96% of an Easy grid and finishes almost half of them, 92% of a Medium one, finishing one in eight, and 83% of a Hard one, hardly ever finishing without a letter. Today's grid is Medium.

Sizes and kinds (October 2026; Korbi: "on mobile, can be too small. Allow zoom. Allow user to determine size. Allow user to choose between "classic", "learning" or both. Allow user to select topics that appear when learning is enabled."). A random grid's size is the menu's Size, 9, 11, 13 or 15 squares across, or its level's own until you choose one; its kind is the menu's Clues: Classic, ordinary words alone, a plain crossword with nothing for the review pile; Lexicon, the knowledge base's entries alone (below); Both, the mix there always was, the default. Today's grid stays Medium, 13 by 13 and Both, made exactly as before: a grid asked for with no size or kind is its level's own size and Both, and the engine was checked against the one before the change, today's grid day by day and random grids at every level, puzzle for puzzle. A mixed grid's knowledge entries: 3 at 9 by 9, 5 at 11, 6 at 13, 8 at 15, Hard one more, and a grid may come with two fewer. A theme needs room for its keystone and four starred clues, which no 9 by 9 grid has (the Twist isn't offered there), and takes as many in a Lexicon grid, since one answer has only so many clues that fit. The levels hold at every size and kind. Finishing a grid unaided takes getting every entry, so a level's band of solvers who finish, set at its own size, is raised to the ratio of the entries (a Medium 9 by 9 grid, thirteen entries, must be finished by more solvers than a 13 by 13 one with twenty-eight); how much of a grid the average solver fills doesn't depend on its size, so that band stands. A Classic grid's only way to be harder is its harder clues: Easy gives every word its plain one, Medium a third of them the harder, Hard every word that has one, which puts its figures in its level's bands as a mixed grid's are (Hard, measured: the average solver fills 84%, in a band of 74 to 90). A Lexicon grid is all names to recall, so few solvers finish one unaided at any level, and it's held to a share band of its own (Lexicon, below). Two things keep the new sizes quick. At 15 by 15 gridfill cuts the long runs itself (its makeGrid option `maxLen`, added for this and passed by nothing else, so Codeword's grids are as they were): passing over grids with runs longer than twelve, as the other sizes do, threw away nearly half of them and took three times as long. And a 9 by 9 fill's search has 4,000 steps rather than 24,000: one that works takes a few hundred at most, and one that won't spent two seconds. Generation measured in CPU time here, 40 seeds a size, level and kind (today's kind of grid for scale: 85 to 100 ms on average, at most half a second): 9 by 9, 9 to 27 ms on average whatever the kind, at most a quarter of a second; 11 by 11, 16 to 41, at most 0.38 s; 13 by 13, Classic 55, Both 65 to 85, at most 0.53 s; 15 by 15, Classic 30, Both 72 to 85, at most 0.53 s; a theme 100 to 160, at most 0.57 s. None of the 1,700 failed to make a grid. (Learning, a kind that put in as many knowledge entries as a grid took, apart, among ordinary words, was measured with them; Lexicon took its place, below.)

Lexicon (October 2026; the menu says Almanac since 3 November 2026, the Lexicon's new name, while the saved kind stays `lexicon`; Korbi: "when it's in lexicon only mode, every word should be from the lexicon database, not a mix of conventional crosswords and other"). Learning, the kind it took the place of, put in as many knowledge entries as a grid took, but they never crossed, so most of its grid was still ordinary words. A Lexicon grid is the knowledge base's alone: every entry one of Crates' clue links, either way round as ever, every answer a thing Lexicon knows (a country or a commodity, or a gas field, a festival, a company). Its words are the links' answers, about 3,200 of three to thirteen letters; that few fill a British grid with ease, half of every entry crossed (a grid with every letter crossed would not take them). The fill draws on them in bands by weight, your topics and the links' difficulty at the level, the likeliest band first: Easy's easy links, then its medium, then its hard; Medium's easy and medium together, then the hard; Hard's medium and hard, then the easy. Each word is then given one of its links, by weight, the word with fewest first, none giving another away (no clue may say another entry's answer, so a grid with NORWAY in it has no clue in bold Norway), and as ever exactly one fill fits the clues. Its level is its links: typical solvers fill a median of 63% of an Easy Lexicon grid, 51% of a Medium one and 34% of a Hard one (120 grids each, every size), few finish one unaided, and each is held to a band of its own (56% and up, 44 to 58%, 25 to 42%). Being names, its entries are mostly named from their reason (Name it); a country or commodity comes where the fill puts one. The theme fits as in a mixed grid, its keystone and starred clues placed first and free to cross where their letters agree, every other entry the knowledge base's too and none naming the keystone. A Lexicon grid takes a tenth of a second here on average, at most about half a second; a theme a third, at most about 0.7 (its fills given 4,000 steps, not a mixed grid's 24,000: one of the knowledge base's words that works takes a few dozen, and one that won't spent them all). Today's grid, Both and Classic grids came out exactly as before, today's day by day and the others at every level and size. A saved Learning setting comes back as Lexicon.
Trap, a bar moved (4 November 2026): with the content marked for removal out, the default (Exclude), only about a fifth of the knowledge base's links are dealt (2,000 of 10,900), too few words to fill a 13 by 13 or 15 by 15 grid of them alone, and a 9 or 11 grid not always. A Lexicon grid is still filled from its words first, and as many of its entries are the knowledge base's as the pool gives (a third of them at the least; 11 to 25 of 28 at 13 by 13), but the rest are ordinary words with their clue-bank clues, the harder ones as in Classic, and its level's share band lies between Lexicon's and Both's by how much of it is knowledge (`shareBand`). Only Include (marked content on) deals a Lexicon grid of the knowledge base's alone at every size; `tests/crossword-grids.mjs` checks both. Never turn the marked content back on in the default deal to fill a grid.

What comes (the menu's Content): Clues, Classic, Almanac or Both; Size; the level; and while knowledge is in play (Lexicon or Both), Answers, Mixed, Countries or Commodities, the links of which answers; Knowledge, Both ways (the level's mix), Name the answer (forward only) or Name the clue (reverse only), a Lexicon grid offering the first and last (its countries and commodities alone are too few to fill one); the twist, where it fits; and, as a line and a link, the topics (next paragraph). The choices are your random grids', and they show with Today's picked too: the game opens on today's, and they once showed only once you'd picked Random, so the topics were never seen. Changing any of them picks Random for the next grid; today's stays as it is, the same for everyone, as the line under the choice says (always there, so no choice moves when it would go). A change that shows or hides another choice (Classic hides the knowledge ones, a 9 by 9 grid the twist) draws the menu again with the choice you touched under your finger where it was. A choice too narrow for a grid deals one with every topic and says so.

Topics (2 November 2026; Korbi: "the topics should be set in settings in the main menu, not for each knowledge game independently… remove the more/less thing, on/off is the only needed"). The menu no longer has a topic control of its own. Where Content had one there is a line saying where the suite's one set of topics stands ("Topics: Culture (10 of 23 on)") and a link, Change topics, which opens the same sheet as Settings' Topics part over the game. Each of the 23 subjects there is on or off, and Crossword's topics, Crates' country and commodity ones (each link's aspects), belong to them (`TOPIC_OF.kb` in `topics.js`): a topic is in, at weight 1 as the old Balanced had it, when its subject is on, and out when it isn't (`topicsOf` in `crossword-engine.js`). With every subject on a grid is therefore the one Balanced dealt, seed for seed; a link's weight is still its best topic's, and the engine still takes a weight of 2, so a mix made elsewhere deals. The page reads the subjects when it deals a grid, never once at start-up, so a change reaches the next random grid (and a table's, from whoever deals it) and a grid under way keeps what it was dealt; learning mode's due cards keep to the same mix. Today's grid takes every topic whatever is on. Nothing on is a mix no link fits: the grid is dealt from every topic and says so, as a choice too narrow always did. A random grid counts for your best only on a preset (All on, Culture, STEM, Trade, All off), not a mix of your own, and the menu's line says so when it is. The topics are no longer kept in the game (`crossword:topics` is left where it was and never read).

Playing. Tap a square to write in it, tap it again to turn across or down (a tap acts as the finger lifts: one that moves drags a zoomed grid, and a second finger pinches it; Screens, below); the bar under the grid shows the clue being solved (a tap on it turns too) and its arrows step to the clue before or after. Letters go along the word, and from its last square to the next entry still open, your own first at a table. The on-screen keyboard acts as the finger lands, as a phone's own does; its Clues key opens every clue in a sheet, and on a tall phone, an upright tablet or held sideways the clues are on the screen too, scrolling in their own box, the clue being solved and the one crossing it marked. A hardware keyboard works: letters, Backspace, the arrows (the first press across the word turns), Tab and Enter for the next clue, Space to turn. In the menu, Check (the letter, the word or the grid) marks wrong letters, struck through, and Reveal gives a letter, the word or the grid, a corner on each square given (the grid counts as given up). Check as you go (Settings) marks a wrong letter as it's typed.

Scoring. Time, as a crossword is scored: the clock runs while the grid is on screen and stands still while the menu is open; each letter revealed adds 30 seconds, and each wrong letter a check finds 15. Checking right letters is free, so checking pays when you're unsure, and checking guess after guess costs more than a reveal. The games screen shows today's time (`noteDayTime`, the first solve of today's grid, with its charges); the menu's About has the best time by level, kept for today's grids and for random ones like them: the level's own size, Both, on a preset of the suite's topics, both ways, mixed answers and no twist (a 9 by 9 grid's time is no best for a 13 by 13).

Learning. A knowledge entry you needed a letter for, or left wrong, goes to the review pile as a name-it card that Deck asks back; one you got unaided moves up the pile if it's there, and a forward one is noted as recognised (`known.js`), so Punt's name-it can ask it the other way. In learning mode (the setting every game shares: Revision by default, README, the pile) a random grid puts in what the mode picks from the review pile first, up to half its knowledge entries (half of all of a Lexicon grid's), the most revised first: Crossword's own cards, then (October 2026; Korbi: "in learning mode it should take items from the deck that require revision") every other game's card due in Deck that the clue links can revise, by what it's about (`entriesAbout` in `crossword-kb.js`): a Crates clue as its link, either way round; a place from Chart, a number from Quote, a question from Punt as a link whose answer is the thing it's about, one of them at random. Until then the other games' cards only made entries about the same things a little likelier, so a grid seldom had one; beyond what's due it still leans towards them (`pile.weakElsewhere`). The other games' cards stay as they are: the grid revises what they're about in its own way, and what you need help with goes to the pile as Crossword's own card, as Punt, Chart and Quote do with each other's. What's due keeps to your topics and the grid's choices (its answers, direction and longest entry): a card none of whose topics is in waits, even in a grid made with every topic because your mix was too narrow for one. A mixed grid places what's due as its knowledge entries go, apart, each leaving the slots round it room. A Lexicon grid takes it one at a time, each in a slot of its length that agrees with what's in (two tried), kept where the grid can still be filled round it, as a quick fill shows: with thirty cards due it took five or so at 11 and 13 by 13 and seven at 15 (two at 9 by 9); placed ahead of the fill as a mixed grid's are, two thirds as many went in, and offered to the fill as its likeliest words, a seventh as many. A grid missing some of what's due is taken once a grid's three attempts have tried, since some of it may never fit (a length no slot has): it used to try all forty, and a card due that the grid had no slot for made every grid take seconds. A Classic grid has no room for any of it. When a grid is done it lists every knowledge entry with its link, the ones going back for review marked.

The twist, Theme: Crates in a grid. Its knowledge entries are one hidden answer's clues, starred and given by their reason alone, without the country; one entry, the keystone, is the hidden answer itself, clued as the country or commodity behind the starred clues. A starred clue is hard on its own (recall with nothing to go on), but the first two or three found give the theme away and the rest come easier; no clue in a theme names its answer, and no other answer of its kind fits the keystone's crossings. A theme is held to its level's band of how much a solver fills, set a little lower, and not to how many finish. Weighed and left out: cryptic clues (the clue bank's are plain), barred grids (made for cryptics, and thick bars read poorly on a phone), diagramless and skeleton grids (too fiddly on a phone), American grids with every letter crossed (the knowledge names don't fit dense grids), and fill-ins (no clues, so no knowledge).

The computer plays too (3 November 2026, Korbi: "'against the computer' makes little sense"; "probably drop from all": no rival that races you, on any puzzle or at a table; Settings: Computer, Off or Partner, and its pace, Steady, Quick or Sharp, from the next grid; never on today's). It is the grading solver, its chances raised or lowered by its pace, playing like a person. As a partner it's a split grid played alone, as a pair plays it (below): you hold the Across clues and see its Down ones as its own ("Sharp's clue (5)"); it writes its answers in its colour as it gets them, from the clue or from the letters in, and when it can't get any it waits for your crossings, so your answers are what unlock its last ones, as theirs unlock yours. Only right letters help it, and an answer it's sure of goes in over a wrong letter of yours. A grid played with a partner keeps no best, and only your own clues go to the review pile.

Together: a pair's duo match from the partner sheet, or a Party's table of up to six, each on their own phone (`together.js`; the table is `crossword-table.js`, pure). Two ways, chosen in the menu's Together part for the next grid:
- Split the clues, the default. One grid for the table, its clues dealt among the seats: a pair splits Across and Down, so every answer you can't see crosses only answers you can; three to six take them round the table in clue order, each some of both. Each phone shows only its own clues (a partner's as "Sarah's clue (5)"); anyone writes anywhere, each letter in the colour of whoever wrote it, and each player's word shows on the others' grids as a bar of their colour with their initial where they are. Nobody can finish alone: the clues you can't see are solved by the crossings the others write, and theirs by yours. A seat nobody's in (asleep, gone) has its clues shown to everyone, so nobody holds the table up. Checks and reveals are the team's, charged to its time. A letter is shown at once and sent a moment later, with any typed meanwhile, in one transaction. The pair's record gets the team's time.
- Race. The same grid for everyone, each solving their own on their own phone; the strip shows how far each has got, then their time; the lowest time with its charges wins. Your run is kept on your phone, so a reload picks it up, and a finished run stands. The pair's record is head to head.
The race is one of the table's two ways rather than a head-to-head on `duel.js`: a pair's request opens one table, which is one or the other, and the menu switches it for the next grid, as Manifest and Punt switch theirs. Party lists Crossword for up to six, split: a race is long and quiet for a table of friends.

Screens. The grid's squares are whole pixels, worked out once from the room there is, and keep their size all game: on a 390 by 600 phone a 13 by 13 grid's squares are 22 pixels, 26 at 390 by 664, and a tap is read from where it lands on the grid, so a finger between two squares still picks one. A short phone's strip (where you are, the rival, the table) shows only with something live in it, the grid having its room. A long clue takes smaller type, so the bar never changes size; a word for a moment shows over the bar, never over the grid. Held sideways (README, Bigger screens) the grid stands on the left with all the height, the clue, the clues and the keyboard beside it, the keys at the bottom; an upright tablet draws the phone's layout larger, giving up a little of the grid for six lines of clues. In Codex the grid is framed in a double brass rule; Modern and Kontor keep a plain edge.

Zoom (October 2026). A phone's squares are small (22 to 26 pixels for a 13 by 13 grid), so the grid zooms, inside its own window: the size and frame it has whole, so nothing else on the screen changes size or moves. Two fingers pinch it about their middle, from whole to squares of 60 pixels; one finger drags it; and a tap still lands on the square under it at any zoom. It moves along with the cursor, gliding there: as you type, the square typed in and, where the window has room, its whole word stay in sight, and a jump to the next clue brings that one in. A tap doesn't move it, unless the window's edge cut the square: a tapped square is in sight already, and a grid moving under the finger would send a second tap astray. A finger landing mid-glide stops the grid where it's seen. Where the strip shows (a tall phone, or a phone held sideways), a magnifier at its right goes between the whole grid and a close-up; on a short phone, where the strip is the grid's room, the menu's Zoom does (Whole grid, Close up), and so does a pinch. The magnifier and the menu offer it for squares under 34 pixels (a thumb's square, as Mines found), in plain pixels: a tablet's layout grows (wide.js), but a pixel is about the same size under a finger, and an upright iPad's 48-pixel squares need no zooming. On a computer + and − step it, Ctrl and the wheel (a trackpad's pinch arrives as those) zoom it about the pointer, and the wheel alone moves a zoomed grid. How close you like it and whether you were close up are kept on this device only (`suite:crossword-zoom`: suite: keys don't sync, and a phone's close-up isn't a tablet's), so a new grid or a reload opens as you left it; a partner's watching frame opens whole. Pointer events alone, as Chart's map: `touch-action: none` keeps the browser's own pinch off the grid and Safari's gesture events are refused, so a pinch on the grid never zooms the page. The arithmetic is `crossword-zoom.js`, pure: the view in its window, a pinch about the fingers, the square under a point, following the cursor, and which fingers make a tap, a drag or a pinch (a new touch forgets a finger whose lift the browser lost, Chart's lesson).
