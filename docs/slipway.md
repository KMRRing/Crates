# Slipway
A carving game (rebuilt 19 October 2026; README, Idle): `slipway.html`, `slipway.js` and `slipway.css`, the workshop in `slipway-carve.js` (no page: the tests run it in Node), the bench drawn by `slipway-3d.js`. Korbi: "a carving, sawing, wood-block modification job, so the user does most themselves; realistically there should be a ghost outline; focus on this as a pure carving game at first." So it is the parts of a ship made by hand, one block of timber at a time, and at first nothing else. The carving was right, and Korbi said yes to the next step, the yard (below): the parts fitted together. There is still no voyage.

Each part is a block of timber with the part's ghost seen through it. Before the first cut the ghost can be slid along the block (◀ ▶) or turned end for end, which matters because each block has one or two knots: a knot left in the finished part costs 12 points, and the plane and chisel cut a knot a third as fast. The tools, in a row under the bench:
- **Saw:** draw a line across the bench and the saw cuts straight through the block along it (the cut is the plane through your eye and the line). Of the two pieces, the one with less of the part in it drops away, so a cut is always "take this off"; a cut through the part is allowed, counted, and graded.
- **Plane:** a stroke shaves under a flat sole about seven cells across, facing the way the wood faced where the stroke began (held square to the block when it's within 8° of a face, so a face meant to be flat stays flat). High spots go, hollows are left.
- **Chisel:** a stroke takes a small, deeper bite all along it; tap for a single chip.
- **Drill:** tap and it bores a round hole straight in along your line of sight, right through; the view snaps square to the block's faces as you turn it, so a hole can be set straight.
- **Sand:** smooths where you stroke it, taking the bumps and never adding wood.
- **Turn:** a drag turns the block round; so do two fingers anywhere (a pinch zooms), or a mouse's right button and wheel. The view button steps through corner, side, top and end views.

Within a stroke the plane's blade stays a shaving (0.45 of a cell) under the highest wood its sole has passed over, and the chisel takes only the wood that was open to the air when the stroke began (`exposed` in the engine), so going over the same place again in one stroke takes nothing more and a deeper cut is another stroke, as at a real bench. Without that, overlapping touches dug pits in a single swipe, and a plane stroke begun on a low spot dived along its whole length. Undo takes back the last ten strokes. While you work, the line under the bench says the waste left (as a share of the block while there's lots, then as a share of the part, which is what's graded), how much of the part you've cut into (in oxblood, and the surface is tinted oxblood where you went past the line) and whether a knot is in it, with the stars it would get. Finish grades it: the score is 100 less 1 for each per cent of waste left (measured against the part's own volume, not the block's, so roughing out with the saw earns nothing by itself: a treenail sawn square is a star, planed to an octagon two, rounded three), 3 for each per cent of the part cut away and 12 for each knot; 55 is a star, 75 two, 90 three. A band about a cell wide either side of the line counts as neither, as a pencil line would.

The block is a grid of cells, each holding how much wood is left in it (so a tool can take part of a cell and the surface can lie between cells), drawn as a smooth surface remade after each stroke by surface nets (a vertex where the surface crosses each little cube of cells, joined across the cells' faces). The wood is worked out in the shader from where each point lies in the log: long grain along the block, rings on its ends and wherever a cut opens them, the rings swirling round a knot's dark heart. The ghost is the part's own surface drawn through the block. The bench takes the theme (walnut, oak, baize or linen, by day and night), and it draws only while something moves. A device without WebGL gets a message: carving needs the 3D.

The parts are the cog's, the easiest first: a mast wedge (two saw cuts), a treenail (a wooden peg), a belaying pin, a deck beam with its camber, a block shell with its slot and pin hole, a tiller square at one end and round at the other, a frame (a rib, a curve), a yard tapering both ways, the stem, the rudder with its stock and tiller hole, and the masthead with its halyard hole. Each part opens when the one before has a star. Kept: the block in hand as `slipway:carve` (its cells run-length coded as text, saved after each stroke, so a partner watching sees it), each part's best as `slipway:parts`, and the stars together as `slipway:best`, which the games screen shows. The merge game's best, a score in the tens of thousands, was moved to `slipway:yard-best` so the games screen doesn't show it as stars; the merge game itself is in the history (commit 5dbc6ba).

`tests/slipway.mjs` checks that every part lies inside its block with timber all round it and that a perfect carving of each is three stars; that a block from a seed is the same every time; sliding and turning the part; the saw (taking the side with less of the part, everything past the line and nothing before it, nothing when it misses, a cut through the part counted); the plane (a shaving from the surface only, one shaving a stroke however often it passes, its blade riding on the highest wood it has passed, twelve strokes going deeper, its facing held square); a treenail sawn square, planed to an octagon, planed again and planed past the line graded one, two, three and no stars; the chisel taking less from a knot; the drill boring straight through; sandpaper never adding wood; the ray finding the face it points at; the surface closed and every triangle facing out; the grade (waste, cuts into the part, a knot); and a block packed into text and back. The page and the drawing were clicked through in a headless browser with real pointer events (a saw cut along a drawn line, plane strokes, a drill, undo, finish, a reload) and looked at in software WebGL at phone and iPad sizes in the themes; whether the tools feel right in the hand has not been tried on a phone.

## The yard (the assembly step; 23 October 2026)
Korbi: "Slipway assembly yes." A **Bench | Yard** pair of tabs in the strip switches between carving and fitting; the yard is `slipway-yard.js` (the rules, pure, tested in Node), `slipway-fit.js` (the page half) and `slipway-yard-3d.js` (the drawing). The earlier critique that proposed an assembly step is not to hand, so this design is a fresh one with the same aim: carving should *buy* something, and fitting should be a set of decisions, not a chore after the real game.

Five assemblies, each built from parts you have carved: the deck (a beam and two block shells slung from it), the hull (stem, frame and beam: a scarf and two knees; a scarf is one timber lapped on another end to end, a knee a bracket between frame and beam), the helm (rudder, tiller and stem: the tiller in its mortise, the rudder on its post), the rig (mast, yard, block: a step, a hoist, a sling) and the whole cog, all eight joints at once with the loads set lower. An assembly opens once its own parts and the treenail, wedge and pin have been carved to a star. Every joint is a numbered tag on the model; tap one and fasten it from four kinds: **lash** (rope: free, weak, and one to a joint), **treenail** (a peg), **wedge** and **pin**. A joint holds the sum of its fastenings' worth, but each *kind* of joint values them differently: a mast step wants wedges (3 against a peg's 2), a scarf and a knee want treenails (3), a hoist, where the yard has to slide, wants a lash and a belaying pin, a tiller wants a wedge so it comes out again. Some fastenings aren't allowed on a joint at all. A repeat of one kind on a joint counts less (1, then 0.7, then 0.4), and a joint takes four fastenings at most, so piling the best one on is not the answer either.

The bench feeds the yard in two ways. The fastenings are a stock drawn from the parts you carved: treenails 2 plus 2 a star of the treenail (up to 8), wedges and pins 1 plus a star (up to 4), shared across the whole assembly, so the cog's eight joints have to be spent for, not filled. And a fastening is only as strong as the weaker of the two timbers it joins: a part carved to 90 or more gives full strength, 55 (one star) about three quarters, and a part at 30 or less 0.6. The decision is where to spend what is scarce, joint by joint, and how far to trust the forecast.

The gale is the test. **Send the gale** draws a load for every joint from its *forecast band*, which is shown with the joint (for the mast step in the rig, 3.7 to 6.0), and the ship holds only if every joint's strength is above its load. A lash-only ship fails every time. A gale that takes the ship leaves the fastenings as they are and the next gale is another draw from the same bands, so a failure shows which joints to strengthen, and a second try is not a repeat of the first. Stars are for thrift: 3 for holding with no more than 15% over the least that would have held this gale, plus one fastening; 2 for within 60% over, plus two; 1 for holding at all; and every gale lost on the way costs one star (never below one). The verdict says the least that would have held this particular gale, so the feeling is "I overspent", not "I was unlucky".

The harness (`tests/slipway-yard.mjs`) is there to keep the yard from being busywork. Against a dynamic-programming search for the best spending, it checks that: lashes alone hold nothing in any assembly; two fastenings of the right kind beat two of the wrong kind in at least three assemblies; better carving never hurts; a three-star hold is possible (above 95%) for every assembly when carved to a star level; and that the hard cases are hard (the whole cog with one-star timber holds under 5%, with two stars about 45% at best; the deck with one star, nearly always; the hull with one star about 31% at best, and the obvious plan, filling each joint to the top of its band in turn, 0%); that the best spending beats that obvious plan by 0.2 or more in at least two cases; that the 15 cases (five assemblies at three star levels) have at least ten different best spendings; that the cog needs treenails, wedges and pins all at the top level; and that the hull's best differs between one and three stars. A saved run is checked on load (`sound()`) so a hand-edited or old one is dropped, not trusted.

Kept: `slipway:fit` (the assembly in hand, each assembly's run: what is fastened where, its gale seed, failed tries, and the finished result, so a partner watching sees the same yard), `slipway:fitted` (the stars by assembly) and `slipway:best`, which now counts the parts' stars (up to 33) and the assemblies' (up to 15): 48 in all. The menu in the yard offers the bench, restarting the assembly and the list of assemblies; the verdict dialog waits if the menu is open when the gale ends. The ship is drawn from each part's own outline (not your carving: the yard is about how they fasten, and your carving is already in the strength), with pegs, wedges, pins and rope drawn at each joint as they go on; drag to turn, pinch or wheel to zoom.

Not yet tried: any of this on a real phone (the yard and its touch handling were looked at in software WebGL at 390×600, 664 and 844 and on an iPad both ways; the cog's eight tags are close together on the smallest screen, and turning the model is the way round that), and tuning the loads against how people actually play.
