# Isomer
Jumble's genre, by its rules (October 2026; Jumble is Tribune's name, and an isomer is the same atoms arranged differently, as a jumble is the same letters). Four jumbled words to put right, one letter to each square; some of the squares are circled, and the circled letters, put in order, are the final answer. The newspaper's final is the punchline to a cartoon; ours is a thing from the knowledge base clued by its hand-written hint, asked either way round, the two Korbi likes: the thing from its hint and its country or commodity ("Peru · Food & drink: a grape brandy it claims as its own" → PISCO), or the country or commodity from one of its clues ("A country · History: Pharaohs: ancient god-kings" → EGYPT). The answer's squares show its words' lengths. The English is `isomer-words.js`'s, the finals and themes `isomer-kb.js`'s, the puzzle, the run and the solver `isomer-engine.js`'s, the pair's table `isomer-table.js`'s, all pure; the page is `isomer.js`. The logo is a molecule of four lettered atoms in a zigzag, the third enamelled and ringed in brass, as a circled square is.

Playing. The jumbles stand in rows, each its jumbled letters beside its squares, the answer's squares under a rule. Tap a row and its letters stand in the rack at the bottom, large, under the thumb; tap them in order and each goes to the next empty square. A tap on a placed letter, ⌫ or Clear takes letters back, and Shuffle puts the rack in another order (a solver's own trick: a new arrangement to look at). A full row that's the word turns verdigris, its jumble struck through and its circled letters in cobalt, and the next row comes up; one that isn't shakes and says so, its letters left where they are to be moved. A wrong try costs nothing: every jumble has one word, so any other full row is no word at all. The final's rack is the tray: the circled letters in a jumbled order, each blank until its word is solved, as the newspaper's circled letters are only yours once their word is; it's put together from them the same way. Hint puts the right letter in the first square that lacks it, locked there, for 15 seconds; on the final it can lend a letter whose word isn't solved yet. A hardware keyboard works too: letters, Backspace, Enter (the next row to do) and the arrows.

The words. A jumble's answer is an everyday word from `wordlist.js`, the commonest tiers by level (SCOWL 10, 20 or 35), never a spelling only one side of the Atlantic has, never an inflection of another word (a plural, a past in -ed, an -ing, an -est with its stem a word: a jumble that ends -ING gives itself away), and its letters spell exactly one word in the widest list (SCOWL 70, both spellings), so it has one answer: FLOAT is never dealt (ALOFT), BOTTLE is. That leaves 234, 507 and 1,250 words of five letters by tier, 267, 663 and 1,879 of six, 255, 631 and 2,079 of seven. A jumble keeps no letter in its own square and no pair of the answer's neighbours, and isn't a word itself, so FLOAT would never show as TLOAF (LO and OA show through); a word whose letters can't be jumbled that way (BANANA, KNOCK) isn't dealt.

Making a puzzle, and proving it has one answer. The final is drawn first: by your topics and the level's clue difficulty, the thing or its country or commodity (each answer as likely as the next, however many clues it has). Its letters are shared out among the words, a circle at least to each and at most three to a word of five or six letters (four to one of seven), and a word is found for each share, the shares with the rarest letters first, while the most words are left; its circles go on letters it has. A word two edits from a final, or with four letters in a row in common (FRENCH beside FRANCE), would hint at it and is passed over. The tray's order is jumbled by the same rules, so it never spells a final. Then the solver counts the answers, up to two each: the words each jumble's letters spell; the things in the knowledge base each final's clue could be, spelled with the tray's letters; and the tray holding exactly the finals' letters. One each, or the deal goes again. A hint that says its own thing (SAUNA in "saunas", COPPER in "Copperbelt") isn't a clue, nor one that names its answer. A puzzle is dealt in 5 to 30 ms on a laptop once the solver's model is made; the first deal on a page makes it and lists the words (a fifth of a second on a laptop, perhaps a second on a phone), which the page does in a quiet moment after it opens, and a puzzle kept from before draws without it.

How hard a jumble is: how deep a person's search goes. People unjumble by trying the arrangements that look most like English first (a common start and ending, familiar pairs and triples of letters) and stopping at the first word they know, so the measure is the answer's place in that order: a letter trigram model (each letter's chance after the two before it, the word's start and end counting as letters) trained on the word list's SCOWL 40 and commoner words, every arrangement scored as it's built. BLACK and BEFORE fall into place at once (depth 1); ISLAND waits behind 61 likelier-looking strings, WISDOM behind 70, ETHNIC behind 165; listing every arrangement agrees. Commonness counts too (a rare word is passed by), and so does the jumble's own look: a jumble that looks like a word fixes the reader on a wrong reading, as anagram studies found. The levels give each length a warm word and a deep one. Easy: depths 1 to 2 and 1 to 3, SCOWL 10 only, jumbles that look like nothing, the final's clue of the easiest kind. Medium: warm 1 to 4, deep 4 to 30, SCOWL 20 and commoner, any jumble, clues mostly of the first two kinds. Hard: warm 2 to 12, deep 14 and beyond, SCOWL 35 and commoner, lures (the most word-like jumble that still hides its word: ETHNIC as CHIENT), clues mostly of the harder two. Graded against 40 puzzles a level, the median depth is 1, 4 and 14, and the standard solver (Sharp, below) takes at the median: Classic 0:32, 1:03 and 2:14; Theme 0:58, 1:17 and 1:39; Double 0:36, 1:04 and 2:24; Jumbo 0:50, 1:47 and 4:40. That's the puzzle's par, shown at the end. Whether Medium's deep band is right is for Korbi's thumb.

What the finals are about. The finals and the themes are Crates' clue links (`bank.js`, through `core.js`): 10,027 finals, 5,052 things and 4,975 of a country or commodity from a clue (over 131 answers: UK, UAE, HVO and the other acronyms are never a final themselves), and 116 countries and commodities a theme can be. The menu's Content: Answers (one of: Countries, Commodities or Mixed, each puzzle one or the other) and, for a random puzzle, a line and a link for the topics (below).

Topics (2 November 2026; Korbi: "the topics should be set in settings in the main menu, not for each knowledge game independently… remove the more/less thing, on/off is the only needed"). The menu no longer has a topic control of its own. Where Content had one there is a line saying where the suite's one set of topics stands ("Topics: Culture (10 of 23 on)") and a link, Change topics, which opens the same sheet as Settings' Topics part over the game. Each of the 23 subjects there is on or off, and Isomer's topics, Crates' country and commodity ones (each link's aspects), belong to them (`TOPIC_OF.kb` in `topics.js`): a topic is in, at weight 1 as the old Balanced had it, when its subject is on, and out when it isn't (`topicsOf` in `isomer-engine.js`); a final is as likely as its link's likeliest topic. With every subject on a puzzle is therefore the one Balanced dealt, seed for seed. The page reads the subjects when it deals, never once at start-up, so a change reaches the next puzzle, the pair's and a race's (dealt from the dealer's topics) included, and a puzzle under way keeps what it was dealt. A mix too narrow for a level deals from every topic and says so, which is what nothing on does too. A random puzzle counts for your best only on a preset (All on, Culture, STEM, Trade, All off), not a mix of your own, and the menu's line says so when it is; today's puzzle always takes every topic. The topics are no longer kept in the game (`isomer:mix` is left where it was and never read).

What you learn. A final you needed a hint for (and a theme's clue) goes to the review pile as a name-it card: the hint with its answer and topic, the name to give, and four options, which is how Deck asks a card it has no typed form for (Deck asks Punt's name-it cards typed, and could ask Isomer's the same way). One found without a hint is marked recognised in `known.js`, so Punt's name-it questions may ask it the other way round, and a card of it that was due moves up a pile.

The modes, the genre's real variants that earn their place. Classic, the newspaper's: two words of five letters and two of six, one final. Theme, Jumble's Brainbusters: the four jumbles are a country's or a commodity's own clues, each with its hint in the clue box while you're on it, and the circled letters name what all four point to; the four are chosen, where they can be, so that no other answer in the bank carries all four (Crates' rule for a crate), and a clue whose letters spell another word can't be one (CHOPIN would be PHONIC, KURTA KRAUT). Double: two finals from one tray, the circled letters unmarked, a thing clued by its hint and topic alone and its country or commodity clued by its kind and region, so splitting the tray is a step of its own and each answer helps with the other; a hint shared word for word by other clues isn't used for it ("The second-biggest producer", under India for Sugar and under Sugar for India, would let the two trade places). Jumbo, the Sunday size: six words of five to seven letters and a longer final, names of several words welcome (Rudolf Diesel, The Fuggers). Weighed and left out: a countdown (the race gives the pressure, and the score is a time already); four-letter children's jumbles (Easy is that); Jumble's crossword (another genre, Codeword's and Crossword's); hiding the circles (the final would have nothing to stand on).

Playing together. The pair's match, from the partner sheet, splits the puzzle: each holds two of the jumbles (three in Jumbo), the other's sealed on their screen (hatched squares, the circles showing, the holder's initial), dealt so that each has a word of each length and one of the two easier ones; and each holds half of the final's clue (one the hint, the other what it's under and its topic; for a country or commodity from its clue, one the clue and the other its hint), with a line saying the partner has the other half. Nobody can see the other's letters, so they have to be read out; a jumble solved shows on both phones and drops its circled letters into both trays, and the final needs every circled letter, so neither can finish alone; either can put it in once the two halves of its clue have been said aloud, and a hinted letter of the final shows on both. The team's clock starts when both are there, hints cost the team, and the team's time (with its hints) is the pair's record, lower the better. A race (`duel.js`): the same puzzle for everyone at the table, each on their own phone, the quickest (time and hints) winning; a Party plays it, up to six. The pair can go from one to the other in the menu (Race instead; Two jumbles each instead): the other phone follows, and a reload sits back down at whichever of the two that phone was at.

The solver (3 November 2026, Korbi: "'against the computer' makes little sense"; "probably drop from all": no rival that races you; it only sets par). It unjumbles as a person does: down the arrangements by how English they look, a try at a time, recognising the word with a chance that falls with its rarity (a word missed costs a pass of 40 more tries), the final known from its clue with a chance by its difficulty and otherwise worked out from its letters, the jumbles quickest first and the final once its letters are in. Three paces: Steady, Sharp (the standard solver, par) and Ace; on Classic Medium they take 2:49, 1:03 and 0:26 at the median. Its Sharp pace is the par shown at the end.

On the screen. A phone held upright is the design: from 390×600 up nothing scrolls but the clue box when it's long; the squares and the rack's tiles size themselves to their boxes (container units), so nothing changes size while a puzzle is played, and a long final's letters stand in two lines of the rack from the start. On an upright tablet everything grows; held sideways the stage takes the left, and the clock and the clue stand at the top right, where they're read, the rack and the tools at the bottom, where a thumb is. Office drops the stage's inset hairline and round the corners (in `isomer.css`; `themes.css` is shared). Checked at 390×600, 390×664 and 390×844, 820×1180 and 1180×820, in Codex day and night and in Office; puzzles of every mode played through with taps; a pair on two phones (the stand-in database) through to the record, to a race and back, with a reload mid-race; and a party's race of three.

The rules (October 2026) are a sheet of their own, from the header's Rules or the menu's The rules, as the other games' are: in plain prose, how the circled letters make the final, the two ways a final is asked (PISCO from its clue, EGYPT from one of its own), what a jumble may be and how it's jumbled, the taps and keys, the hint and the score, the four modes and the levels, today's puzzle and the pair's split. Its numbers come from the engine as it opens, so the sheet can't drift from the game, and a solo puzzle and the rival stand still while it's open, as for the menu. Looked at on a phone (390×664, 390×844) and an iPad both ways, in Codex day and night and in Office.

Scoring and today's. The score is the seconds on the clock and 15 for each hinted letter, lower the better; bests by mode and level in `isomer:times` (not `isomer:best`, which the games screen reads as highest-is-best). Today's puzzle is a Classic at Medium, every topic in, Mixed, dealt from the suite's day, the same for everyone; it counts the moment its last answer goes in, its score the games screen's figure (a time: `noteDayTime`), and the tile opens it while it's still to play, asking before it leaves a puzzle you've started.

What's kept, under `isomer:`: `run` (your puzzle and how it stands: every square, the hints, the rack's order, the clock; saved at every move and every five seconds, so a partner watching sees it), `opts` (mode, level, answers), `times`, `today`, `pair` (your own squares at the pair's table), `where` (which of the pair's two tables you were at) and `race:duel` (a race's run, apart from yours). A watching frame runs no clock and writes nothing. The clock stands still while the menu or the games screen is open and while the page is out of sight; at a table it doesn't.

`tests/isomer.mjs` (about two seconds) checks the depth against listing every arrangement, the solver's counts, inflections, the word pools, that jumbles hide their words and lures look more like English than plain ones; names as letters and the finals and themes; 168 puzzles over every mode and level (each with exactly one answer, the tray exactly the finals' letters, the circles within bounds, every jumble hiding its word, none echoing a final, every word in its level's band, the same seed the same puzzle, a theme a crate by Crates' rule, Double's clues apart, Jumbo's size); the levels apart in what they take the solver, and the depths; the solver's paces apart; the rules (placing, a wrong row, taking back, a solve, the tray opening, hints locked, over a wrong letter and lent, the final, the score, Double's shared tray); a run kept through JSON; the pair's split through the database's round trip (each holds two jumbles of each length and they hold all four, the clue's halves make the whole and neither alone does, neither can solve the other's jumbles or open the whole tray alone, the hints, the end, the record, a stale tap, a partner's solve shown); the suite's topics (every subject on the old Balanced puzzle seed for seed in every mode, Energy alone and STEM alone dealing only their finals, nothing on dealt from every topic and saying so, an old mix with a weight of 2 still dealing); and today's puzzle the same for a day and another the next. In a browser (November 2026): the menu's topics line and its Change topics sheet at 390 by 664 and 1180 by 820 in Codex day and night and in Office, and a change in the sheet reaching the next puzzle: STEM's finals alone, a mix of your own unranked, nothing on a puzzle of every topic with the toast saying so.
