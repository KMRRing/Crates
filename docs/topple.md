# Topple
The fourth idle game (October 2026; README, Idle): `topple.html`, `topple.js` and `topple.css`, its rules in `topple-engine.js`. A table of sixty-one hexagons, four out from the middle every way, in birch inlaid in a mahogany frame ("The table in 3D", below). Three pieces of standing dominoes wait under it, each piece in one of three enamels, each colour with pips of its own (one, two, three a half) so they're told apart without their colour. The pieces are chains of one to five dominoes: lines, bends of sixty degrees, arcs, waves and forks of three (no three of a piece all touching, none touching more than three others, so never a solid blob), each in every way it lies, laid as dealt (a piece isn't turned, though each of its dominoes can be once it stands, below), a straight line twice as often as another shape of its size. When all three are down three more come. Drag one onto the table and, while it's held where it fits, the dominoes of its colour it would join are ringed, with what their best push would bring down ("9 together: all fall in 7, +630", or "9 together: 7 fall at most, +490").

How dominoes fall (October 2026; Korbi: "Dominos should all either point at one adjacent hex, or at a line between two adjacent hexes"). A standing domino is a slab, the same front and back, so what it has is an axis: the way its broad faces look, a way and its opposite. Round a hexagon there are twelve ways a domino can point, every thirty degrees: at the six neighbouring hexagons, and at the six lines between two neighbours that touch (the side those two share). So there are six axes, thirty degrees apart: three hexagon axes, along the rows of the table, and three line axes, the slab turned thirty degrees off the rows. A domino falls along its axis. Falling at a hexagon, it knocks only the domino standing there, and only if that one's broad face is mostly toward it: its axis square to the fall or thirty degrees off; sixty or ninety degrees off, mostly edge-on, it stands. Falling at a line, it knocks the dominoes on both hexagons either side of it, each unless it stands exactly edge-on to the fall (square, thirty or sixty degrees off, they fall). It's the way of the fall that counts there, not the way to each hexagon, since the falling domino comes down on both along it. Each one knocked falls along its own axis, the way nearer the fall that knocked it, and knocks in turn by the same two rules. Only dominoes of the pushed one's colour are ever knocked: each piece is one enamel, and the whole design rests on it. So a straight line on its own axis falls end to end; a bend of sixty degrees falls through, from either end, when the domino at the bend faces the line between the way the fall comes in and the way the chain goes on (it's thirty degrees off the fall coming in, so it's knocked, and falling at a line it takes the next); a turn of 120° is never taken (the domino past such a turn always touches the one before it, and falls only where that one's own fall comes down on its hexagon, at the line between the two: a fork's other half); and a fork, a domino facing a line, is the only way one domino brings down two. A push scores ten times the dominoes felled times the steps they take to fall (the serpent rule): a run of ten pushed from its end falls in ten steps, 1,000; the same run pushed from its fifth domino fells six, 360; a fork that brings down two lines of three falls seven in four steps, 280, where a line of four is 160.

A knock is a push: press a domino and slide it along its axis, either way, the way it should fall. Once the finger has gone four tenths of a hexagon along the axis from where it pressed (and a few pixels at least), that way is chosen: what a push that way would fell is ringed, what of its colour would stay standing is marked, and the steps and the points are written over it all ("10 in 7 steps: +700; 2 stand"); let go to push. Slide back to where you pressed, or across the axis, or more than two and a half hexagons away, and letting go does nothing. Held still a moment, the domino shows its two ways as arrows, each with what it would score (none towards the table's edge, where only the domino itself would go). A tap is never a push (it chooses the domino, below), and nor is a slide with no knock due: that shows what the push would do and when the knock comes, and does nothing.

Turning a domino (October 2026; Korbi: "The user should however be able to rotate individual dominos after they are placed"). A tap on a domino chooses it, and a brass handle shows out along its axis, on the side towards you. Drag the handle round and the domino turns to point where the finger is, snapping to the twelve ways (an axis is two of them, so six positions); a tap on the chosen domino turns it a step, thirty degrees clockwise; a tap on the bare table lets it go, and a tap on another domino chooses that one instead. While one is chosen or turning, what the best push on the dominoes joined to it would bring down is ringed and written over them, as for a piece held over the table. Turning is free, as often as you like, at any time a fall isn't playing, a knock due or not, so a chain can be set up and then pushed. It's the pattern of Heliograph's mirrors, with one change: a drag on the chosen domino's body turns it too, to face the way the finger is dragged, rather than pushing it. The handle stands only three quarters of a hexagon out, and a finger that missed it by a little would otherwise slide the domino along its axis and spend the knock; so to push the chosen domino, let it go first (or choose another), which a slide along its axis with the knock due says. The turns are kept with the game, and the domino chosen as `topple:sel`, so a partner watching sees the handle.

How a piece faces as dealt (`axesOfCoords`): so that it falls whole from an end where its shape allows. A line faces along itself; a domino at a bend of sixty degrees faces the line between its two neighbours' ways, so the piece falls from either end; a fork's middle faces so a fall goes through its two longest arms, since no fall can take all three; one beside a single other faces it; a lone domino takes a hexagon axis by where it stands, so a table of them isn't all one way. Of the 141 pieces, the 121 that aren't forks fall whole from either end; the twenty forks (the fork of four in its two ways, the forks of five in eighteen) fall all but their short arm, one domino, from the end of either long arm. A piece faces only for its own shape, so two pieces laid side by side seldom fall into each other by themselves: joining them is what turning is for.

The knock is the table's to time, not yours: when a hand's last piece is down and a knock has come due, the next hand, dealt, waits dimmed until it's made (the strip says "Knock now", and before then "Knock after this hand" or "Knock in 2 hands"). A knock can't be kept for later and none comes in between, so a chain has to be worth pushing when the knock comes, and a hand that fits nowhere ends the game, since the knock only comes once a hand is finished. The knocks come every hand for the first sixteen hands, then fewer: each finished hand adds the knock rate to a credit and a knock falls due when the credit reaches one, the rate falling in a straight line from one a hand at the sixteenth hand to fifty-five in a hundred at the thirty-sixth, and staying there (`PACE`). And a bell hangs on one hexagon of the table: a push whose fall passes it (the domino standing on its hexagon among those that fall) scores 500 more, a flat sum (`BELL`; it was double until 24 October 2026, see "The bell is a flat sum" below), and the bell moves to an empty hexagon at random. The bell is what takes play round the table: a corner is a good place to start a line, and the bell is rarely there. The score and your best are in the strip, the best kept as `topple:best` the moment it's beaten, so the games screen has it; the game in play is kept (`topple:run`) and opens again where it was. A first game's first pieces say what to do, written on a plaque on the empty table. Held sideways the table is on the left with all the height, the score, the knock and the three pieces beside it, low where a thumb is.

**Skipping a knock** (23 October 2026). Korbi: "user can skip a knock, which will give +100% score on the next topple. This is additive with the +100% from the bell so 3x total if both are done". When a knock is due the strip shows a small Skip beside "Knock now": nothing falls, the knock is gone (it isn't owed back, and the pace goes on as it was), and the next knock you make scores a hundred per cent more, shown by a "+100%" badge in the knock's place until it is spent. Bell and skip add, not multiply, so a push over the bell after a skip is three times, not four (`knockOn`: points times one, plus one for the bell, plus one for a skip). The gilded hexagons of the levels (below) still double whatever the sum is, so bell, skip and gilding together are six times; I kept that as it was rather than fold it into the sum, which would have changed every level's balance the bot tuned the goals on. The bonus does not stack: while one stands, the next knock that falls due must be made (so skipping goes with knocking, every other knock at most), and Skip is greyed where no piece in hand would fit without the knock, since skipping there would end the game. The compass numbers on a pressed domino, the best push shown for a chosen one, and the points rising from a fall all count the bonus (a fall's rising text shows the base and the times). It is part of the game as saved (`boost`, 1 or 0; a game saved before it has none, which reads as 0), so a game opened again keeps it. The harness's players never skip, so none of the goals or paces were retuned: how much a skip is worth to a good player (it gives up a knock, the one move that clears room, for a point of leverage on the next) has not been measured, only that it is playable. `tests/topple.mjs` checks the skip (nothing falls, the knock leaves the table's time, the boost stands, the next knock is 320 for 160 and spends it, with the bell 160 times two plus 500, not offered where it shouldn't be and refused there, the best push counting it).

Zoom (October 2026; Korbi: "Allow user to zoom in a bit on dominos"). Pinch the table to bring it closer, up to 1.8 times (`ZOOM`: enough to see a domino's facing plainly on a phone, and no more), and back out to the whole table; a double tap on the bare table zooms in there, or back out if it's zoomed; the wheel does it on a desktop. While it's zoomed, a drag on the bare table moves it, only as far as its edge (the camera's window stays inside the whole table's, so the table can't leave the view), and the little tilt is for the whole table only. A second finger turns any touch into a pinch, so a pinch that starts on a domino never pushes or turns it, and the finger left down after one does nothing until it lifts. Everything drawn over the table (the arrows, the rings, the scores, the handle) goes through the same projection, so it follows, clipped to the table's box. The zoom is a view, not the game: it isn't saved, and the table's box keeps its size (only what's drawn in it is larger). The flat table zooms the same way.

The strength of a push, retired (October 2026). A knock was weak, medium or strong: how many of the three hexagons ahead of a falling domino it could take, dealt from the seed and shown a knock ahead. Under the six axes each domino's facing decides what a fall takes, so the strength had nothing left to decide and went, with `force`, `FORCE_ODDS`, and the strip's "Medium push now" and its bars; a fall moves and sounds as a medium one did. The draw from the seed that dealt each knock's strength is still made, and passed over, so a game saved before then deals on exactly as it would have: a game of version 4 opens as version 5, each of its three axes the same hexagon axis of six (doubled) and the strength dropped, nothing thrown away. Its bends were faced on the old rule, along a line, so they stop a fall now until they're turned. Bests are kept.

The table in 3D (October 2026): the dominoes are lacquered slabs with a bevel, a bar across the middle and their pips (`topple-3d.js`, on the vendored three.js that Orrery also uses), standing on a hexagonal table of birch in a mahogany frame with a brass stud at each corner. There are no image files: the wood is drawn by a shader (birch in three tones, each hexagon its own piece with its grain turned a third of a turn from its neighbours', the mahogany's grain running along each side and meeting at the corners in a mitre), so it's there offline and the same at any size. Each theme has its light by day and by night (a cool light with a warm fill at night), and the table follows the theme as it changes. A phone upright is taller than wide, so the table sits with a corner at the top and the bottom (a quarter turn about the vertical) when that fills the room better, and the camera height, between 50 and 66 degrees, is picked the same way, so the table is as large as the screen allows at any size; a piece in the tray is drawn turned the same way, so it looks as it will on the table. Press and drag on the bare table, while it's shown whole, to tilt it a little (about seven degrees, as a watch dial's does); let go and it eases back in a third of a second; a tap leaves the table level and lets a chosen domino go. A domino turned eases round to its new axis in a seventh of a second. The arrows, the rings, the written scores, the handle and the hint are an SVG drawn over the canvas through the table's own projection, so they follow the tilt and the zoom. Where there's no WebGL the page draws the same game flat (`topple-flat.js`, the same calls, no tilt and a simpler fall), so it still plays.

The fall (`fall` in `topple-3d.js`): each domino of a push hinges over its foot along its own axis, the way the engine says it falls, and falls under gravity, bounces a little where it lands, lies for a moment and sinks into the table; a few specks fly where it lands, the table shakes a hair at each landing and the bell, if it was rung, drops, rings and hops to its next hexagon. A chain keeps its rhythm: a domino falls when the one before it has reached it, and the two of a fork go at once. The sounds (`topple-sound.js`) are made as they play, in Web Audio, with no files: a short bright clack, wood on wood, where one domino meets the next (slightly different in pitch for each, panned across the table with it), a duller thud where one lands flat, a tap when a piece is laid, a heavier knock for the push itself and the bell's inharmonic ring. A fall hands the whole run over with each sound's time, and they're scheduled on the audio clock, so a long chain clacks evenly whatever the frames do; sounds within 25 milliseconds of each other become one louder sound, so a fork or a cone of dominoes isn't a racket. The menu's Sound switch (`topple:sound`) turns them off, and the table, its sound and the clock of its fall all stand still while the menu is open. Run by hand in a headless browser, a fall of 13 dominoes scheduled 22 sounds (the tests don't cover the page); how they sound has not been heard.

Why it's a hexagonal table (October 2026, on the rules before the six axes, when a fall went straight on or sixty degrees either side and every domino faced along a row): the square version let the pieces and the fall turn any way, and the strategy was thin: laying every piece in the corner, with a habit of putting each beside its own colour, was most of the game. Tried in a harness (`moves` lists every place each piece in hand fits, `chains` what's standing and its best push), the same simple players on the old table lasted: the corner habit about 43 pieces, the corner habit by colour 62, a careful player (keeping what each piece joins in lines that fall whole, with room for the rest of the hand) 87, and one that also brings its lines to the bell 85, the careful player's move being the corner-by-colour habit's 62 times in a hundred and the bell player's 54. A straight port to hexagons was worse. What changed it was three rules together: a fall that goes only forward, so that what stands beside a line is never taken and the shape of a group matters as much as its size; a push whose direction is yours to choose, so the same chain is worth different amounts from each of its ends; and pieces that are chains, so that what's laid is always something that can fall. Then the same players on this table: the corner habit over after about 35 pieces, by colour 52, the careful player about 117 (41 to 176) and the bell player about 106, ringing the bell about eighteen times a game, and the careful player's move the corner-by-colour habit's only 45 times in a hundred (the bell player's 38): there was something to decide at nearly every piece. The board's shape made no difference to length (an eight by eight grid with every other row shifted half a hexagon played the same), so it's the hexagon, which has no ragged edge. A fall that fanned, straight on and both sixty degrees at once, was easier and so more trivial (the careful player's move was the corner habit's about three in four). The six axes keep all three: a fall still only goes forward (within thirty degrees, or sixty from a domino facing a line), the push is still yours to aim (now by the facing as much as the sense), and the pieces are still chains.

Why the knocks taper (October 2026): a knock every hand until the twelfth and then every second was a cliff: careful play was over in about 58 pieces, since the table filled all at once, where a straight-line taper lasted 117. A knock every hand throughout never ended a careful game (26 of 30 were still going at 1,500 pieces); every second from the first hand ended it in about 26. Bests from the square table keep their scores; a game saved on the square table, or before the facing of each domino (version 3), can't be played on this one and is replaced by a new game, and one of version 4 is carried over (above). Every random thing (the pieces dealt and their colours, where the bell goes) comes from the game's seed and the number of the draw, so a game opened again goes on as it would have. A piece is named by its hexagons from its first ("0,0;1,0;2,0", a line of three going east), so a saved hand doesn't depend on the order the pieces are made in.

Why these numbers (October 2026, the six axes). Played out by simple players in the harness (`node tests/topple.mjs measure`: 80 games each, on seeds 100 to 179, every knock the push that scores most), those who turn nothing are over fast: the corner habit after about 23 pieces (14 to 32), by colour 23, a random player 19. A piece faces only for its own shape, so without turning a knock takes about one piece's worth (five dominoes) and the table fills within eight hands, knock every hand or not. The careful player turns every group of a colour into the longest fall it can find after each piece (a long path through the group with no turn sharper than sixty degrees, its bends faced through line-facing dominoes, then each domino tried at every axis while that makes the best push score more, which is how it finds forks), and lays where the rest of its hand can still all be laid, then where what the piece joins falls furthest for its size. On the pace before (a knock every hand to the sixteenth, then fewer to seven in ten at the fifty-sixth) it lasted about 170 pieces (53 to 350), against about 100 before turning, so `PACE` now tapers in twenty hands to fifty-five in a hundred, which brings it back to about 112 pieces (53 to 161), felling about eleven a knock and scoring about 43,500; 31 of its knocks in a hundred fork. The bell player, which also lays over the bell, lasts about 98 (52 to 143), rings the bell about fifteen times a game and scores about 48,000; the same careful player turning nothing is over in about 20. Every game ended. Turning is where the game is now: it is worth some ninety pieces. Those are a simulation, not a play-test: the harness plays well enough to tune the pace by, and a person who looks ahead will do better, a person new to turning worse.

**Levels** (23 October 2026; Korbi: "12 levels for now", features brought in gradually, "the first level should be exactly the way topple currently is", "no pressure for the user to advance to a later level at any point", "maybe even current should be 'classic' then 'levels' should be a separate thing"; rebuilt on 24 October as fourteen tables, below). The menu's Mode chooses between two things that don't touch each other. **Classic** is the game as it has always been (three colours, a plain table), with its own game (`topple:run`) and best (`topple:best`), and is the one whose best the games screen shows (a level's score is another measure and is never entered there). **Levels** (`topple-levels.js`, pure, with its tests in `tests/topple-levels.mjs` and `tests/topple-rules.mjs`) is a ladder of fourteen tables, each beaten at a goal score. Switching mode, or level, leaves a game where it was and takes it up again as it was left (`topple:lruns`); *Replay* deals the level again. Nothing in it presses: beating a level opens the next and says so once, a level once open stays open and can be played in any order, the game goes on past the goal for stars if you like, and the end of a game says how far short of its goal it came and offers Replay and Levels, never "Next". The menu lists the levels as tables in miniature (`topple-map.js`), with number, name and stars.

**The rebuild** (24 October 2026; Korbi, after the idea of five base colours and mixes: "build them all, make sure the goals are very difficult, equivalent of 20k in map 1, just make sure it's theoretically possible and there are no soft locks"). The first eighteen levels (tall dominoes, the pearl, the stair, the marbles) were replaced by fourteen, each bringing in one thing and keeping what the ones before taught. What they are built from:
- **Five colours** (yellow, red, blue, black, white) and **mixes**: orange (yellow and red), green (yellow and blue), purple (red and blue) and grey (black and white). A mix is knocked by either parent and knocks either parent, and not another mix nor a colour that isn't a parent (`kin`, `MIXES`). The **rainbow** (id 9, once the pearl) is knocked by every colour and knocks every colour. Classic keeps its three colours and its own enamels.
- **Heavy dominoes** fall only once two dominoes have fallen into them in the one knock (of any colour; a ball counts once). A **gate** names two colours (yellow and green) and falls only once a domino of each has reached it, exactly those colours (a yellow is no green, though they are kin), or a rainbow for either. Both score double when they fall, as a gilded hexagon does (not twice over). Pushed by hand a heavy domino or a gate always falls, so neither can ever lock a table. Some tables start with them standing (`start`).
- **Springs**: a domino on one reaches the hexagon past the next as well (over water or a gap, not past a stone). **Marsh**: a domino there falls and knocks nothing. **Hedges** stand on an edge between two hexagons and stop a fall across it. **Portals and stairs** (`links`): a domino that falls on one end also brings down the domino on the other, whichever way it faces. **Cups**: a ball that stops in one scores a flat 1,500, and another is put on the table.
- **Floors**: a table may have two, of sixty-one hexagons each (a cell is `floor * 61 + hexagon`; `rules.nb` says each hexagon's neighbours, so a hexagon at the rim of an upper floor can have the floor below as its neighbour: a **drop**, which a fall goes down but never up). A piece is laid on the floor asked for (`lay(game, slot, q, r, floor)`, `moves` lists them with `floor`); every domino on every floor falls in the same knock. The drawing puts the floors apart (`layerGap`).
**Crossings double** (24 October 2026; Korbi: "make sure that the features of each level are actually impactful and need to be engaged with"): a knock in which a fall crossed something doubles, once, as a gilded hexagon does and not on top of one: a spring's throw over a hexagon, a portal or a stair, a step from one floor to another (a drop, or the mouth of a cave). `crossing` in `topple-engine.js`; the knock's result says `crossed`, and the page says "crossing" beside "gilded". (An earlier version of this README said a drop scored like any other fall. It did, and it showed: see "Engaged with, or only there" below.)

A level is beaten when a knock takes the score to its goal; the score then also counts toward two stars, at 1.6 and at 2.6 times the goal (`STARS`), shown on a meter under the score. Stars are kept for the best game and never go back (`stars` in a level's game, counted at each knock).

The fourteen tables, with their goals:
1. **The parlour** (14,000): five colours on a plain table. Korbi's number was 20,000 for a table of four colours; the fifth colour cuts what a game scores by about two fifths, so the goal came down with it (below).
2. **Cairns** (13,000): orange, and stones where nothing is laid and a fall stops.
3. **The river** (18,000): green, and a river with two fords.
4. **Gilded cross** (37,000): heavy dominoes (three stand at the start), and four gilded hexagons.
5. **Ziggurat** (30,000): purple, and terraces to a gilded summit: a fall goes level or down, never up.
6. **Archipelago** (17,000): islands in a water ring, springs to jump it, and gates (two stand at the start). Pieces of three at most.
7. **Waterfall** (23,500): grey, and two floors: a plateau of rock with a stream across it over a lower plain. A fall that runs off any edge of the plateau lands on the plain, and two stairs in the west join the floors; either crossing doubles the knock.
8. **Two courts** (52,000): a wall of water, bridges (a straight line of two or three that may lie on water) and portals.
9. **Fenland** (18,000): a river with no ford, marsh, bridges and twins (a pair of two colours: in one chain with one half, in another with the other).
10. **Hedgerows** (36,000): hedges, and the rainbow to join what they divide.
11. **The cup** (30,000): a hillside that falls away to the west, three balls and three cups worth 1,500 each.
12. **Two floors** (59,500): a gallery above the ground floor, stairs at five places, and every edge of the gallery a drop; crossings double.
13. **The spiral** (16,500): a road round a mountain, a step up every fifth of a turn, a sheer drop at three o'clock, and a cave on a second floor under the last quarter, open at the foot of the cliff; two gates stand at the start.
14. **Grand table** (24,500): everything at once, nine colours, a moat with two fords, four springs, portals, three cups, balls, and a floor above joined by stairs.

The deal keeps to pieces of at most `maxSize` (5, or 4 or 3 on the cramped tables), and **every piece it can deal must fit the empty table**: on a table cut in two a line of five fits nowhere, and a piece that fits nowhere stays in the hand for ever, and a hand is dealt only when its last piece is down, so it would end the game from the first hand (Two courts and Fenland did, until their pieces were cut to four; `tests/topple-levels.mjs` checks every table). The knocks come at the game's own pace on most tables and a gentler one (`GENTLE`, a knock nearly every hand once forty hands are played) on those so cut up they have little room to spare.

**Scenes** (24 October 2026; Korbi: "a table, and just tables in general, that are a bit more aesthetic. A stair leading up a cliff with a far drop down or something like that"). A level has a `scene`, which is only how its table looks (the rules never read it, `topple-levels.js`): `birch` is Classic's and the first and second levels' (exactly as they have always been: the 3D table was compared pixel for pixel before and after), and four more: **marble** (Ziggurat, Two courts, Two floors), black marble with gold veins in each hexagon and a white marble frame, on a plinth; **jetty** (The river, Grand table), weathered planks with pale painted lines, in a tarred timber frame on piles standing in a sea that fades out round it; **cliff** (Waterfall, The cup, The spiral), sandstone flagstones with moss at their joints in a frame of bare rock, on a pillar of rock that narrows into mist; **garden** (Gilded cross, Archipelago, Fenland, Hedgerows), gravel raked in rings round each hexagon, a black lacquer frame, a moss-stone base and a stone lantern at each corner, lit by night. The pit of a hexagon of no table is a real hole cut through the top and the apron, with walls of rock running down into mist. Stones, water and steps follow the scene's colours too.

They are all drawn by the same shader as the birch (`uStyle`, `topple-3d.js`: the wood's hexagon cells, bands and frame, with the field and the frame made of something else), so there are no image files and they are there offline; each scene has a palette for day and one for night, and the theme still decides the light and the enamels, so a scene follows Codex, Modern and Kontor. The flat drawing (`topple-flat.js`) gets the same scenes in a colour each (`data-scene` on its svg, `--tp-…` in `topple.css`), and its drop is mist-coloured. Checked at 390×600, 390×664, 390×844 and iPad 820×1180 and 1180×820, with the page never scrolling, in Codex, Modern and Kontor by day and by night.

**The bell is a flat sum** (24 October 2026). Korbi: "The bell should give a flat bonus, +500, not a multiplier bonus. That encourages chasing it around with small chains while building the big one that then gets a multiplier from skipping a round." A push that passes the bell now adds 500 to its points (`BELL` in `topple-engine.js`; `knockOn` returns it as `bell`), and nothing multiplies it: a skipped knock (+100%) and a gilded hexagon (double) multiply the fall's points alone, and the 500 comes after. So a line of four is 160 with the bell 660 (it was 320), after a skip 160 times two plus 500, 820 (it was 480), and with gilding as well 160 times four plus 500, 1,140 (it was 960). Against doubling, the flat 500 is worth more for any knock of under 500 points (a line of seven falling in seven steps is 490, so nearly every small chain) and less for a bigger one; that is the point: the bell now rewards a small knock made to ring it, while the large chain waits a round to be skipped to. The popup shows the multiplied points and, after them, `+500 bell`. The old "3x with both" of the skip paragraph above no longer holds. **Goals:** they were set (about 0.6 of the careful bot's median) with the bell doubling. Played again with the flat bell (`measure careful games=10`), the careful bot's median is still above every level's goal bar two (Quarry 8,850 against 9,300 and Crossroads 5,080 against 5,300, the two that were closest), and it still meets each goal in 4 to 10 games of ten (Classic 10 of 10, median 39,800), so the goals were not moved. The bots rarely chase the bell, so this measures the bell's change to a plain game, not what chasing it adds.

**The 3D table fell back to flat** (24 October 2026; Korbi: "Topple is no longer the sleek 3d game it was before? Please revert it"). The balls' seams were made with `TorusGeometry`, which the vendored three.js doesn't have (the bundle holds only the classes the pages use), so making the table threw, `topple.js` took it for "no WebGL" and drew the flat table, on every level and in Classic, from the balls' commit until this fix. Nothing in the rules' tests could show it. The seams are a small ring built in `topple-3d.js` now (`ring`), and `tests/topple.mjs` reads the file for every `new THREE.X` and fails if the vendored three.js has no such class. Classic itself was not changed: it is the table it was, checked against the commit before the levels.

**Balls** (24 October 2026; Korbi: "maybe can also add balls as feature that roll until they hit something or lose momentum"), on The cup (three balls) and the Grand table (two). A ball sits on a hexagon like a bell does but is in the way: nothing is laid on it (`BALL`, -2 in the table's cells, so every test for a domino, which is a colour of 0 or more, is false for it, and a saved game keeps them as it keeps the rest). A fall that reaches one sets it rolling. A domino knocks a ball when it falls onto its hexagon, level or down, and the ball goes the way it was hit. It starts with 2 steps of momentum and 1 more for each two steps the fall had already come, 6 at most; a flat hexagon costs 1, a step down nothing (so a ball runs on down a slope), a step up 3 (it must have them). It stops when its momentum is spent, at a stone, at a domino it can't knock (standing edge-on to its roll, on a higher step, or one already down in this fall); at a domino it can knock it stops and knocks it, a step after it stopped, and the fall goes on from that domino; at another ball it stops and hands on what it has left less one. Into water, a pit, or off the table it is lost; into a **cup** it scores 300 flat (on top of the bell, which neither it nor anything multiplies). Either way the level puts a new ball on an empty floor hexagon (not the bell's) by a draw from the game's own seed, so the number on the table is always the level's and two players on one seed see the same table. Two balls never come to rest on one hexagon: the later stops a hexagon short and knocks nothing. The rules are `rollOf` (one ball's roll) and `cascade` (a whole knock: dominoes and balls together, in time order); `chain` is `cascade`'s dominoes alone.

How a ball scores: it is one more unit in the knock, and each hexagon it rolls is a step. A knock's points are 10 times its units times its steps, as before, so a push that fells three dominoes, rolls a ball three hexagons and then knocks two more is 6 units in 8 steps, 480. The bell rings if a ball passes over its hexagon or stops on it, and a gilded hexagon doubles if a ball passes over it, as if a domino had fallen there; a skipped knock's bonus doubles the fall's points and the bell adds its flat 500 after (gilded doubling the fall's points, as it does for dominoes). So a skip still pays the same on a table with balls, and a ball is a cheap way to reach a far bell. The knock's preview says "N and M balls in S steps", the ring is drawn round each ball that would roll as round each domino that would fall, and the bots are taught by the same engine call (they choose each knock by `knockOn`, so they use balls when it pays, and a table with balls is laid like any other). What they don't do is lay for a ball: a ball is mostly a hazard to them (it sits on a hexagon until a fall happens to reach it). That showed when the Grand table first had four balls on a river with no ford: the careful player's games ended after about 20 pieces, so it has two balls and two fords.

Drawn, a ball is a lacquered oxblood sphere (coral at night) with two brass seams, so that it is seen to roll; it slows to a stop over the route the engine gave, runs a step up or down smoothly, hops back a little if it strikes a domino, is lost over the brink in free fall or sinks in water, and the bell it passes is knocked off its hexagon as it goes (`topple-3d.js`, `fall`). The flat drawing rolls a circle along the same hexagons. Oxblood because the table's blacks, birches and blues all hide a navy or a dark green ball.

**Why the goals are what they are.** Korbi: "make sure the goals are very difficult, equivalent of 20k in map 1, just make sure it's theoretically possible". Table 1 with five colours is already a hard table for the harness's careful player (`tests/topple-players.js`: turning each group into its longest fall and laying where the rest of the hand fits; the bell player is the same, also laying to bring its falls over the bell): over 16 games each it scores a median of 13,200 and 16,400, and reaches 20,000 in 4 games of 32, about one in eight. That is what 20,000 means here, so every other goal is set to the same rarity: 1.35 times the mean of the careful and bell players' medians on the table (16 games each, seeds 100 to 115), rounded to the hundred or half hundred. The goals therefore track what the table gives and not a number: 13,000 on Cairns, 16,500 and 17,000 on the small Spiral and Archipelago, 52,000 and 59,500 on the two big, open tables. They were set twice: after the features were made to count (below), the doubling of crossings and the cups' 1,500 moved the scores of six tables, and those goals were set again from new runs. The two stars, at 1.6 and 2.6 times, are for those who go on.

**Table 1, five colours, and how hard each goal is.** Korbi, 24 October 2026: "Is level 1 maybe a bit too difficult now? Topple - increased from 4 to 5 colours. For each check how tough it is to actually get the score." He was right. The same bots on the parlour with four colours score a median of 24,300 (careful) and 20,300 (bell), and reach 20,000 in 33 to 66 games in 100; with five they score 13,400 and reach it in 3 to 25 (64 games each: the rate moves a lot with the seeds, the median little). Twenty thousand was set against four colours, so on five it asked about twice what he meant. The goal is now 14,000, which the two bots reach in about 4 games in 10, among the easiest of the fourteen, as an opening table should be. The other tables were set by the 1.35 rule and stand: over 32 games each (seeds 300 to 331, 400 pieces) the careful and the bell bot reach their goals in 6 to 56 games in 100, and score a median of 52 to 139% of it, the corner habit (laying each piece at the nearest corner and pushing the longest chain) a tenth of it or less everywhere. A bot that searches every fall is a better player than most people, so a goal the bots reach one game in four is out of reach most games for a person: that is what "very difficult" comes to here, and Korbi's own play is the measure that matters.

**Engaged with, or only there.** Korbi, 24 October 2026: "make sure that the features of each level are actually impactful and need to be engaged with in order to get the nerfed score rather than just being there". A feature is tested two ways (`tests/topple-features.mjs`, `tagsOf` in `tests/topple-players.js` reads what a knock used off the dominoes it brought down). What a level *adds to use* (a mixed colour, heavy dominoes, gates, springs, portals and stairs, drops, balls, cups, the rainbow, gilding, a step down): the careful player plays the table twice, as it is and refusing every push that uses the thing (it takes the best push that doesn't). What *stands in the way* (stones, water, marsh, hedges): the same player on the table with them taken off. Over ten games each, the median score of a player who refuses the feature, against the table as it is:
- mixed colours: about a quarter of it or less on every table that has them (Cairns 2,930 against 11,250; Ziggurat 1,080 against 28,970); gilding on Gilded cross 5,630 against 24,730, heavy dominoes 3,720; the rainbow on Hedgerows 4,810 against 24,520, Two floors 6,740 against 39,120.
- balls on The cup 5,420 against 23,380, and its cups 15,090; a step down on Ziggurat and The cup under a fifth, on the Spiral a quarter (the tables are built on it).
- gates on the Spiral 5,760 against 11,410, Archipelago 6,890 against 10,430; springs on Archipelago 7,170 against 10,430.
- portals on Two courts 21,390 against 36,320; stairs on Two floors 23,560 against 39,120; on Waterfall drops 12,110 and stairs 14,240 against 17,120; on Two floors drops 29,360.
- on the Grand table, any one of mixes, gates, springs, stairs, balls, cups, water or the rainbow refused leaves it at four fifths of the score at best (springs 12,860 against 21,450, the rainbow 8,320, the cups 16,150).
- in the way: Cairns' stones off, 27,620 against 11,250; the river off, 47,130 against 13,310; Archipelago's water ring off, 51,290 against 10,430; Two courts' wall 99,900 against 36,320; Fenland's river and marsh off, 146,290 against 14,650 (the game then never ends); Hedgerows' hedges off 44,030 against 24,520; the Grand table's moat and stones 40,810 against 21,450.
What the first measurement found, and what was done. Waterfall's drop and stair were used in 0 to 2 per cent of the scoring and the stair's absence changed nothing; Two floors' stair 1 per cent; The cup's cups slightly *helped* a player who refused them; Archipelago's springs and the Spiral's gates cost a refuser a sixth; the Grand table's springs and cup nothing. The cause was the same in each: a crossing or a cup is a way to join two chains, and a player who doesn't need to simply doesn't. So crossings now double a knock and a cup is worth 1,500 (the bell's 500 is the other flat sum), Waterfall's plateau drops on every edge and has two stairs, not four (with four a stair stood in for a drop and refusing a drop cost nothing), Two floors drops on every edge of the gallery and has five stairs, the Spiral deals a gate in one piece in eight, the Grand table has three cups and four springs, and the river's own `water` is read as an obstacle (nothing is laid or crosses it) and not as something to use. The test that stays in the suite replays the game on record that reaches each goal (`tests/topple-reached.js`, chosen so that this holds): in it every feature its level adds is used, refusing any one of them leaves it short of the goal, and four games on each table with its stones, water, marsh or hedges off score a fifth more at least. That is one game, the same every time; the ten-game medians above are `node tests/topple-features.mjs measure games=10 levels=…`, and are what to look at when a table changes. A feature that gets used but can be done without is still a feature a person may skip: the bots only show what a good player *can* find.

**Possible, and no soft locks** are tests, not hopes. `tests/topple-levels.mjs` plays a game of the careful bot on record for every table (`REACHED`: a player and a seed, found by playing seeds 100 upward) and asserts that its score reaches the goal, so no goal is out of reach (if the deal ever changes, find them again). It checks that laying everything in a corner and turning nothing reaches no goal. And it checks, on every table and 150 deals: every piece the level can deal fits its empty table (the dead-piece lock above); the first hand has a move; every heavy domino or gate that starts standing has two neighbours of floor to be hit from, stands where the level says, falls when pushed, and a gate wants colours the level deals; every colour of the level is dealt, and heavy dominoes are. A knock that is due always has something to push (a domino stands, or the table has been refilled), and any standing domino may be pushed on its own, so a heavy domino or a gate can never hold a table. What cannot be promised is only the end of a game, a hand with nowhere to go, which is how a game is meant to end. The first measurements found three tables wrong and they were retuned before the goals were set: Archipelago and the Grand table ended after 17 and 37 pieces (pieces cut to three), Waterfall, Hedgerows and Two floors never ended, since a set of colours that are mostly each other's mixes makes every domino knock its neighbours (Waterfall now plays the five colours and grey, Hedgerows five and purple, Two floors five and green), and Two courts at the game's gentle pace ran 197 pieces.

**The table.** Drawn in 3D (`topple-3d.js`) from the level's rules: stones are boulders, one rough shape to each, standing on the table; water is a pool set into it, with a ripple that moves only while something else does; a step up is a hexagon standing proud with a dark line round it; gilded is a brass line round the hexagon; no table is a hole; a spring is a coil, a cup a ring set into the table, marsh a green patch, a hedge a low wall on the edge, a portal or a stair a ring on each end. The nine colours and the rainbow are nine enamels (the rainbow banded) with pips of their own, so they tell apart without colour; a heavy domino is a thicker slab with a brass band, a gate is halved in its two colours. Classic keeps its three enamels (`rules.palette`). Where there is no WebGL the flat drawing (`topple-flat.js`) does the same from above. A level's first table shows a plaque with its number, name and goal, until the first piece is laid. What is drawn over the table (the rings, the tags, the handle) follows the steps (`baseOf`, `topOf` ask the view where a hexagon's floor and a domino's top are).

**Floors** (24 October 2026). A table of two floors draws them apart (`layerGap` in the rules, `floorYs` in `topple-slab.js`): the floor in play is drawn whole and the others faint (the ground fainter still over a cave), and a column of buttons at the table's side, the highest at the top and each with the number of dominoes standing on it, chooses the floor (kept as `topple:floor`; high in the side column when the screen is held sideways, so a tablet's keyboard never covers it). A press, a drag and a laid piece mean the floor in play (`lay(…, floor)`), and a fall that goes down a floor is drawn going down. A level's miniature in the menu (`topple-map.js`) shows its floors side by side.

**Feature help** (24 October 2026).
