# Polder
The third idle game (October 2026; README, Idle): `polder.html`, `polder.js` and `polder.css`, its rules in `polder-engine.js`. A sea eight squares by eight, the coast along its foot, open sea beyond the top and both sides. Three dyke pieces wait under it: lines, corners, cups, Ts and steps of one to five squares, none of them solid, each laid as it's dealt and never turned. Drag one onto the water and, while it's held where it fits, the water it would cut off from the open sea is drawn as farmland and the dykes that would go with it are ringed; let go and it's laid, let go anywhere else and it goes back. When all three are down, three more come. Water flows across a square's sides, not its corners, so dykes touching only at a corner hold it back (a knot of earth is drawn there to show it). Water the open sea can't reach is a polder: it drains, and the dykes touching it, corners included, go with it. A polder scores ten times the square of what drains in one go (one square 10, four 160, twelve 1,440), so one big polder is worth far more than several small ones, but a big one holds much of the sea up while it's built. The coast is the cheap side to build against, where a cup of three dykes closes a square; the sides and the top are open sea, so a polder there needs dykes all round.

Why silt: with dykes alone the sea never fills (a player taking the piece that drains most and leaves least built laid well over a thousand pieces, most often 3,000, without running out of room), so every few pieces the sea leaves silt on a square of water. Nothing can be built on silt and it holds no water back; only a polder takes it away, counting its squares like water. It settles along the coast first, then beside silt already there, a sandbank growing up from the shore, so it can be planned for: wall the bank in and drain it (silt settling anywhere would only be in the way). It comes every five pieces at first, every four after 60, every three after 150 and every two after 250 (the strip's pips count down to it). A piece that fits nowhere is dimmed, and when none of those left in hand fits, the game is over. The score and your best are in the strip, the best kept as `polder:best` the moment it's beaten, so the games screen has it; the game in play is kept (`polder:run`) and opens again where it was. A first game's first pieces say what to do, written on the empty sea. Held sideways the sea is on the left with all the height, the score and the three pieces beside it, low where a thumb is.

Why these numbers: played out by simple players (`moves` in the engine lists every place each piece in hand fits), one laying anywhere has no room left after about 25 pieces; one taking the piece that drains most and leaves least built and silted lasts about 100; one looking two pieces ahead in its hand lasts about 155 and scores about 9,000, its biggest polder about 13 squares. Silt every three pieces from the start ended even the look-ahead player in under 80. Every random thing (the pieces dealt, where the silt settles) comes from the game's seed and the number of the draw, so a game opened again goes on as it would have.

`tests/polder.mjs` checks the deal from a seed, the pieces (each joined along its sides, none solid, measured from its top left), where a piece fits (on water, not on a dyke or silt or off the sea), polders (four dykes touching only at their corners holding a square; the coast holding one, the sides and the top not; silt holding nothing; a polder from side to side taking its silt; the dykes at its corners going with it), the points, a piece laid (the polder drained and cleared, the hand, the game it was laid from left as it was, three more after the third, a piece where it doesn't fit refused), the silt (along the coast, beside silt, more often as the game goes on), the end when no piece fits, a game saved and opened again, and whole games: laying anywhere out of room in under 60 pieces, laying with thought lasting half as long again or more.
