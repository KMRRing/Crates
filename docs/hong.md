# Mahjong
Hong Kong mahjong (香港麻雀) at a table of four: you and three computer players (Mei, Wing and Lok), or, together, you and your partner across the table with two of them between you. The rules are those played in Hong Kong homes and clubs (新派清章): 144 tiles with the flowers or 136 without, three fan to win (or one, or none, when a chicken hand will do), ten fan the most a hand pays, half-spicy payouts (the base doubles up to four fan, then every two), the discarder paying the whole of a win off his discard (4 × base) and a self-draw paid by all three (2 × base each), the head bump (of two who could win on one discard, the first after the discarder takes it), the pass block (pass a win you could take and you can't win off a discard until you next draw, 糊一唔糊二), liability (let go the tile that makes someone's fourth set, or third dragon set, and you pay all of their self-drawn win, 包), robbing a kong, play to the last tile, and the dealer keeping the deal on a win or a drawn hand; no seven pairs. A game is the East round, two rounds or all four. The Rules button has the fan table. The rules are fixed to fan, flowers, three fan and the East round (below for the no-fan game that was once in the menu).

`hong-engine.js` is the referee: the wall, the deal and its flowers, the turn, the claims and kongs, the fan of a winning hand (every split of it tried), who pays what, and the next hand. `tests/hong.mjs` checks the scoring of the spec's example hands and the limits, the deal, the head bump, the pass block, robbing, liability and seven flowers, plays 400 random hands through (each ends, every tile accounted for, every payment balanced), then the computer players: their reading of a hand, the calls and the defence a good player makes, whole hands among themselves, and against plain efficiency players.

**How far the rules are checked** (25 October 2026; Korbi: "Is there a way you can verify that the Hong Kong mahjong engine, with flowers, min 3 fan, classic rules, is the optimal one? I really want to make sure it's 100% accurate"). Hong Kong mahjong has no single rulebook (a table agrees its minimum, its cap, its flowers and its limit hands), so "right" can only mean right against a written table, and "optimal" can't be shown at all for the play (a game of hidden tiles has no proof of best); what can be done is to hold the referee to a published table with a second program that shares none of its code. `tests/hong-oracle.mjs` does it: the fan table of the English Wikipedia's *Hong Kong mahjong scoring rules* (every pattern, the limit hands, the flowers, the self-draw, kong, last-tile and robbing items, the half-spicy base points) is applied by a separate scorer to 30,000 random winning hands built to land on every pattern, melds open and concealed, kongs, flowers, self-draws and robbed kongs, and the fan must match the engine's on every one; it also checks, against a plain search, that the engine calls the same sets of tiles complete. Run first on 150,000 hands it found one difference, which was a real mistake: the engine took 門前清 and 坎坎糊 away from a hand holding a concealed kong, and the table says only an *open* meld does either (a concealed kong is a concealed set). That is fixed, in the referee and in the computer players' reckoning. The base points and the split of a payment (a discard pays 4 times the base, a self-draw 2 times from each) agree with the table's 1 and 1.5 times, as scaled. The rest is this table's choice where the sources part, so a different table would score it differently: a chicken hand has no pair rule (平糊 is any four chows), 坎坎糊 is eight fan in place of all pungs, the other fan stacking to the cap (four guides say eight, the English Wikipedia ten as a limit; Korbi chose eight, 25 October 2026), 門前清 is lost only to an open meld (the English Wikipedia and Mahjong Logbook: the Chinese Wikipedia and one HK01 article also lose it to a flower, so it is the guides' split; kept as the likelier, on Korbi's "whatever is more likely official"), a concealed kong keeps it (the same answer), a last tile is one fan (HK01's "two" counts the self-draw in; Sing Tao, the English Wikipedia and Mahjong Logbook give one), no seven pairs, and no dealer double or 七搶一 (robbing the eighth flower). Beyond the 30,000 random hands it checks every one of the 118,800 ways to hold fourteen tiles of one suit (13,259 complete), that a hand scores the same with its suits turned round and with the seats moved on one, and that the computer players' count of tiles from ready (`shanten`) is the plain distance to a complete hand on 200 random hands. Six guides were read for the table (the English and Chinese Wikipedia, two HK01 articles, Sing Tao, Mahjong Logbook, Yoho): they agree with the engine on every fan value they name but the contested ones above. What the second scorer does not reach is the turn: the claims, the head bump, the pass block, liability, the flower deal and the kong payments are covered by the example hands in `tests/hong.mjs` and by 400 whole random hands (every tile accounted for, every payment balanced), not by a second implementation.

The computer players (`hong-bot.js`, thinking in `hong-worker.js` off the page's thread) see what a seat sees: their own tiles, what's been shown, the discards and the wall's count. A hand is read against the routes that reach the minimum (any hand, a half or full flush in each suit, all pungs, thirteen orphans), each with its shanten (how many tiles from ready) and its live tiles (unseen copies that would bring it closer). A route's chance of winning is worked out go-round by go-round: the stages still to make, each with the chance its live tiles come by draw or by call, then, once ready, the chance a winning tile comes by self-draw or off a discard, against the draws left and the chance someone else wins first. Its value comes from the chances of each fan it could show (flowers still to come, a value honour pair punged, the concealed hand while it lasts), so a plain hand that needs a self-draw to reach three fan is valued as one. A hand one tile from ready is worked out exactly: every tile that would make it ready, the best ready hand it makes, its waits scored by the real rules; and a hand that keeps a second route open counts a little of that too. Danger: each opponent's chance of being ready (by turn and calls, as measured in the bots' own play), whether they can win off a discard at all (from the fan they can still reach with what they've shown), the suit their calls point to, the tiles passed since they drew (safe, by the pass block), what dealing in costs beyond what their self-draw would cost anyway, and liability. A discard is chosen by value less danger, a call when the hand after it beats letting the tile go, a kong when it costs nothing. The first version played out a few hundred random continuations of each discard: four or five tiles from ready that was mostly noise, and among themselves its bots drew seven hands in ten. Calibrated against its own play, this one wins two thirds of its hands among itself, and over the same deals comes out about 22 points a hand ahead of three plain efficiency players, which win often but small and never defend. `tools/hong-arena.mjs` plays every deal four times with the seats rotated, so only the play shows, and pits versions and tunings of the bot against each other (`medium` for the two strengths).

The computer players play at two strengths, Medium and Hard: the hidden ramp (`ramp.js`, kept as `hong:ramp`) steps them up after a game in which you finish first and down after one you don't; the strength you had chosen in the menu is where it begins. Hard is the reckoning above with two additions, and over 2,000 deals it plays level with the players before them (within a point a seat-hand, the arena's precision). It passes up a discard win when waiting for the self-draw is worth much more: the common case is four concealed pungs waiting on two pairs, where the discard completes one pung as an exposed one and the hand is six fan (128 points), while the self-draw is the limit; waiting there is worth about twice as much, and the coach says so ("Pass the win…: waiting for it is worth about 272 points"). It counts the extra fan of the hand's last discard (海底) when judging who could win off it. Its discards vary at random only among those within a quarter of a point of the best. Medium takes every win, weighs danger at six tenths of Hard's weight and lets its discards wander among those within five points of the best, the better likelier; over the same deals Hard comes out about five points a seat-hand ahead of it (2,000 deals, every seat rotated). Four more expert reads were tried in October 2026 and left out, since none came out ahead of the reckoning above over 2,000 deals: an honour's danger by whether it's worth fan to that opponent (a dragon or the round's wind to anyone, his own wind to him), a flush read from the suit missing from someone's discards, a safe tile kept back in a slow hand, and a heavier weight on liability. The danger measure, calibrated on the bots' own play, already holds most of what they add, and each made the bots fold winnable hands more often than it saved. At a table together the strength is the room's (the device that deals brings its setting, either of you can change it), so both devices move the computer players alike; the coach is always Hard's, and gives the same answer each time it's asked.

The coach (Menu → Coach, one on/off switch since 4 November 2026; a saved Hints, Check or Always is on as it was) speaks in one of four ways. Always, as at first: at each of your decisions, the computer's move in your seat and why ("Discard 7 dots: keeps a half flush in bamboo, one from ready, 12 tiles would help; 34% to win"), its tile marked in your hand with a jade bar. Check (October 2026; for its first day it was called If you differ): nothing until you've moved, and then only where it would have moved otherwise: what it would have done, how many points worse it values yours, and the two reasons that cost most, further from ready, fewer wins, a smaller hand and more danger before the rest ("Your 5 dots: about 2.3 points worse than the coach's 9 bamboo. A step further from ready (two from ready); wins less often (21% against 34%)."), its label in oxblood so it isn't taken for advice; Why? sets its move and yours side by side among the others. The points are what the hand is worth after the move on average (each win it could make, by what it pays and how likely it comes, less the danger of the discard), the measure the coach decides by. The word stays until a turn of yours it agrees with (a claim you pass on as it would doesn't end it, so it isn't gone before you've read it), and a move it values as well as its own, within a tenth of a point, counts as agreeing. Always says the same after a move it advised against, while the others play. Hints: only when you ask, Hint asking it about one decision (Check has Hint too). Off: nothing, and no Hint; until Guan Dan's coach came in, Off was what Hints is now, and a coach left there is now Hints. A coach that speaks unasked keeps its room under your tiles (four lines on a phone, a longer reason scrolling inside it), so the table no longer changes size each time it speaks and falls quiet. Every choice it weighs carries its value, so a kong it passed up, a win on your own draw let go for a discard (the win's points against the hand the discard leaves) and a discard win taken or waited on are measured too. Why? opens the choices in words, the coach's first, each with what speaks for it (+) and against it (−): for a discard, the way it keeps and how far along, a step nearer or further from ready than the coach's, more or fewer tiles to help, how often it wins (or, winning as often, how much less its wins would pay), a bigger or smaller hand, the danger and from whom, the hand's other way kept or closed, and how many points worse on balance (or "as good: either would do"); for a claim, letting it go against each call (the value honour's fan, the discard after it, nearer ready or not, a route given up for a smaller hand, the concealed fan lost and a set shown to read); for a discard win, taking it against waiting for the self-draw. Under them, the discards it weighed as numbers (the route each keeps and the next-best one, how far from ready, the tiles that would help, the chance to win, the danger) and what it makes of each opponent. At the end of each hand, unless it's Off, Your play: how often you and the coach agreed, and the three decisions where it would have gone another way that cost most, in the same words, with its own move's reason.

No fan (once Menu → Scoring, October 2026; no longer offered, and a game begun under it finishes under it) is the game as it's played at tables that don't count fan (雞糊, chicken hands). Any complete hand wins, and every win pays alike: self-drawn, 2 from each of the others; off a discard, 2 from the one who let it go and 1 from each of the others (robbing a kong counts as off a discard, its owner paying the 2). The dealer (東) pays and receives double: the dealer's own win takes 4 from each by self-draw, else 4 from the discarder and 2 from each of the others, and a dealer who pays pays twice. The dealer keeps the deal on a win or a drawn hand, as in the fan game. A kong takes 1 from each of the others as it's made (one added to a pung once no one has robbed it; a robbed one goes back to a pung and takes nothing), and if the first four discards are one tile, the dealer's and then the same from each of the others in turn with nothing called between (跟莊, following the dealer), the dealer pays each of the others 1; neither is doubled. No flowers, no minimum, no liability (包); the head bump, the pass block and robbing a kong stand. Your seat's line says "No fan: any hand wins", and the result says who paid what and why, kongs and the follow included. The best game is kept apart (`hong:bestNoFan`, in the menu's line), since its wins pay a few points where the fan game's pay tens; the games screen shows the fan game's. Together, the table plays the scoring of the device that deals it; the puzzle rush keeps the fan game's rules, its puzzles having been found there. `tests/hong.mjs` checks the payments (each way a win comes, the dealer winning, paying and looking on), the kongs (concealed, off a discard, added, robbed), the follow paid and broken, the deal kept, and that a no-fan deal has no flowers whatever the fan game's settings.

The computer players and the coach play it as it pays. Their routes are the plain hand and the thirteen orphans (a flush or all pungs is only a slower way to the same win), every wait is worth the same, anyone ready can take a discard, and dealing in costs twice one's share less what their win would likely cost anyway, doubled with the dealer at either end. A win is taken at once, self-drawn or not (Hard in the fan game sometimes waits for the self-draw); a kong is worth its 3 points besides the free draw; those after the dealer lean to following his first discard, the last of them for the point itself, and the dealer counts the chance of being followed against a tile everyone may hold. The margins counted in points (how much better a call must look, the discards alike enough to choose among, what the coach thinks worth saying) shrink in proportion, to 0.15 of the fan game's. A plain hand two or more from ready is reckoned slower than the fan game reckons it, each step's chance a go-round at a fifth of what its live tiles would give rather than nearly a half (0.47, 0.35, 0.25, 0.2 and 0.12 tried; 0.2 best). In the arena, over 1,600 deals with the seats rotated, the computer player that knows no fan comes out 5.5 points a hand ahead (± 0.16) of the fan game's playing these rules, and the slower reading alone is worth 1.2 (± 0.16); defence weighed at half or one and a half, the margin a call must clear, how often calls come, a second route's share, the others' speed and how often a wait comes off a discard made no difference within the arena's precision. Among the computer players the dealer is followed about one hand in two hundred. A concealed hand at no fan is reckoned as ready to call as an open one (October 2026): at the fan game calling costs a concealed hand its fan, so it seldom calls, and no fan had kept that reading, which made opening the hand look like speed. The coach once advised chowing 5 dots with 3 and 4 out of a made 2-3-4 (the chow then 28% to win, passing 23%), though a chow off the player before only takes the place of a draw, and this one brought nothing; it passes now, and still chows where the call completes a set. Over 12,000 deals in the arena, the seats rotated, the computer player reading it so comes out 0.06 points a hand ahead (± 0.03), winning 25.4% of hands against 24.6%; counting a chow as the draw it replaces as well made no further difference.

Puzzle rush (Menu, October 2026): find the best discard or call in positions from the computer players' own hands, as many as you can, with every game's rush settings (README, Puzzle rush: Random or Today's; 3 min, 5 min or Survival; three misses end the run; until the settings were made one, five minutes and no choice).
