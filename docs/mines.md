# Mines
Minesweeper (October 2026), by its usual rules, on fields that never need a guess. A field of cells hides mines; dig a cell and it shows how many of the cells touching it are mines, and a cell with none round it opens its neighbours by itself, and theirs, so one dig can open a patch. A flag marks a cell you're sure of, and keeps it from your own dig. Tapping an open number is chording (Minesweeper's word for working a number at once): if as many flags stand round it as its number, the rest of its neighbours are dug; if its hidden neighbours are exactly the mines it still lacks, they're flagged. The field is cleared when every cell without a mine is open; flags are never needed. What Codex adds: every field is proven to clear by reasoning alone from a marked start (no guess, ever), its difficulty is the reasoning it takes rather than only its size, it comes in three tilings, each flat or wrapped round a solid you turn in your hand, there's a field a day, and it's played together, as one team that has to talk or as a race. A Rules button (below) has all of it in plain words.

**The start and a mine.** Every field has its start ringed in brass: a cell with no mine on it or round it, so digging it opens a patch, and the solver's proof begins there. Until it's open no other cell can be dug (a tap elsewhere says so), which is also the guard against a first tap that lands a thumb's width off. Since a field never needs a guess, a mine dug is always a slip, of reasoning or of the thumb, and on a phone it's often the thumb: so by default a mine is a strike, as in Survey (it stays on the field, shown, counts as found, and costs 60 seconds) and the field goes on. The menu's A mine: Ends the field is classic Minesweeper's rule for those who want it, the field lost at the first mine, with The same field to try it again. A hint costs 20 seconds: it rings the cheapest thing what's open proves now (green a safe cell, red a mine) and rings in blue the numbers that prove it; it never trusts your flags, so it will also say a flag is wrong. A strike costs three hints on purpose: if a mine cost less than a hint, a stuck player's cheapest way on would be a guess, and the game is about never having to. Your score is your time plus those costs, lower is better; the clock starts with your first dig and stands while the menu, the rules or the games screen is open or the app is away.

**Three tilings.** Squares are Minesweeper's own, each cell touching the eight round it. Hexes (Korbi's example) touch six: pointy-topped hexagons in rows, every other row shifted half a cell. Triangles, pointing up and down in turn along a row, touch twelve: three along their edges and nine more at their corners, as triangle Minesweeper counts them, so a number can be as high as 12. Each comes in three sizes, Small, Medium and Large: for squares Minesweeper's 9×9, 16×16 and its Expert's 30×16 (drawn 16 across and 30 down, to stand up on a phone), and hexes and triangles about the same number of cells (8×10, 14×18 and 16×30 hexes; 11×10, 17×15 and 23×21 triangles, a little fewer cells, since a triangle's number has less room round it). Who touches whom is `mines-grid.js`, checked against the geometry itself: a cell's neighbours are exactly the cells its outline touches. Mines has no content of its own, so nothing comes from the knowledge base: what you choose in the menu's Content is the field, Random or Today's, and its shape (with 3D on or off beside it), size and level, each one of three.

**In 3D** (October 2026). Korbi asked for two switches, hex and 3D, each working on its own. Hexes were already one of the three tilings, so the menu's Content has Shape (Squares, Hexes or Triangles) and under it a 3D switch, and the two together make the field; either deals a new one at once, as Shape always has, and both are kept with the menu's choices (`mines:prefs`, `solid`). 3D wraps the field round a solid, so it has no edge: squares cover a cube made of little cubes, hexes a sphere, triangles a geodesic sphere (Korbi asked for the first two; triangles have the third so that the switch means the same for every shape). A solid is turned in the hand: one finger drags it round as a trackball turns, the cell under the finger following it, and a flick spins it on, slowing within half a second; two fingers pinch it closer or further and, once it's larger than its box, slide it; the magnifier goes between the whole solid and a thumb's size, as on a large flat field. Its sizes are about the flat fields' numbers of cells: a cube 4, 7 and 9 cells along an edge (96, 294 and 486 cells), a sphere of 92, 252 and 492 cells, a geodesic sphere of 80, 320 and 500 triangles. Today's field stays flat (the week's own shapes, below), each kind of solid field keeps bests of its own (`mines:times`, by the field's shape: `cube:medium:hard`), and a field's link names it (`#s=SEED&f=cube.medium.hard`). Everything else is as on a flat field: the start, hints, strikes or sudden death, the computer partner and rival, and both ways of playing together.

**Who touches whom on a solid** (`mines-solid.js`, checked against the geometry as the flat ones are). On the cube a cell touches every cell its square shares a point with, the flat field's own rule, across the cube's edges as well: a cell on an edge touches five on its own face and the three beyond the edge, as if that face were laid flat beside its own. At each of the cube's eight corners three faces meet where a flat field has four squares round a point, so the 24 corner cells touch seven: three on their own face and two on each of the other two, and the three cells round a corner touch one another along the cube's edges. Every other cell touches eight. The sphere is a Goldberg polyhedron blown up onto a sphere: no sphere can be covered with hexagons alone, so exactly twelve of its cells are pentagons. It's made as the dual of an icosahedron whose twenty faces are each cut into m×m triangles (m is 3, 5 and 7) and pushed out onto the sphere: each corner of those triangles is a cell, outlined by the middles of the triangles round it, six of them, or five at the icosahedron's own twelve corners. A cell touches the cells it shares an edge with, so a hexagon's number counts six cells and a pentagon's five. The geodesic sphere is those triangles themselves (m is 2, 4 and 5, so 20·m² of them), each touching every triangle it shares a point with, as on a flat field of triangles: twelve, or eleven beside one of the twelve corners where five triangles meet. Nothing marks a cube's corner, a pentagon or an eleven: the numbers count what touches. A team's numbers are shared out on a solid by colouring its cells so that as many side neighbours as can be are in other shares: a chessboard on each face of the cube (but at its corners, where three cells touching one another can't all differ), two hexagons in three on the sphere, a triangle's three sides on the geodesic sphere; it's found by improving a first guess until no cell gains by moving, the same split on every device.

**A solid's levels.** The solver reads nothing but who touches whom, so it and the proof are unchanged: a solid field is dealt only if the solver clears it from its start without a guess, at the reasoning its level asks, in the same bands as its flat tiling's (the 80-triangle sphere, smaller than a flat Small, a little less). What changes is the mines. A flat field is hardest at its edges and corners, where mines hide behind the border and most fifty-fifties are; a solid has none, so at the same share of mines its random fields clear from their start far more often and need fewer hard moments (at Intermediate's 15.6%, 93 of 100 random medium cubes cleared where 73 of 100 flat 16×16 fields did, with a twentieth of the hard moments). Matched by what the reasoning takes, measured on hundreds of random fields of each, a solid carries more mines than its flat tiling: a quarter to a half more on the cube, about a fifth more on the sphere, a tenth or so on the geodesic sphere. The cube has 18, 22 and 26 mines for Easy, Medium and Hard when small, 54, 60 and 72 medium and 88, 96 and 116 large (18% to 27% of its cells, where squares are 12% to 21%); the sphere 18, 21, 24; 50, 57, 63; 98, 110, 122 (20% to 26%; hexes 15% to 22%); the geodesic sphere 8, 12, 16; 32, 46, 58; 50, 74, 92 (10% to 20%; triangles 9% to 16%). The start is always a cell with a whole neighbourhood, never a cube's corner cell, a pentagon or an eleven. A solid field deals in a few milliseconds (the slowest measured, a large hard sphere, 36 ms in Node).

**Turned to face you.** A cell seen edge-on is a slanted sliver, and a finger on it may have been meant for its neighbour: so a tap or a hold on a cell turned more than about 65° from you, or drawn under half a thumb's size, doesn't dig or flag it but turns it to face you, and the line says to tap it again. On the cube its face is squared to the screen, seen a little from above and from the right so the faces round it show; if the solid is larger than its box it also slides the cell to the middle. A hint turns the cell it names to you the same way, and a field opens with its start facing you.

**How a solid is drawn** (`mines-3d.js`, by hand on one canvas with no library, so it works offline). In perspective, the eye four of the solid's radii away, and lit from the upper left, so each face shows which way it turns. A hidden cell is a tile standing up from the surface with bevelled walls (on the cube, a little cube), a dug one is sunk level with the surface, so a solid reads as the flat field does, and a dug tile sinks as the flood's wave reaches it; between the tiles the solid's body shows in the grooves, a dark brass by day and below the dial at night. Only the cells facing you are drawn, back to front, and of a tile only the walls that face you; what's on a cell (a number, a flag, the start) is drawn upright on the screen whichever way the solid turns, so it always reads, sized to the cell as you see it, smaller as it turns away. A sphere's cells are flat, so its body is their outlines rather than a circle, which showed round the rim of the 80-triangle sphere. The colours are the page's tokens and the palette, flags and mines are the flat field's own (`mines-draw.js`), so every theme by day and by night draws it its own way. A frame of the largest field, the 500-triangle sphere, takes 4 ms of script on average (7 at most) with the processor slowed four times, as a phone's might be, and about one at full speed; nothing is drawn while nothing moves. The view (`mines:view`) keeps the turn, the zoom and the slide, so a reload or another of your devices opens the solid as you left it.

**The rules.** A Rules button in the header, before the menu, and The rules in the menu's play part (at a table too) open the rules on a sheet of their own, as the other classics' do, in short plain prose under small headings: the aim; the rules (what a number counts, who touches whom on each tiling, and an example: a 1 with one hidden cell beside it, a 1 whose mine is found, a 1 2 1 along a wall); in 3D (the three solids and who touches whom on each); playing (the start, digging and flagging, working a number, a slanted cell turned to you, hints and a mine); the levels; the score; and today's field (the seconds a mine and a hint cost and the week's fields read from the engine, so the two never disagree). The sheet scrolls inside itself, its head with Close staying at the top, opens at its top each time, and the clock stands while it's open.

**How a field is made and proven.** `mines-engine.js` lays the level's mines at random, none on the start or round it, and hands the field to the solver (`mines-solve.js`), which plays it from the start the way a person does, cheapest reasoning first, in four levels: one number (a number whose mines are all found frees the rest of its neighbours; one with as many hidden neighbours as mines still missing has them all mines); two numbers together (the 1-1 and 1-2 patterns: what two overlapping numbers prove between them); a group (the numbers round one number, every way of placing their mines tried, keeping what all of them agree on, over a patch of at most 22 hidden cells: a person's "if this were a mine, then…"); and the count (near the end, with 40 hidden cells or fewer, the mines left over every cell left). A field is kept only if the solver clears it, which is the proof that it needs no guess: each step it takes is forced, true in every way the mines could lie given what's open, so at every moment there is a certain move, and the field has exactly one answer. Nothing deeper is used (a search of the whole field mid-game would find more, but no person would), so a field the solver clears is one a person can. The search inside the group and the count has a budget counted in steps, never in time, so every device deals the same field from the same seed. The first field that clears and meets its level is taken, else after 400 tries the one closest to the level among those that cleared (a field that clears is never given up for one that fits better). Random fields at Expert's density clear from their start about one time in six, hexes at their hardest one in ten, triangles two in three; the solver takes a few milliseconds a field, so a deal is quick: in Node the slowest, a large hard hex field, took 0.2 seconds at most and 80 ms on average, and while a large field is dealt the rete turns.

**Difficulty.** Measured by what the reasoning takes, never only by the mines: the hardest level a field needed, and its hard moments, the rounds where one number wasn't enough. Each level asks for a band of hard moments on each shape and size, set from samples of each (squares, Medium: none on Easy, one to five on Medium, five or more on Hard; Large Hard ten or more), with more mines as it rises: squares keep Minesweeper's counts where they meet them (10 on Small Easy, 40 on Medium Medium, 99 on Large Hard, Expert's own). The samples said something about the twists. On hexes two numbers almost never prove more than one does (six neighbours overlap too little: of 230 random medium hex fields at their Medium density that cleared, three needed it, where more than half of the same size of squares do), so a field of hexes either clears one number at a time or sticks on a true fifty-fifty; hexes therefore take more mines than squares for the same level (fewer would leave them all easy), and a Hard one is the densest that still clears, ending on the count. On triangles nearly every field needs two numbers together, and often (the middle Large Hard one 37 times): twelve neighbours overlap a lot, so triangles take fewer mines, and even Easy allows a few such moments. An Easy field of squares or hexes never needs more than one number at a time. `tests/mines.mjs` deals three fields of every shape, size and level and checks each clears from its start, has its level's mines, meets its band (or is the closest of its 400), and that Hard needs more than Medium and Medium more than Easy everywhere.

**Today's field.** One a day, the same for everyone (the suite's day, UTC), growing through the week as a newspaper's puzzles do: Monday small squares, easy; Tuesday small hexes; Wednesday medium squares; Thursday small triangles; Friday medium squares, hard; Saturday Expert (large squares, hard); Sunday large hexes, hard. The menu says which it is. Its score is counted the moment the last safe cell opens and is the games screen's figure for Mines (`mines:today`, the first clear of the day); the tile opens today's field while it's still to play. Today's is played alone (the computer partner sits it out; a rival may race it). Any field also has a link (Copy a link to this field: `#s=SEED&f=shape.size.level`, or `#d=DAY` for a day's).

**Playing together, as one team.** The pair's match from the partner sheet (and a Party's, up to six) is one field for the table whose numbers are shared out: each player reads the numbers on their own share of the cells, and sees the others' dug cells open but with a dot in their colour where the number is. The shares are as fine as the tiling allows, so every number has the others' all round it: a chessboard on squares (every cell's four edge neighbours are the partner's), alternate rows on hexes (four of the six), up and down triangles on triangles; for three or more a lattice of the same kind. Flags (in their placer's colour), digs, mines, hints and the clock are the team's, and anyone may dig or flag anywhere. Neither of you can clear it alone: a team's field is dealt only if the solver, reading just one share of the numbers (and the empty cells, which open for everyone), gets stuck before the end for each of you, while both shares together clear it, so the less able player holds numbers the other can't do without, from the first patch to the last. The way to talk is to talk; for a pair apart, Point (a third tool, while together) rings a cell on everyone's screen for four seconds, and pointing at one of your own numbers shows it to everyone from then on, underlined in your colour: "what's this one?" and its answer. A player away for half a minute (a phone asleep, Back to solo) hands their share to the others, so a table never waits on a phone that slept. A pair's cleared field goes to the pair's record as the team's time (a field lost to a mine counts as played, not as a time). Measured with two computer players each holding half the numbers, a split cell by cell needs a lot of talk: about 35 numbers shown on a medium field, 135 on Expert. Splitting into blocks of two to four cells was tried and barely changed it (most cells of a small block sit on its edge); only quarters of the field did, at the cost of each working alone, which is what the split is meant to prevent.

**As a race.** The menu's together part chooses the next match: One team or Race. A race is a head-to-head on one deal (README, Head to head): everyone gets the same field, digs it on their own phone from the same countdown, and sees the others' progress under the score (the share of safe cells open, sent every second and a half); the lowest score at a cleared field wins, and a field lost to a mine (when the table plays that rule) comes last. Your own field, bests and today's stay as they were. The table is `duel.js`'s in both modes, its lobby, countdown, Rematch and Back to solo; the team's field lives beside the table in the same branch (`field`, in `mines-team.js`), every move a transaction on it, shown on your screen at once and set aside if the table has moved on (a cell someone else dug a moment before). Choosing a shape, 3D, a size or a level in the menu deals a new match at it for everyone (asking first if one is under way); the rule for a mine, and One team or Race, apply from the next. A race's winner goes to the pair's record as a win.

**The computer.** Settings › Computer: Off, Partner or Rival, at a pace of Steady, Quick or Sharp, for your own fields. Both think with the solver and are paced like a person: a moment to see what a number proves (longer for two numbers than for one, longer still for a group or the count) and a moment for each tap. The partner is a one-person team: it holds the other half of the numbers (its cells carry its copper dot), digs and flags what its half proves, trusts its own flags and never yours (it points at a flag of yours it knows is wrong), and when the field has stood still for four seconds it shows you one of its numbers that would let you on, or points at one of yours it needs (Point at it to show it); after nine it asks for the next of yours on the edge all the same, since sometimes it takes two at once. Point at one of its numbers and it shows you that too. A steady partner reasons with no more than two numbers at a time and leaves the rest to you; Sharp uses all four levels. A partnered field keeps bests of its own. The rival plays its own copy of your field: its whole game is worked out when the field is dealt, from the solver's steps and its pace (with now and then a slip of the thumb onto a mine), so it's shown against your clock, pausing when you do, as a share in the strip and its score once it's through; your result says who won, and the menu keeps the count against each pace. On a medium field of squares, Steady takes about four minutes, Quick two and a half, Sharp one.

**Twists.** Minesweeper's real variants, and what came of each: hexes and triangles are in (the logic changes with the neighbours, as above, and each looks its own); classic sudden death is the menu's rule for a mine; a field without edges came as the 3D solids, where you can see it has none (a flat field that wraps round at its edges was left out, since on a phone the wrap can't be seen); several mines to a cell, and the puzzle variants where numbers lie or count a knight's move away were left out, as changing what a number means rather than where it looks. Minesweeper Flags (MSN Messenger's two-player game: turns, each mine found scoring, a find giving another turn) would make a third way to play together, against each other on one field; it isn't built (below).

**Fingers.** A tap digs (or flags, with Flag chosen); holding a finger down for a moment does the other, with a buzz; a right click flags with a mouse. Holding a number rings its neighbours (on hexes and triangles, where the eye doesn't know them yet), and a number tapped that can't chord rings them briefly. A field that fits the box at a thumb's size (34 px a square cell on a phone, 36 a hexagon's width, 40 a triangle's side; more on a tablet) or nearly (80% of it) is drawn whole; a larger one opens close up round the start, pans under one finger and zooms between the whole field and well past a thumb's size with two (a mouse: the wheel pans, with Ctrl it zooms), and the magnifier at its corner goes between the whole field and close up. A drag never digs: a finger that moves more than nine pixels is panning (on a solid, turning it; above). There's no undo, on purpose: a dug cell has shown its number, so taking the dig back would be a free look under the paper; a flag comes off with the same tap or hold that put it on, and a mine dug by a slip costs its minute and the field goes on (with sudden death, The same field starts it over). The page never scrolls: the field takes all the room under the score, the line and the tools under it; held sideways the field takes the left with all the height and the score, the line and the tools stand at the right, the tools low. The field is one canvas (`mines-draw.js`), drawn from the theme's own tokens (paper tiles with a brass edge standing up from the page, dug cells sunk into it; at night the tiles lit a little and the dug cells the dial itself), with Minesweeper's colours for the numbers in enamels (1 cobalt, 2 verdigris, 3 oxblood, 4 navy, and on), every theme by day and by night. A flood opens as a wave from the cell dug.

**Kept.** The field in play is `mines:run` (its seed and settings, its cells as one character each, strikes, hints, the time, what the partner has shown and asked), saved at every move and every five seconds while the clock runs, so a reload, another of your devices or a partner watching sees it as it stands, the computer partner's moves and its rings included; the view, how close and where (and on a solid how it's turned, kept as a quaternion: four numbers that name a turn in 3D without the gimbal lock of three angles, where two axes line up and one way of turning is lost), is `mines:view`. Bests, the lowest score by shape, size and level (and with the computer partner apart), are `mines:times`; your record against the rival's paces `mines:rivals`; the menu's choices `mines:prefs`; a race's own run `mines:duel`.

**Tests.** `tests/mines.mjs` checks the tilings (neighbours both ways, exactly the cells that touch, a finger finding its cell, the shares covering the field about evenly for two to six), the rules (the start first, the flood, flags, both chords, a wrong flag under a chord, strikes, sudden death, 3BV: Bechtel's Board Benchmark Value, the fewest clicks that clear a field without flags, which speed players divide a time by), every deduction the solver makes on small random fields against every way the mines could lie, a one-number and a two-number deduction each following from those numbers alone, the patterns at their levels, the dealt fields as above, the same field from the same seed, hints that alone clear a field and name a flagged cell safe, the rival (it clears its copy, the same way each time; Sharp quicker than Quick, Quick than Steady), the split (both shares clear every team field, neither alone does) and two computer partners clearing fields together with no mine dug, talking, the team's field through the database's own round trip (the start first, moves on an older copy set aside, flags by seat, showing and pointing, two players clearing it, a rematch leaving it behind, sudden death), a run kept as JSON at every move, and today's field the same for everyone. For the solids (October 2026): the cube at four sizes (6n² cells, the 24 corner cells touching seven and the rest eight, neighbours both ways and exactly the cells whose squares share a point, an edge cell's three beyond the edge, a corner cell's three and two and two, the three round a corner touching), the Goldberg sphere at five (10m² + 2 cells, exactly twelve pentagons touching five and the rest hexagons touching six, cells touching exactly when their outlines share an edge, every corner on the sphere, and corners − edges + cells = 2, Euler's count for anything shaped like a ball), the geodesic sphere at three (20m² triangles, twelve or eleven neighbours, three along the sides), the shares for two to six on each (and for two, 92% of the cube's side neighbours in the other share, 66% of the sphere's, 93% of the geodesic's), every deduction on small cubes, spheres and geodesic spheres against every way the mines could lie (that check now lists only the cells next to a number and counts the rest, which are all alike, so it's exact and quick on a solid too), fields of every solid, size and level dealt and cleared from their start at their level, more mines than the flat tiling's level for level, the same field from the same seed, the rival, team fields that need both shares, two computer partners clearing them, and runs kept as JSON. In a browser: fields of every shape played to the end with mouse clicks; every solid played to a win by touch (Chromium's own touch events: a tap digs, a long press flags, a drag turns the solid, a slanted cell turns to face you before a tap on it counts, and no tap on one ever dug), the largest at a quarter of the processor's speed; the zoom button and a pinch, a flick that spins and then keeps the view, a hint turning its cell to you, a reload bringing back the run and the turn, the 3D switch both ways from the menu, today's staying flat with it on; a team match on a cube and a race on a sphere in two tabs on the stand-in database; the computer partner on a cube and the rival on a sphere; every solid in every theme by day and night at 390×600, 664 and 844 and on an iPad both ways, the start dug by touch, nothing scrolling or changing size; the rules sheet; touch through Chromium's own touch events (a tap digs, a held finger flags, a drag pans and digs nothing, two fingers zoom); a team match and a race in two tabs on the stand-in database (the same field on both, complementary numbers, the clear on both, the race's result), on a phone and on an iPad held sideways; today's field from the games screen's tile, a reload mid-field, the menu stopping the clock, a field lost to a mine and dealt again, the rival, and a field cleared with the computer partner, its requests answered by pointing; every theme by day and night at 390×664, and some of them at 390×600 and 844 and on an iPad both ways.

**Not done.** Minesweeper Flags, the turn-taking head-to-head on one field. A computer player at a table (duel.js has no computer seats, so the partner and rival are for your own fields). A table of three or more as one team is tested in its parts (the shares for up to six, who reads when someone's away), not played through. A dealing worker: deals run on the page, fast in Node, not yet timed on an iPhone. Untried on a real phone: the hold and the pinch on iOS Safari, and the buzz (iPhones have none from a page).
