# Calcudoku
Fill the grid so that every row and every column holds each digit once, and every cage makes its target by its operation (October 2026): the genre KenKen made famous, under the name setters use for it. A cage's digits add, multiply, subtract or divide to its target (+ and × over any number of cells, − and ÷ over two, the larger digit first, as printed puzzles have them), and a cage of one cell is a digit given in all but name. Unlike Killer's, a cage may repeat a digit wherever its cells share no row or column, so an L of three cells adding up to 4 is 2, 1, 1. Six sizes, 4×4 to 9×9 (Size, in the menu, where picking one deals a new grid), and three rules (Rules, one of): **Classic**, each cage's target and operation; **Hidden**, the targets alone, so which operation makes each cage is part of the puzzle (the no-operation variant); **Products**, every cage a product, a puzzle of prime factors. The grid is drawn on the digit grid Sudoku made (`digits.js`), its cages as heavy rules with the clue in the corner of each cage's first cell, as KenKen prints them; nothing in the board was changed for it. There is nothing to choose from the knowledge base: the genre's content is its arithmetic.

**Twists**, weighed. Kept, each because it changes the reasoning: Hidden, where a 2 on two cells could be 1 and 2 (by × or ÷), 1 and 3 (by −) or 2 and 4 (by − or ÷), so every cage is read by every operation; and Products (Simon Tatham's Keen has it as Multiplication only), where you reason by factors: only a cage whose product holds a 7 can hold a 7, and a line multiplies to n!. Left out: zero allowed (digits 0 to n−1: a product with a 0 in it is 0 and says only that the cage holds one, and the rest is the classic's reasoning with other numbers); sums only (Killer without its boxes, which Sudoku has); − and ÷ over more than two cells (a "largest first" convention some sites use, arbitrary to a solver); modulo, powers and negative digits (gimmicks); boxes as in Sudoku (that is Killer again); and anything past 9×9, whose cells would be too small for a thumb and a clue on a phone.

**How a grid is made, with one solution.** `calcudoku-cage.js` lists every way of filling a cage that makes its target (digits differing wherever cells share a line; a hidden operation is every way any operation makes it), and `calcudoku-solve.js` is an exact solver on top: it settles what's forced (a cell with one candidate, a digit with one place in a line, every cage's cells narrowed to the digits its remaining ways use), then tries each candidate of the cell with fewest, counting solutions up to two. A grid starts as a random Latin square (the same solver filling an empty grid in a random order), cut into cages grown from random cells to sizes drawn from the level's odds (mostly two and three cells; fours at the harder levels, and fives only from 7×7 up). Each gets a clue: two cells a quotient where one digit divides the other (printed puzzles favour them), else most often a difference; longer cages a sum or a product, a product only up to 999 in Classic so a phone's corner never has to hold "5184×" (and in Products a cage that would multiply past 9999 is split). A cage with more than 800 ways of being made is split too: that is more than a person weighs (a 9×9's six cells adding up to 27 have tens of thousands), and it made the solvers crawl. Then the human-style solver below works on the grid with the techniques up to the level's hardest. Where it gets stuck, a short exact search (300 guesses) looks for a second solution: if there is one, the cage where the two differ gets another clue that rules it out, or is split; if not, the grid is only too hard there, and the stuck cage gets the sharpest clue its digits make (the one with the fewest ways), or is split. Once the solver finishes the grid, which on its own proves one solution (every step is sound, so nothing but the solution it found survives them), cages are joined while it still finishes it: first the cells left on their own past the level's few (Easy keeps up to half a line of them, Medium two or three, Hard and Expert one or two), then, while the grid is easier than its level, the cages that gave the solver its first footholds (joined at random, a grid hardened far less often). Last, the exact solver counts its solutions, up to two, which on a finished grid takes milliseconds. A try that misses is thrown away for the next seed in the same stream, so the same seed always makes the same grid (integer arithmetic only), which is how today's grid and a match's are the same on every phone, and how a link (`#s=SEED&r=RULES&z=SIZE&l=LEVEL`, or `#d=DAY`) brings one back. The grids look like a newspaper's: of the classic 6×6s made in testing, an Easy's cages were 27% sums, 21% products, 20% differences, 13% quotients and a sixth single cells; an Expert's 43%, 28%, 17%, 7% and one in twenty.

**How hard a grid is, and the levels.** Difficulty is the hardest technique a person needs, never the number of cages. `calcudoku-logic.js` is a human-style solver that always takes the easiest step it can find, each technique a function that finds one step and says why, in tiers that follow Keen's: rank 1, the one-cell cage, the last cell of a line, hidden and naked singles, and a cage's arithmetic (a cage read on its own against its cells' candidates: they keep only the digits some way of making it still uses, the tightest cage first); rank 2, a cage in a line (every way of making a cage puts a digit in its cells on one line, so no other cell of that line has it), a line into a cage (a line's every place for a digit lies in one cage, so the cage's ways narrow to those with it there), naked and hidden pairs and triples, and a line's total (a line adds up to n(n+1)/2 and multiplies to n!: less the digits placed and the cages wholly inside it with a known total, the one to three cells left over make the rest); rank 3, the X-wing, swordfish and XY-wing, quads, and the totals of two or three lines side by side (each digit twice or three times); rank 4, a contradiction (suppose a cell of two candidates took one: the plainest steps break the grid within twelve of them, so it takes the other). A grid that needs more (a guess) is never dealt. From 6×6 up the four levels are the four ranks: Easy, Medium, Hard and Expert. A 4×4 is over before anything past a cage and a line comes up, so it has Easy and Medium (anything beyond the plainest); a 5×5 adds Hard (ranks 3 and 4); a Products 5×5, whose cages say so much, has only Easy and Medium. Every one of the tests' 62 grids (each rules, size and level) lands on its level. Grids are made in a worker (`calcudoku-worker.js`), so a hard one never holds the screen: on the machine here a 6×6 of any kind takes under half a second, a 9×9 Classic or Products under one, and a Hidden 9×9 Hard or Expert half a second to two; a phone is slower, so one spare grid of each kind is kept ready on the device, made in the background after each deal, and New grid is at once (the rete shows only when there's none).

**Today's grid** is a classic 6×6 at Medium, the same for everyone (`today()`'s date its seed), counted the moment its last digit goes in: the games screen shows today's time (`noteDayTime`, kept in `calcudoku:today`) and opens on it while it's still to play. It's always played alone, with the computer off, so the times compare.

**Hints teach.** Hint costs 30 seconds. A wrong digit comes first (every step after a wrong one is built on sand), struck through; then pencil marks that leave out the digit their cell needs; then the easiest step from the grid as it stands, counting your pencil marks where you've made them, named in the line under the grid ("The 16× cage can only be made as 1 × 4 × 4 here, so the lit cells lose every other digit"; "Every way to make the 7+ cage puts a 1 in its lit cells, all in row 2: no other cell there is 1"; "Columns 8 and 9 hold each digit twice; less what's placed and the cages wholly inside, the lit cells multiply to 36"; "Suppose row 5, column 5 were 3: follow the lit cells and the cage of 189 could no longer be made. So it's 6"), its cells lit and its target picked out. In Hidden the hint knows no more than you: it reads each cage by every operation. Mark clashes (on by default) marks in oxblood a digit twice in a row or column and a full cage whose digits don't make its target, reading only what's on the board, never the solution. Fill in every pencil mark (in the menu) writes the digits each cell's row, column and cage, read on its own, still allow.

**The computer** (3 November 2026, Korbi: "'against the computer' makes little sense"; "probably drop from all": no rival that races you, on any puzzle) is the same solver at a person's pace (`calcudoku-bot.js`): each step takes the time a person needs for its technique (a one-cell cage a second, a hidden single four, a cage's arithmetic two and a half and a second more for every doubling of the ways it weighs, a cage in a line twelve, a line's total half a minute, an X-wing 45, a contradiction a minute and a half and 8 seconds a step of its chain), singles quicker with many on offer, with a little luck either way; at three paces, Steady (a newspaper solver), Keen (a regular) and Sharp (a competition solver). Keen takes about a minute over a 4×4 Easy, five and a half over a 6×6 Medium, ten over a 6×6 Expert, fifteen over a 9×9 Medium and 27 over a 9×9 Expert; Steady half as long again, Sharp a little over half. In the menu (Computer: Off, With it): **With it** gives you and it a share of the clues each, as a pair at a table gets (below): its clues are a "?" on your grid, its digits arrive in copper as it finds them, working from what it can see (your digits included), and it waits for you when that isn't enough. A grid solved with it isn't your best: it's a team's time.

**Playing together.** A duo match (asked for from the partner sheet) or a Party's table of up to six (a race first; a pair starts Together), on the head-to-head table (`duel.js`: the lobby, Ready, the countdown to a start everyone shares), in either of two ways, switched from the menu (Race instead, Together instead), which deals a new match for everyone at the rules, size and level of whoever asks. **A race**: the same grid for everyone, each on their own phone, the others' digits to go in the line over the grid; the lowest time with hints wins; the pair's record counts the wins. **Together**: one grid for the table, and each player sees only their share of the cages' clues. Every cage is outlined on every screen, but a clue only someone else holds shows as a soft "?". What anyone writes is everyone's at once, in that player's colour, and anyone may write anywhere; each of the others' cells is framed on your grid in their colour with their initial, as in Sudoku, so two don't set off for the same one; pencil marks stay your own; hints are the team's (30 seconds each, the hint shown to whoever asked, from the clues they see); the team's time runs from the start to the last right digit, recorded as the pair's best. The less able player can't just watch: they hold clues the other needs. The clues are dealt round the table in reading order, then shown to everyone a few at a time until a relay finishes (each player works out all they can from what they see, at the grid's own level; what they find goes to the others; and round again). Shown at random, more were shown than the relay needed (on a hard grid one player was left holding none, and the other could finish alone), so each shared clue is then offered back to whoever holds fewest and kept private wherever the relay still finishes. On the grids measured, each player of a pair keeps 4 to 9 clues to themselves on a 6×6 (0 to 4 shared) and 9 to 16 on a 9×9 (2 to 10 shared), and the relay takes 2 to 9 rounds; the tests count each share's solutions with the exact solver and never find fewer than two, so neither can finish alone, and the two can always finish without a word (asking "what's your 12 in the corner?" is quicker). A table of three to six gets its shares the same way, round the table. The split is worked out from the grid and the table's size on each phone, in the worker (up to half a second here), the same everywhere. The players who began at the start hold the shares; one who sits down later shares one of theirs; a share whose player has been away for 20 seconds is lent to everyone else (its clues shown) until they're back, so a phone that sleeps never leaves the table stuck. Computer players don't sit at a table, as in Sudoku: a seat nobody has would be no share at all, and a share that goes unheld is lent. Two phones (two browsers on a stand-in database) played a Together match to the last digit, each seeing only their own clues and every digit arriving on the other's board, and then a race.
**Co-op** (3 November 2026, `docs/coop.md`, `calcudoku-coop.js`, `digits-coop.js`): a paired partner who watches a solo grid can ask to join it, and both then write on one grid with every cage's clue shown to both: not Together above, which shares the clues out on `duel.js` and is never this. Digits and pencil marks are shared (a layer of digits and one of marks for each digit, so two players marking different digits in one cell both stand); selection is each player's own and your partner's cell is framed as in Together; undo leaves what your partner changed since; a hint is the team's. No computer partner, no Show the solution, and a shared solve earns no best, today's time or Random round.

**Controls.** As Sudoku's grid has them: tap a cell, then a digit; Notes writes in pencil; a digit tapped with no cell selected lights where it stands; each key shows how many of its digit are still to place; Undo, Redo, Erase, Notes and Hint under the keys; Fill in every pencil mark and Show the solution in the menu; on a keyboard the arrows, the digits (with Shift in pencil), Backspace, N for Notes, Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y. On a phone the keys are a row of four to nine, taller on a tall phone; at 390 by 600 nothing scrolls and a 9×9's four-digit products fit their cells. The clue in a cell's corner has no paper behind it, the digits are a little smaller and lower than Sudoku's to clear it, and the pencil marks in a clue's cell sit under it. The line under the grid is three lines high whatever it says (two on a short phone, where a longer hint scrolls inside it), and the clock and the kind over the grid never wrap, so a hint arriving never moves the pad or shrinks the grid. On an upright tablet the phone's layout is drawn larger. Held sideways, the grid takes the left with all the height and the clock, the line and a keypad three across (two for a 4×4) stand beside it, the keys at the bottom.

**The rules sheet** (October 2026, as in every classic): Rules in the header, or The rules in the menu, opens the rules in short plain prose: the aim, a cage's clue and its operations (− and ÷ on two cells, a digit repeating only where its cells share no line), the three rule sets, a worked example (an L of three cells making 4+), how to play here, the levels with each technique said in a few words and the sizes that offer them, the time and today's grid. It scrolls inside itself under its pinned head, opens at its top each time, and the clock stands while it's open. Rules beside the menu's button left Calcudoku's long name no room for a partner's pill on a 390-pixel phone: the pill's margin (the title's own gap spaces it already) pushed the buttons 8 pixels past the header's edge, so on a narrow phone it has none and the buttons close up a little, as Mahjong's and Guandan's do.

**Scoring and what's kept.** A grid's time is the time it was on screen: the clock stops while the menu, the rules or the games screen is open and while the phone is elsewhere, and the grid hides behind a plate meanwhile, so the stopped clock buys no thinking. A hint adds 30 seconds. Bests are by rules, size and level, lowest first, in `calcudoku:times` (not `calcudoku:best`, which the games screen reads as highest-wins); the games screen's figure is today's time. The run in play is `calcudoku:run`: the grid itself (its solution, each cell's cage, the operations and the targets, as strings, so it opens at once and a partner watching sees it without making it), your entries with their undo, the selection, the Notes switch, the time and hints, and with the computer its share and its next digit; the settings `calcudoku:settings`, the spares `calcudoku:spares`, the way a table plays `calcudoku:together`, and a match's own run `calcudoku:duel` (duel.js keeps it), so a reload anywhere comes back as it was.

`tests/calcudoku.mjs` checks a cage's ways (repeats only where cells share no line, − and ÷ on two cells, a hidden operation's ways each once) and the labels, the exact solver (one solution, none, two), 62 grids of every rules, size and level (one solution each, counted; at their level; the cages a partition of the grid, each in one piece, its clue made by its digits, none past four digits or 800 ways; every step of the human-style solver agreeing with the solution and having words; the solver finishing each), that the grids need techniques of every rank and use every operation, grading and hints on them, the computer (every digit right, in order, the paces in order, the same race every time, a partner's next digit right), grids through JSON and the database (more than 36 cages included) and tampered ones refused, the splits (every clue seen by someone, a private share each, neither able to finish alone, the relay finishing, the same on every phone, for a pair and a table of three), the table through the database's round trip (a digit in for this match only, who placed it, the last right digit solving it with its time, the team's hints, a share nobody holds, a latecomer's share), today's grid the same each time it's made, with nothing in the engine but its seed, and the levels each size offers; about ten seconds.

Not done: there's no mistake limit or check-as-you-go mode beyond Mark clashes (a hint finds a wrong digit, at 30 seconds); computer players don't take seats at a table (above); a Hidden 9×9 Hard can take a phone a few seconds the first time (the next is already made); a table of three or more and a share lent while its player is away are checked in the tests but weren't played in a browser. Whether a 6×6 Medium is the right daily (a weekday ladder, a 4×4 on Monday up to a 9×9 at the weekend, would suit a codex), whether Keen's pace matches a regular, and whether Zero deserves a place after all are for Korbi's thumb to say.
