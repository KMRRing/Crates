# Slate
(Called Glyph until October 2026: glyph.html forwards to slate.html, and saves, rooms and the switcher id still say glyph, so nothing in play broke.)

Tap the title (Crates or Slate) to switch games. Slate lives at slate.html.

Fill a small crossword so every across and down run is a real word. Coloured fields carry secret letter rules, and the colour family says what kind:
- Blues and greens judge each letter on its own (a tick or cross on the letter).
- Yellows and oranges judge neighbouring pairs, read left to right and top to bottom (the mark sits on the join).
- Purples and pinks, dashed, judge the whole field once every cell is filled (one mark for the field).

When a board ends, the result shows lives lost, placements, checks, clues (rules revealed) and time (time on screen for solo boards; from dealing to the end in a together game). On Easy, checks, suggestions and rule reveals are unlimited; otherwise two of each per board. Check and Suggest judge against every winning board, not our fill: any fill of real words from the full list (repeats allowed) that keeps every rule, including the whole-field rules that can't be judged yet. Check asks which letters must change for a winning board to exist, keeping all the others: letters nearest the highlighted word are protected first (rings counted outward through crossings; no nearer letter is marked while changes further out would do), then fewer letters, then typed letters before placed ones. Those letters get an orange outline until they change, and while the cursor is on one its key is outlined too; a clean board says "All of this can still win". The answer is exact when the search finishes, and says so when time ran out first. Typing: tap a cell and type; the cursor moves along the row (the default) or, after tapping the same cell again, down the column, and Place (bottom left of the keyboard) puts the word down. Letters typed but not placed belong to their word: moving within it (a tap or the arrow keys) keeps them, moving to another word drops them. Retyping one letter of a word and pressing Place counts as a new placement. Delete empties the cell under the cursor (typed or placed) and the cursor stays; on an empty cell it steps back one and empties that, so repeated presses walk back along the word. On a keyboard the arrow keys move the cursor, as in the New York Times crossword: pressed across the word being typed where another word crosses, the first press turns to that word; otherwise the cursor goes to the next cell that way, over gaps, and typing then runs that way wherever a word does. A complete word that isn't in the word list can still be placed: you're told, it's underlined in red, and like a frame it isn't judged. Placing letters that don't complete a word is allowed, to build a frame: they show greyed with a dotted underline, aren't judged, don't count as placements, and the first Clear removes them; a letter is judged once it's part of a complete real word. While the cursor is in a single-letter field, the keyboard shows the letters that field has taken (green) and rejected (red). In a pair field, once a neighbour in the same field has a letter (placed or typed), the keyboard shows the letters known to go with it (green) and known not to (red), from the pairs tried so far, each read in the field's direction (left to right, top to bottom); with two lettered neighbours, green needs both known to pass and red needs one known to fail. In a whole field (October 2026), once every other cell of the field has a letter (placed or typed), the keyboard shows the letters known to complete it (green: the field has passed with that set of letters) and known not to (red: it has failed with them), from the whole fields tried so far, so a set already judged isn't tried again; this works from any cell of a field of three, four or five. A whole field's rule looks only at which letters the field holds, never at their order (true of every whole rule; tests/slate.mjs holds them to it), so a set counts in any arrangement: once ABC has failed, B and C in the other two cells, either way round, turn A red. Repeats count (AAB failing says nothing about ABB), and while another cell of the field is empty nothing is coloured, since the letter still to come could change the verdict. With the field's rule revealed, every letter is coloured (in a whole field, once its other cells are filled). One function decides for all three kinds of field (`keyVerdicts` in slate-gen.js), so the cases can't drift apart. Clear (between Fields and Check letters) escalates: the first press empties letters that aren't part of a complete real word, the next also words that break a field's rule, the next the whole board; clearing never counts as a placement. Fields opens a sheet with everything each field has passed and failed, and a ? per field: two per board (the "2 × ?" beside Close counts them down) reveal a field's rule (for a single-letter field the keyboard then shows every letter green or red). In a together game a revealed rule, and that field's marks, are shown to everyone at the table. New board is in the menu. The board and keyboard size themselves to the visible screen, clear of Safari's bars. Our fill uses only very common words; any fill that obeys every rule and makes real words wins too. Every rule is revealed when the board ends.

Lives and the rules in play (November 2026). Guessing used to be as good as working a rule out: a wrong word cost nothing, so a player could try fills until one stuck, and the rules hardly mattered. Now a board has lives (five on Easy, six on Medium, seven on Hard, shown as pips in the header; at a table they are the team's). The pips first sat in a row of their own over the board; on a 664 px phone that row took a cell's width off the board (Korbi, 1 November 2026: "the board is now a bit small on mobile"), so they moved up beside the menu button and the board has its old room back, and the board says which rules are in play. A word that puts up a failing mark (a cross on a letter, on a pair's join, or on a whole field) that wasn't on the board before costs one life, however many marks it puts up; a mark that stays up costs nothing again, and clearing a word and laying it again costs again. A frame, a check, a suggestion and a rule revealed cost nothing. The toast that says a life went names the field that failed, so it's plain which rule is costing you. The last life ends the board ("Out of lives": our fill and every rule are shown) and the result's headline is lives lost, not time, since time rewarded brute force; the games screen's daily mark is still the time of a solve. A board dealt before lives (a saved one) plays as before, without them.

The rules in play (the legend, `legend` on the board, made with it in `generate`) are the fields' rules and, for each type of field, two other rules of the level that could have been (three on Hard), listed in the catalogue's order so the list gives no field away. A field's sheet lists every rule in play of its kind, whole rule beside its name, and crossing off the ones the field can't be is the player's work (1 November 2026; Korbi: "the fields should show the options that exist for that category (single double group) but it should be the users job to go back and cross of impossible options manually"). The first version narrowed the list itself from what the field had passed and failed and said "Worked out", which did the deduction for you. Now a tap crosses a rule out and a second tap takes it back; the marks are pencil marks, yours alone, kept per board on your devices (`glyph:struck`; at a table each player keeps their own), and nothing checks them: cross off the true rule and the keyboard will mislead you. The keyboard follows what you've left: a letter is green when every rule left takes it and red when every one rejects it (`keyVerdictsAmong`), so the more you've worked out, the more keys it colours. `legendOf` and `optionsOf` in slate-gen.js are the lists; `candidates` and `dealsOf` (what the notes allow, each rule one field's at most) stay for the bots below, who play as a careful player would. A rule revealed still shows a field's rule outright. A life is counted by `replay` in slate-gen.js, the same replay of the log that makes the notes, so a table agrees without storing anything but the log.

Measured, not guessed (`tests/slate-lives-bots.mjs`, its players in `tests/slate-bots.js`): a guesser that lays any real words that fit, the most filled word first and every crossing word kept possible, wins none of eight boards at any level in its lives (and one that also reads the keyboard's colours wins about a fifth of Easy boards and almost none beyond). A planner that guesses which rule each field has from the legend and the notes, asks the solver for a winning fill under each guess that keeps what's down and lays the word that passes under most guesses, loses 1.5 lives on average on Easy, 2.7 on Medium and 2.8 on Hard, and wins every Easy board in five lives, 98% of Medium in six and 93% of Hard in seven (the rest were searches that ran out of time); without the list of rules in play it loses 3.8 on Easy where it loses 1.7 with it. So the lives are tight enough that guessing loses and loose enough that careful play has room, and the list is worth having. A planner never uses Suggest or Check, which a player has two of, so people should do better than the planner on a hard board. No board is thrown out for being easy to guess, since none was.

Boards come in 36 shapes (12 per level) in which every horizontal and every vertical run of two or more cells is a word of three to five letters: there are no two-letter runs, so rows stacked on each other are always joined by real down words. Each board turns or mirrors its shape at random. Easy shapes have five or six words, Medium six to eight, Hard eight to ten with rows stacked into blocks. tests/shapes.mjs checks every shape in every orientation. Difficulty comes from the rules, each rated easy, medium or hard; every level mixes all three field types. The rule catalogue (slate-gen.js): vowels, consonants, alphabet halves and thirds, straight or curved letters, enclosed spaces, keyboard rows, typing hand, mirror symmetry, odd alphabet positions, rarer letters and hidden keywords for single letters; ascending, descending, vowel-consonant, same class, same shape, same keyboard row, same hand, within three letters and double letters for pairs; no duplicates, no vowels, exactly one vowel, a repeated letter, one shape, rearranges into a word, one keyboard row and an even alphabet sum for whole fields.

Together: a pair's match (asked for from the partner sheet on the games screen) or a party's table of up to six (README, Party), each on their own phone, everyone filling the one board at once and sharing everything: the placements, the checks and clues, and every field's marks (October 2026; until then two, in seats 0 and 1). Each letter shows in the colour of whoever put it there (a word laid through someone else's letter leaves that letter theirs; a frame's letters paler), and the word each of the others is on has a bar of their colour along its cells' feet, their initial in the corner of the cell they're on, so you can see who is working where and not type over them; the line over the board names everyone in their colours, dimmed while their phone sleeps or they've gone. Checks, rule reveals and suggestions are the team's (two of each per board between everyone, as alone), and a teammate's check or reveal is said in a line as it comes, since its orange letters or coloured keys would otherwise just appear. The result counts the words each placed. Emptying the board (Clear's third press) asks first when some of the letters are someone else's. Each request said yes to (`?new=`, a party host's or your partner's; a party names Slate by its branch, glyph, the partner sheet by its name, slate) deals a new table, whoever arrives first dealing it at their own level and seating as many as it asked for (`sitDown` in rooms.js); a reload, or your other device, sits you back in your own seat, letters and all. Opened in a room, the page shows the rete until the table's board is drawn, never your own meanwhile (whose clock used to run while the room was joined), and your own only if the join doesn't come off; Back to solo (the menu) returns to it as you left it. Joining reads only Slate's part of the room, not the whole room, with that part held while joining so the joining transaction takes one round trip, not two. A table dealt as you sit down is at your own board's level, Easy before you've had one. Each player's cursor is written to their own seat (`players/<uid>/at`) as it moves, at most every 150 ms; the board's redraw for someone else's news waits while a finger is on it, since a cell redrawn under a finger loses its tap. The table is `slate-room.js`, pure; `tests/slate-room.mjs` seats parties of three and six and a pair, deals for either kind of request, gives a player's other device their seat, and fills a board with six through the database's round trip, every letter keeping its placer.

Words: SCOWL word lists (sizes 10-20 for our fills, up to 70 with British and American spellings for what you may place), packed in slate-words.js.

Suggest: a word for the highlighted slot that lies on a winning board keeping every letter entered, placed or typed; our fill's word where it can be, else common words first. It's typed into the slot for you to place with Enter. If no winning board keeps your letters it says so ("No winning board keeps your current letters", or "No solution found" when the search ran out of time) and isn't spent: Check shows what has to change. A full slot has nothing to suggest. Both are worked out off the page (`slate-worker.js`, `slate-solve.js`): a depth-first search over the full word list, the slot with fewest words left first, with the rules propagated as letters go in (a single-letter field narrows its cells, a pair narrows the partner of a placed letter, a whole field narrows as letters arrive and exactly at its last cell); Check goes ring by ring outward, learning small sets of letters that can't all stay and dropping the fewest that break them all. `tests/slate-solve.mjs` plays simulated boards and checks that clean boards (ours or another winning one) are called clean, that the orange letters are enough and each is needed, and that Suggest's words win. Definitions: double-tap a cell to see the complete words through it defined; the double tap leaves the cursor and any typed letters as they were. The dictionary is Slate's own (`slate-defs.js`), so it works offline: up to three senses for each of the 10,510 words players may place, from Open English WordNet 2025 (CC BY 4.0, credited in the dialog), the first sense of each part of speech first. An inflected form shows its base word's senses (BILLS: plural of BILL), and a word that is also a form of a common word says so (FOUND: also the past tense of FIND). The 1,500 or so words WordNet lacks (function words, slang, abbreviations, dialect and archaic words) were defined for Slate; 35 of the rarest have no entry. It is 877 KB (329 KB as served), loaded on the first lookup rather than with the board, and replaced dictionaryapi.dev, a free service that was often down and lacked common words. One entry per line, so edit it in place; `tests/slate-defs.mjs` checks every fill word is defined and every pointer lands on a definition.

On a narrow phone the four buttons under the board share the row equally, so the last one never runs off the edge.

What's left of Check and Suggest on a board that limits them shows as a small brass badge on each button's corner, not as "(2)" in the label, which ran out of four buttons to a row.

Held sideways (README, Bigger screens), the board takes the left column under the header, its cells up to 100px rather than a phone's 84 (`fitLayout`), and the dock stands beside it, the keys at the bottom as tall as the column lets them be; on an upright tablet the board, the keys and their limits grow with the page (`scale()` from `wide.js`).

A whole field's dashed cage is one outline round all its cells at every size (October 2026). It was traced in pixels, and where a cell came out a fractional size (as on the larger iPhones and on iPads, upright and sideways) the edge of the bridge between two cells and the edge of the next cell could differ in the last decimal place, and the outline then split into separate loops at the hairline between them, a dashed line through the middle of the field (18 of 132 cages, over six boards at twelve phone and tablet sizes). It is now traced on whole numbers, each cell two units from the next, and placed in pixels only at the end (`slate-cage.js`); `tests/slate.mjs` draws seven field shapes at fractional sizes and finds one loop each. Phones whose cages were whole draw them exactly as before.
