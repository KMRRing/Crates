# Guandan
Guan Dan (掼蛋), the partnership climbing game from Jiangsu, by the official competition rules (《竞技掼蛋竞赛规则（试行）》, 2022): you and a computer partner across the table (Lin) against Wei and Fang, or, together, you and your partner against them. Two decks, 27 cards each, play passing to the right. Each side has a level, 2 to the ace; a hand is played at the level of the side that won the last, and its level cards rank above the ace and below the jokers; the two hearts of the level are wild (★), standing for any card but a joker in any play, and alone are just a level card. The plays: a single, a pair, three alike, a full house, a straight of exactly five, three pairs in a row, two triples in a row (the ace low or high, never round the corner; a level card counts as itself in a sequence); bombs beat them all: four alike, five, a straight flush, six to ten alike, and the four jokers on top. When everyone else passes, the last to play leads; if he has just gone out, his partner leads (接风). The side first out rises three levels if its second player is out second, two if third, one if last. From the second hand the last out pays the first his highest card (never a wild) and gets back any card of 10 or lower; after a one-two finish both losers pay, the higher card to the first out (alike: each pays the player before him); holding both big jokers, the payers pay nothing and the first out leads. A match is six hands (the higher level wins; another hand if they're level), or played to the ace, which a side passes by winning a hand at its own ace with its partner not last (Menu).

`tribute-engine.js` is the referee: the cards (a card is its id, 0–107), every reading of a set of cards (more than one when the wilds allow it), how plays compare, the trick, 接风, tribute and its return, the result and the next hand. `tests/tribute.mjs` checks every kind of play and the illegal ones (two pairs in a row, six in a straight, a small and a big joker as a pair), the wilds, the bomb ladder, the trick and the partner's lead, single and double tribute, resistance and returns, the levels and passing the ace; then plays whole matches of computer players (every card accounted for after every play) and puts them against greedy players.

The computer players (`tribute-bot.js`, thinking in `tribute-worker.js`) see their own cards, every play and pass, the counts and the tribute, and remember every card played. They play at two strengths (Menu → Computer players): Hard, the default, and Medium.

A hand is organised into the plays that cost it the fewest leads. Each play is worth the chance it gets made without a lead of its own (it holds when led, or goes on someone else's play): a low single nearly always needs one, a pair of aces often doesn't, the big joker almost never does; a bomb counts below nothing, since it wins the lead back. The split tries sequences in every combination up to four and straight flushes first, places the wilds where they cost least and has triples carry the cheapest pairs, and keeps the arrangement with the smallest total. That one measure gives an expert's habits without a rule for each: a wild makes the weak triple a bomb and leaves the strong one its full house (444★ and AAA55, not AAA★ and 44455); it fills the gap in a straight that would otherwise leave four singles; a level card goes into a sequence only when that saves plays; and a bomb is broken for a straight only when keeping it would leave more loose cards than a bomb is worth (一火保两单: 3 5678 9999 is played as 56789, 999 and 3; with one stray card it stays a bomb). Until October 2026 a hand was split by its number of plays alone, a bomb was never broken for a sequence, and the level cards went into runs as readily as threes.

Hard reads the table: how likely a play is to hold, reckoned for each opponent from the cards he could hold and how many he holds (below, Counting what's still out) and from the kinds he has passed on from the other side (a pass on your pair of 8s most likely means no higher pair). Leading: the play that sheds the most of what the hand still needs, longer ones first, shaped by the next opponent's count (京报: the kinds a hand of nine, seven or five cards can't easily follow), more cards than a short opponent holds (he can neither follow nor bomb), low singles and pairs for a partner close to out, and the kinds the opponents have shown they lack, a pair or a single taken out of a bigger combination when the table favours it; with two plays left, a bomb is kept to win the lead back and a sure play goes first. Following an opponent, it contests the trick with the play that costs the hand least, even one that breaks a combination, and keeps its controls from small plays early: the lead is worth more than a little of the hand's shape, and that alone made more than half a level a hand of difference against beating only when it cost nothing. A short opponent after it is blocked with a card he can't beat (顶牌); it bombs by the count of whoever played (炸五不炸四，打七不打八: at five he could go out on a full house or a straight, at four his four may be a bomb of his own, at seven he's two plays from out), or when the bomb and one more play take it out, and leaves the trick to its partner when he may well beat it without one. Its partner's play stands (让牌), unless taking it blocks an opponent about to go out, or holds and leaves its hand able to run out. Late in a hand (anyone within ten cards of out, or sixty left in all) Hard looks ahead: each choice worth considering, singles and pairs taken from combinations among them, is tried in up to 32 deals of the unseen cards that fit what the table has shown (each player's count; the tribute and return cards where they are; nothing above a payer's tribute card in his hand; the big jokers with the payers who resisted; deals where someone passed on a play that would have taken him out weighed down), the rest of the hand played out by these rules and scored by what it does to the levels (a rise capped at the ace, the ace passed winning the match, the tribute owed next hand); another play replaces the rules' choice only when it's clearly better over the same deals. Its choices vary at random only among plays it reckons alike. Medium leaves out the reading of the table, the blocking, the breaking of combinations to lead and the look-ahead, bombs by a plainer rule (an opponent down to seven, but not four), is slower to contest a trick, and now and then misjudges one or wanders among its leads.

In the arena (`tools/tribute-arena.mjs [deals] [greedy | medium | hard | other-bot.js]`, with ARENA_SKILL and ARENA_SEARCH for the bot's own strength and look-ahead; each deal played twice with the sides swapped, every second one from tribute at a random level) Hard with its look-ahead comes out about three quarters of a level a hand ahead of the players before October 2026 with theirs (its side out first in five hands in eight, one-two in a third of them against their one in seven), and about half a level ahead of Medium; its rules alone are worth about a level a hand against the old players' rules, and its look-ahead about a third of a level more than its rules. Medium is about half a level a hand stronger than the old players' rules; against greedy players either strength's side goes out first in nineteen hands in twenty. `tests/tribute.mjs` checks the organisation (the wild on the weak triple, the bomb broken for a straight or kept, the wild in a gap) and the judgement at both strengths (more cards than a short opponent holds, the lowest single for a partner on one, the bomb kept for the last play, the bomb at five and not four, the partner's pair left to stand, and Hard's 顶牌), and plays Hard against Medium. The return: the card that costs the hand least, low, from a rank held more than once (less likely to complete the payer's bomb) and, for Hard, not a 5 or a 10 (every straight needs one); to a partner, the most useful card the hand can spare.

Hint, at any decision (unless the coach is Off, below): Hard's play in your seat (whatever the strength of the others), its cards picked for you, its reason, and what told against the next best ("Pass: keep your bomb; West has 4 left, and four may be a bomb of his own. Next best: Bomb with four 4s; against it: spends your only bomb, your one sure way back to the lead."); tap again for the other plays it weighed, each with what speaks for it and against it. Why? lays them side by side: for every play, the plays your hand would need after it, its chance of holding (no opponent beating it except with a bomb, as the computer's players play: below), late in a hand what the look-ahead made of it (levels to your side on average), and its case, + for and − against, in the terms the computer weighed it: what it sheds or breaks, the controls it spends, the opponents' counts (京报, a short opponent who can't follow, one who could go out on it), your partner's count, the kinds the opponents have passed on, 顶牌, 火不炸四, and for a beat that seldom stands the price it sets the opponent after you (he must beat your J, not the 8). Pick takes a play's cards (for a day after the puzzle rush came in, Why? didn't open from the hint: it took your tap for the list of plays). While a hint stands (October 2026), its cards still the ones picked, your cards are set out as the coach plans them: its play first, then a column for each play it counts on to go out with the rest of your hand (in a row, held sideways, a group each), every other one shaded. That's the split behind "plays to go out after it", the hand organised in a vacuum into the plays that cost it fewest leads, so a strong combination broken for a hint shows where its cards went and what the rest then makes ("Your cards as it plans them: this, then 9 plays to go out"); tap Hint again and the next play's plan replaces it, pick a card yourself and your cards stand by rank again. Why? shows the same in small cards: your hand as it stands, as the coach splits it, and under each play what the rest makes after it (Then); after a check, from your cards as they were before your move. The hint's look-ahead runs a little longer than a computer player's (up to 0.7 s), since it waits on your tap. Your cards stand in columns by rank, the strongest on the left (jokers, the wilds, the level, the ace…), each showing its whole corner, the rank and the suit under it (until October 2026 a card behind another showed its rank alone, so a straight flush, five ranks in a row in one suit, couldn't be seen in the hand; a column of five or more of a rank draws its corners a little smaller, so it doesn't take the table's room); tap cards to pick them, and Play shows what they make before you play it; a return dims the cards you can't give. The card counter (Menu) shows how many of each rank you haven't seen. The computer players move at the pace you choose and stand still while the menu or the rules are open. Your match is kept as you leave it (`tribute:run`); the games screen shows your streak of matches won. Together, you and your partner are always one side, across the table at seats 0 and 2 (the first to sit down at 0), against Wei and Fang, each seeing only your own cards; the device that sat down first moves Wei and Fang while its player is there, and the other steps in only when a move hasn't come for four seconds (its partner's page asleep, or gone): both used to work out every move and race to save it, twice the work on each phone. Each move is a transaction that checks the hand hasn't moved on, and the room keeps each seat's cards under its own key so a seat with none doesn't shift the others. Your own play shows at once: the room's answer is a round trip away, a large part of a second from far enough, and only you move your seat, so it comes back as you made it or not at all (then the table is put back as the room has it). A seat kept for your partner until they sit down shows their name, and the news speaks to you as you ("You hold the turned-up 7♠, and lead"), not by your name. Until October 2026 a match together stopped at its first move: the room's copy of a hand was tidied by giving every log entry a list of cards, undefined for those that aren't plays, and the database refuses a whole write with undefined anywhere in it, so every move came back "Couldn't save that". `fromRoom` and `toRoom` in `tribute-engine.js` read and write the room's copy, and `tests/tribute.mjs` plays whole matches through a model of the database, move by move: every write one it accepts, every state back exactly as written. Their strength is the room's (the device that deals the table brings its own setting, and either of you can change it in the menu), so both devices move them alike. Each request you say Play to deals a new match; your solo match isn't dealt or played on while you sit down, and Back to solo finds it as you left it. The pair's record counts a match played to its end, as a team. A party of three or four (README, Party) plays one match (October 2026): the first two to sit down are partners, across from each other as a pair would be, the others the other side, in the seats between, the computer taking any seat nobody has; the device of the first to sit down who's here moves the computer, and the menu says who's with whom ("You and Mei against Bo and Ana").

The coach (Menu → Coach, October 2026) speaks in one of four ways, as Hong's does. Hints, the default and what there was before: nothing unless you ask, Hint at any decision (above). Check: Hint too, and after each move of yours it would have made otherwise, its word on it with its label in oxblood, so it isn't taken for advice: what it would have played, how much worse it reckons yours, and the two reasons that cost most ("Your K: about 0.7 levels a hand worse than the coach's pass. Breaks a pair of Ks, so your hand then needs 3 plays to go out, not 2; may be beaten in turn (it stands about 20%)."). Always: the same, and at each of your decisions its play and why without your asking, its cards ringed in your hand (Hint picks them for you). Off: nothing, and no Hint. How much worse is in the coach's own two measures. Its play and yours are played out in the same 80 deals of the cards you can't see (the rest of the hand by Hard's rules, scored by what it does to the levels, as the look-ahead does), and the difference is said in levels a hand when the deals can tell: at least a tenth of a level, and twice its standard error clear (the standard error: how far an average over that many deals may stray by chance). Otherwise it's said in plays to go out, what your hand needs after yours against after its, split as the computer would ("Your three 3s: a play further from out than the coach's full house, 3s (6 to go, against 5). Breaks a full house, 3s."). Early in a hand that's nearly always the measure: a deal's outcome varies so much then (a standard deviation of over two levels between the two plays' results) that 80 deals can't tell a few tenths of a level apart, and the rules decide by the plays there anyway. A move as good by those measures counts as agreeing: no further from out, or within a tenth of a level of its own with the standard error counted in. A return is measured by the plays alone, since the deals of a whole hand couldn't tell one card's worth. The word stays until a move of yours it agrees with. Why? sets its play and yours side by side, its first and yours next, each with its average over the check's deals (the look-ahead's own numbers, from other deals, are left out so the two never disagree); and the hand's result ends with Your play: how often you and the coach agreed, and the three moves that cost most, in the same words. The check works from the table as it was before your move, in a worker of its own (it takes up to three seconds, and the computer players, in theirs, never wait on it), and is kept in the run (`tribute:run`), so a reload finds its word where it was. A coach that speaks unasked keeps its room under your cards (four lines on a phone, three on one under 700 pixels tall, a longer word scrolling inside it), so the table doesn't change size as it speaks and falls quiet. `tests/tribute.mjs` checks it on a puzzle's worst play (a gap in levels, within the deals' error of the puzzle's own), on the coach's own play (nothing said) and early in a hand (said in plays).

Counting what's still out (October 2026). The hint counts every card: every play, your hand, and what the tribute showed (a card paid or given back is with the player who got it, a payer holds nothing above the card he paid, wilds aside, and the big jokers are with the payers who resisted). Each other player is reckoned on his own, with the cards known to be his and a share of the rest by how many he holds, so the ace you paid West counts against your K in his hand only, and a payer whose tribute card was a Q can't beat it at all; before, the opponents shared one pool, and a card known to be one's counted against both. Bombs are counted the same way: four or more alike with the wilds standing in, straight flushes with a gap or two the wilds can fill, the four jokers. Where the count rules something out, and only then, the hint says so outright: "nothing left out there can beat it, not even a bomb" (with why: no rank has four cards left, the four jokers aren't all out there, no suit has five in a row left), "counting what's gone his four can't be a bomb: he needs two plays more", "no bigger bomb can be made"; "nothing unseen beats it but a bomb" only when no card that could beat it can be with them (it was said from 97%, and "can't follow it" from 90%). Why? opens with the count: the jokers, wilds, level cards, aces and kings still out (not in your hand, not played), and the chance one of the opponents holds a bomb, or why none can be left.

The chances are the computer players' own, since they're the opponents. The count alone takes the unseen cards as dealt at random among the players with room for them, and against what the players really held it was far out, always the same way: they keep their bombs, pairs and high cards while they shed the rest, and they don't beat everything they could. Where it gave a player of four to eight cards 1% of holding a bomb, he held one a third of the time; a full house it gave 1% of standing stood 41% of the time against Hard, since breaking a hand to beat it costs more than the trick. So each chance shown is the count as their play bears it out, by logistic regression (the log-odds of what happened fitted as a weighted sum of the count's log-odds and a few facts of the position) on what they held or did in their own play. That one opponent holds a bomb: by the log of his count and, for a bomb bigger than yours, its tier, from 89,000 positions in 4,500 hands of Hard's rules; fitted to two thirds of them, it was within 1 or 2 points on average on the rest where the count was 12 to 17 out, and within 2 or 3 against Hard with its look-ahead and against Medium. That either does: a fit of its own, since theirs aren't independent (taken as independent, 74% meant 59%). Holds, the chance no opponent beats a play except with a bomb: by the kind of play and whether it's led, from 8,000 plays against Hard (with its look-ahead, as it plays) and 55,000 against Medium, within about a point on other hands where the count was 16 and 23 out, so the numbers follow the strength you play against. What the count makes certain stays certain, and nothing less shows above 99%. The computer players still decide by the count, as the arena tuned them; the fitted chances are what you're shown. In the arena, reckoning each opponent on his own made no measurable difference to their play (+0.007 levels a hand over 6,000 hands, a standard error of 0.006), and a rule counting the bombs that could stop a run-out lost a little (−0.035 ± 0.02), so they don't use it. `tools/tribute-chances.mjs` plays the hands, records what happened and prints the fitted numbers with how close they come on games held out of the fit (`bombs`, about five minutes; `stands medium`, two; `stands hard`, half an hour, since Hard answers with its look-ahead): run it again whenever the computer players change.

Puzzle rush (Menu, October 2026): find the best play in late positions from the computer players' own hands, as many as you can, with every game's rush settings (README, Puzzle rush: Random or Today's; 3 min, 5 min or Survival; three misses end the run; until the settings were made one, five minutes and no choice). A puzzle is kept only when one play is clearly best. The plays worth weighing (Hard's rules without the look-ahead, Medium's choice, a greedy player's, and the others the hint weighs: up to six different answers) are each tried in deals of the cards the player can't see, fitted to what the table has shown, the rest of the hand played out and scored by what it does to the levels, in batches of 30 deals until one play beats every other by 0.3 levels a hand or more, four standard errors clear (a standard error: how far an average over that many deals may stray by chance), at most 300 deals; about one decision in fourteen passes. The rating comes from who would have got it wrong (Hard's rules, Medium, the greedy player) and how close the call; a run starts easy and rises, four at each rating, those seen lately last. A play is the same answer whatever cards of its ranks make it (a pair of 9s is a pair of 9s in any suits); one the puzzle never weighed is tried against the best in 80 deals of its own while you wait, and counts if it comes within a tenth of a level. The card counter shows in a puzzle whatever its setting, since what's still out is part of the question. The verdict: Best, As good, or Not the best, with what each did in levels a hand, what tells against yours and for the best; Why? sets the plays side by side, the best first and yours next, in the hint's terms, and the best's cards are picked in your hand. The bank (`tribute-puzzles.js`, 193 puzzles from 320 deals, every other one a later hand from tribute at a random level) is found by `tools/tribute-puzzles.mjs <first deal> <deals> <out.jsonl>`, then `merge` to write it (two at once on different deals use both cores: 160 deals each took under half an hour); a puzzle is the deal and the moves before it (`tribute-rush.js`), replayed through the engine. `tests/tribute.mjs` replays every puzzle and checks that the plays it weighed are legal there and different answers, and that the best is the one worth most. The run is kept as it goes (`tribute:rush`), your bests by clock in `tribute:rushBest` (a lone best kept before the clocks counts as a five-minute run's), today's runs in `tribute:rushDaily`, the run and clock last started in `tribute:rushNext`; while the rush is on, the menu is the rush's (Start a run, the run's results, Back to the match, the run and the clock, your bests by clock), and the match's About gives the bests in a line. Your match waits as you left it, and Back to the match returns to it. It's solo only.

Readings chosen where that left room, to be checked by playing: the plane tilts 20 degrees, a tablet's larger sizes come from the root's rem; the board colours are fixed (a deep blue, deeper at night) rather than a theme's, since Office uses its brass for text; a play is drawn as large as the middle's height lets a card be, the cards drawn closer together first when it is too long and smaller only then (a bomb of ten); and a short upright phone (under 640 pixels high) gives the plane's sides and bottom to the plates. What can be worked out without a page, where a play's cards lie and how large they're drawn, is `arrange`, `fitPlay`, `widthOf` and `slotOf`, tested in `tests/tribute.mjs`.
