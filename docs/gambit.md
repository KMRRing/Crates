# Gambit
Chess against Stockfish at a strength you choose, two players on one phone, or a match with your partner (October 2026, beside River: the open engines being what they are, a game against a real one, at any strength, on the phone). The rules are FIDE's Laws of Chess as chess.js has them (`vendor/chess.js`, BSD, already in the app for the chess puzzles, once a game of their own): castling, en passant, promotion to any piece (a pawn reaching the last rank offers queen, rook, bishop and knight in a column over the square). A game ends by checkmate or resignation, or drawn by stalemate, by the same position a third time, by fifty moves each without a capture or a pawn move, or with too little left to mate; the draws are declared at once, without a claim, as online play does. `gambit-rules.js` keeps a game as plain JSON, the same shape as River's (the start, the moves in the engine's words, the result, who plays White), changed only by `act()`.

The board is the puzzle rush's (`chess-board.js`, its parchment and bronze squares, the cburnett pieces of `chess-pieces.js`), which gains `mountBoard` beside the rush's `mountPuzzle` (left as it was): a whole game from White's side or Black's, tap or drag with the legal targets shown, a second tap putting a piece down, the last move marked and sliding in, a king in check in red, a hint's arrow. Menu, Coordinates on the board, turns the files and ranks off. Over and under it, each side's seat: its king on a light disc, its name (with the computer's level and rating), "thinking" while the engine searches, the pieces it has taken and its lead in material (pawn 1, knight and bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9). Your seat says whose move it is, check, the hint, or the opening by its ECO code (the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings' index, A00 to E99) and its name ("C42 Petrov's Defence"), from a small table of Gambit's own (`gambit-openings.js`, 132 positions), matched by position so a transposition is named as its usual order is (1. Nf3 d5 2. d4 is D02). The moves run along a row in standard algebraic notation.

Hint shows Stockfish's strongest moves for the side to move as arrows, with its view of the position in words and pawns ("Hint: Nf3 or d4 · better for you, about a pawn (+1.1)"; level, a little better, better, much better, winning, or mates in n). Take back undoes to your last move (with the computer, its reply goes too). At the end, a sheet says who won and how, the opening and, against the computer, the strongest level you've beaten, then reviews the game: each position searched once at full strength (60,000 positions each), your three costliest moves listed by how many pawns each gave away by the engine's reckoning (a missed or allowed mate counts as ten), with the better move ("2. g4: about the game given away; better was e3."). The review is kept with the game, so it isn't searched again after a reload.

The coach (Menu, Coach, October 2026) is River's (README, River): Hints, the default, the strongest moves as arrows when you ask (the best, and any within a fifth of a pawn of it); Check, Hint too and a word under your seat after a move of yours that gave a good deal away, until your next ("3. Nxe5?? gives away about 3.7 pawns; better was Bb5, d4 or Bc4"), the move marked ? or ?? in the list, and Show to see the position before it with the coach's moves in green and yours in oxblood; Always, the same and the strongest moves as arrows at each of yours unasked; Off, nothing and no Hint. A mistake is a fall of 0.2 in winning chances and a blunder 0.3 (Lichess's curve and marks), so three pawns given away a rook up is no word; a move that lets in a mate or misses one says so. Each position is analysed once (the best three moves, MultiPV 3, on 400,000 positions), shared by the hint, the advice and the check, and a move outside the three is searched on its own (200,000). `tests/gambit.mjs` checks the judgement (the marks, a won game left alone, mates) and, with the engine, the Scholar's mate trap: after 1. e4 e5 2. Qh5 Nc6 3. Bc4, ...Nf6?? lets White mate in one, ...g6 is no word.

The computer player is Stockfish 19 as Nathan Rugg's stockfish.js builds it for Chess.com (npm `stockfish@19.0.1`, GPL v3): the lite single-threaded build, `vendor/stockfish/stockfish-19-lite-single.js` and its `.wasm`, shipped unchanged (their SHA-256, the source's address and the licence are in `vendor/stockfish/NOTE.txt`). Single-threaded because the threaded builds need SharedArrayBuffer, which needs cross-origin isolation headers GitHub Pages can't send; lite because its small NNUE net keeps it at 1.9 MB, where the full build is 94 MB, and it still plays far beyond any club player. It runs in a worker of its own, spoken to in UCI by `uci.js`, loaded only when a game against it needs it (the rete shows while it starts), and is kept offline (`tools/offline.mjs` keeps every `vendor/*/*.wasm`, since an engine's script fetches its WebAssembly by a path it builds). The tests run the same script in Node as a child process.

Eight levels (`gambit-levels.js`), named by rough rating: Beginner 700, Novice 1,100, Casual 1,300, Club 1,600, Strong club 1,900, Expert 2,200, Master 2,500 and Full strength 3,000; a new game starts at Casual. Casual to Master are Stockfish's own limiter (UCI_LimitStrength, with UCI_Elo at its floor of 1,320, then 1,600, 1,900, 2,200 and 2,500), rated as Stockfish rates it: its developers calibrated it against other engines at a minute a game, on the CCRL list's scale. It chooses at a shallow depth among its best four moves with a random push that grows as the rating falls, so it errs as a weaker player does. Below that floor a level draws among the engine's best moves after a tiny search, each weighted e^(−loss/T) by the centipawns it gives up against the best (`choose()` in `uci.js`): Beginner among its 12 best after 600 positions at T = 300, Novice among 10 after 1,000 at T = 140. So Beginner, offered a knight for nothing beside eleven quiet moves, more often plays one of those (a queen it takes about two times in three), and Novice takes the knight half the time or more and the queen nearly always. Full strength is the engine's own move after 800,000 positions, a second or two on a phone. Every level searches a set number of positions, not for a set time, so it plays alike on a slow phone and a fast one.

How they were measured: `tools/engine-levels.mjs` plays matches in Node, the page's own engine script and `uci.js` playing both sides, each game opened with two random sensible moves and the colours alternating. 300 games among the eight levels and seven candidates tried on the way (ten a pairing, twenty for some) were fitted together to one scale by maximum likelihood under the Elo model (`node tools/engine-levels.mjs fit`; the games are kept in `tools/engine-levels-gambit.jsonl`), placed by the limiter's floor at 1,320. Beginner came out at 720 ± 110 (drawing among 16 or 20 moves at a higher T was no weaker: 710 to 730) and Novice at 1,070 ± 70; the limiter at 1,530 ± 130, 1,850 ± 200, 1,950 ± 230 and 2,480 ± 340 for its 1,600, 1,900, 2,200 and 2,500, its own ratings within the errors (1,320 to 2,500 measured 1,160 of the 1,180 it claims); Full strength won all ten of its games against Master, so it is at least about 3,000. These are engine ratings: set against people's they are rough (an engine's mistakes aren't a person's), which is all a name like Club means.

Your game is kept as it changes (`gambit:game`: the moves, who plays which side, the level, a hint shown, the review), so a reload, another of your devices or a partner watching sees it as it stands; `gambit:opts` keeps the menu's choices, `gambit:best` the strongest level beaten (the tile's "Best") and `gambit:record` your wins, draws and losses at each level, a game counted once, as it first ends (as River's). The computer's move waits while the menu, the Rules or the games screen is open, and comes no sooner than 0.7 seconds after yours. The page is laid out as River's: upright, the computer's seat, the board the width of the phone, your seat, the moves in a row, Take back and Hint (New game once it's over); sideways, the board on the left with all the height and the rest beside it, the buttons low. Together, as River: colours by lot, White first, each device moving its own side from its own side of the board, Resign in the menu, the result to the pair's record. `tests/gambit.mjs` checks the moves through `act()` (castling, en passant, promotion and underpromotion), every ending, the state as the room keeps it, the opening table (every line legal, no position named twice, transpositions), whole games between levels move by move through the database's round trip, the stronger levels beating the weaker, and the engine kept offline.

The puzzle rush (Menu, Puzzle rush; until October 2026 a game of its own, Rush, with its own tile, then folded into Gambit as River's rush is in River): chess tactics, one to four moves, as many as you can, with the settings every game's rush shares (README, Puzzle rush): Random or Today's; 3 min, 5 min or Survival; three misses end the run. It opens straight onto an easy warm-up puzzle; solving it starts the clock, and it counts for nothing (not the score, not the rating). The board shows the position from your side, the opponent's move plays after a beat, and you move by dragging a piece or by tapping it and then a square, with the legal targets shown; every move has to be the puzzle's, except that any checkmate is accepted where the solution mates. Premoves: tap your next move while the opponent is replying and it plays the instant the reply lands, if it's legal then, and is judged like any other move (the squares show purple while it waits). After a miss the board shows your move, then the wanted one as its two squares, yellow where it starts and green where it lands, and the line is written over the board before the next puzzle; after each puzzle, its rating and Lichess's themes for it. Your rating decides what you're served: a running rating across every run, Elo-style against each puzzle's rating, that moves fast for your first twenty puzzles and slowly after, with gains halving at every miss in a run so a careless run can't climb; it shows by the count (with a ? while it's settling) and can be reset from the menu. A run starts about 250 below your rating and climbs 40 a solve to about 150 above it (a miss doesn't raise it); today's run ignores ratings so everyone gets the same puzzles, from 800 and 60 more a solve. The results give the count, accuracy, your rating and its change over the run, your best for the clock (or today's), the hardest solved, and every miss with its line and a link to the puzzle on Lichess; The game and Again under them. A miss goes to the pile, and Deck brings it back on the board. The menu, the rules and the games screen stop the clock; a timed run ends if the app goes to the background, or when you go back to the game (you're asked), while a survival run, or the warm-up, waits where it was, after a reload too.

It's a page of its own (`rush.html`, `rush.js`, `rush.css`) under Gambit's header, its logo and its name, not a part of `gambit.html` as River's rush is of River's page: so it keeps the identity it had as a game, its keys (`rush:`), what your partner sees you playing ("Puzzle rush"), and the puzzle battle's requests and Party's, all of which go by a page's name. Its tile is gone from the games screen (`apps.js` keeps an entry for it without one, for its name and Gambit's logo); The game (under the board, and in the menu as Back to the game) goes back to `gambit.html`, where your game waits as it was. Gambit's About gives its bests in a line, starting Gambit over (Settings) starts its rush over too, and Gambit's Coordinates on the board holds for its board as for the game's, from either menu (`gambit:opts`). Upright, the count, the clock and the strikes are over the board, what to do above it and what the puzzle was under it, the buttons low; held sideways, the board takes the left as tall as the screen allows and the rest stands beside it, the buttons low, as River's rush does. The board's look is `chess-board.css`, shared with Gambit's game and Deck's chess cards.

The bank is 20,000 puzzles from the Lichess puzzle database (CC0), popular ones with one to four moves to find, 2,000 in each of ten rating bands from 600 to 2,400+, in `puzzles/band-*.txt`. A run reads a band the first time it climbs into it (about 200 KB each, so a run starts in a second). Every band is kept on the device with the rest of the app (2 MB, downloaded once, since an update fetches only files that changed), so the rush plays offline from the start; until October 2026 a band came down only when a run first reached it, so offline a run couldn't climb past what had been played online. The rules are chess.js (BSD, in `vendor/`) and the pieces the cburnett set by Colin M. L. Burnett (CC BY-SA 3.0), via `chess-board.js`.

The run is kept after every move, yours or the opponent's: the puzzle, how far into it, the count, the strikes, the clock and how much of the puzzle's five seconds of grace is used (`mountPuzzle` in `chess-board.js` picks a puzzle up part-way, replaying the moves made). A partner watching sees the board as you play it and the clock running on; a survival run, or the warm-up, comes back after a reload where it was. (The wanted move's square was once marked "hint", which picked up the suite's clue-button style, a 30-pixel box, and grew far past the board; it's "want" now.)

Together, a puzzle battle (October 2026): asked for from the partner sheet while you're in the rush, or started from a Party for up to six (Party lists it as Puzzle rush). Everyone at the table gets the same puzzles in the same order, chosen by their number in the run rather than by anyone's rating (from about 650, 60 more a puzzle), on the match's clock (3 min, 5 min or Survival; the menu's Mode starts a new match on another, for everyone). There's no warm-up: the countdown is the start, and every clock runs from the start the table shares, through the menu or a phone put down (a battle's clock doesn't stop). The others' counts show under yours, where your rating was. Most solved wins, then fewer misses; then, in survival, the quicker (on the clock, the same count and misses is a draw). A battle counts for your rating and the pile as any puzzle does, but not for your bests or today's, and your solo run waits where it was. A reload picks a battle up where it was, the clock as the table has it; a seat taken over on another device deals again the puzzles before the one it was on, unplayed, so it goes on with the same puzzles as everyone else. The lobby, the countdown and the result are the suite's (README, Head to head).
