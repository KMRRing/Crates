# Nonogram
A picture hidden in a grid (October 2026): each row and column carries its clue, the runs of filled cells along it in order ("3 1 2" is three filled, a gap, one, a gap, two), and the clues together allow exactly one picture. The pictures are the knowledge base's, so finishing one is also a question: whose outline, whose flag, which lake. Four kinds, chosen in the menu (Pictures: Countries, Flags, Lakes, Regions or All, one of): a country's outline (Chart's own shapes, `chart-countries.js`, Natural Earth at 1:110m, 168 countries), its flag in colour (195 flags), a lake (Chart's 34, from Lake Geneva to the Aral Sea), and a region, the part of a country (31 October 2026; below). In a flag every run has its colour too, shown on the clue as a chip of that colour, and the usual colour rules hold: two runs of different colours may touch, two of the same colour need a gap. With Pictures on All, Settings' subjects choose what is dealt (3 November 2026, when Nonogram became a Knowledge game): `TOPIC_OF.nonogram` in `topics.js` puts countries, flags and regions under Geography and lakes under Nature, and a kind whose subject is off is left out of the mix; a kind chosen in the menu is always dealt. A region narrows any of them (Worldwide or Chart's seven regions, one of), and the size is the menu's Size: Small, Medium or Large, about 100, 225 or 400 cells, the grid taking the picture's own shape (a 15 × 15 for a square country, 20 × 10 for a long flag), never longer than 14, 20 or 26 cells on a side nor shorter than 5, 7 or 9. A country too long and thin for any size (Chile, the Gambia, Indonesia's archipelago) or too scattered to be a picture (the Solomon Islands) isn't dealt.

Regions (31 October 2026; Korbi: "nonogram needs more maps, always giving me the same one"). 422 parts of twenty countries whose divisions are worth knowing: Switzerland's 26 cantons, Germany's 16 Länder, Austria's 9 states, Italy's 20 regions, France's 13, Spain's 15 autonomous communities, the 12 Dutch provinces, Belgium's 11, Poland's 16 voivodeships (provinces), Estonia's 15 counties, the United States' 48 states, Canada's 13, Mexico's 32, Brazil's 27, Argentina's 23, Australia's 8, Japan's 46 prefectures, China's 31, India's 32 and South Africa's 9. They are Natural Earth's 1:10m admin-1 boundaries (public domain, the source of Chart's country outlines) in `nonogram-regions.js`, 340 KB, which `tools/nonogram-regions.mjs` writes from the download (its header says how): each unit simplified to 1.2% of the frame it is drawn in (a picture is at most 26 cells across, so more would never show), pieces under 0.4% of it dropped. Natural Earth gives Italy, France and Spain as 110 provinces, 101 departments and 52 provinces, so those are merged into the regions people name (by the `region` it gives each, with English names for them: Piedmont, Brittany, Andalusia). The question names the country and what its parts are called ("Which canton of Switzerland is this?", "Which state of the United States is this?"), and the names offered as you type are that country's own, so a Swiss canton is never one of a few hundred; a region answers to its English name and its own (Genève, Bayern, Québec). The Region menu filters by the country's continent. Hawaii, the Canary and Balearic Islands, the Andaman Islands and Nagasaki are an island chain, a scatter of specks rather than a picture, and Tennessee is too long and thin for any size, so they are left out; Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh are too, their borders a dispute a puzzle game need not draw. Regions of a country with islands (Okinawa, Nunavut) are drawn with their islands together: a country's picture takes a piece when its centre is within a quarter of the picture's diagonal, which would leave Okinawa as its one big island, so each unit carries the narrowest `reach` (a quarter, a half, one or two diagonals) that takes in nine tenths of its land. A miss goes to the pile as a picture without a knowledge-base entity yet (`about` is empty), so no other game asks about it; Deck's card offers the same country's other regions as the wrong answers.

How a picture is made (`nonogram-pictures.js`, with the choice of pieces in `nonogram-land.js`, which `tools/nonogram-regions.mjs` uses too). A country is drawn in Mercator, as every map draws it, and only its main land: its largest piece and whatever lies near it (a piece joins when its centre is within a quarter of the picture's diagonal of what has joined), so France comes without French Guiana and the United States without Alaska and Hawaii, while Indonesia's or Japan's islands, near one another, stay; pieces cut at the antimeridian (Russia's Chukotka) are moved round to their side. Each cell is sampled at 36 points, scanline by scanline, inside by the even–odd rule (Lesotho is a hole in South Africa), and filled when the land covers half of it; rows and columns left empty at the edges (a small island stretching the frame) are cut off. The knowledge base has flags only as emoji, which every phone draws its own way, so each flag is described in `nonogram-flags.js` as the shapes it's made of (stripes and their proportions, crosses, discs, stars, crescents, triangles at the hoist, diagonal bands, the Union Jack in a canton, an emblem as the blot of colour it makes at this size) and painted cell by cell, each cell the colour most of its 25 samples have; of the grids near the size's, the one whose edges fall most cleanly between cells is taken (thirteen American stripes in thirteen rows). A flag's commonest colour is its blank, so what's left to draw is its pattern in the other colours, and the finished flag shows the blank's colour again; Nepal's pennants stand on nothing at all. Every flag was checked against its design at Medium and Large; Afghanistan's and Syria's are left out, since they changed lately and phones still disagree about them, and Chad's and Romania's (Monaco's and Indonesia's) are the same picture, so either name is right for both.

Exactly one answer, reached by reasoning (`nonogram-engine.js`). A search counts the answers the clues allow, up to two: the complete line logic, then a guess on an open cell and both ways followed. A picture that has one is then solved the way a person would (below), and is dealt only if that reasoning finishes it. When the even split of the cells doesn't, the generator tries a little more and a little less of each cell (a cut of 45%, 55%, 40%, 60%, 35%, 65% instead of a half), then changes cells one at a time, each time one where two answers differ, the cells the shape half covers first, so the picture moves as little as it can (up to ten). Of the outlines about one in five needed a cut other than the half and about one in twenty a changed cell or two; a few lakes need more. A picture makes the same puzzle at a size on every device, so today's is the same for everyone and a match the same for everyone at the table (`tests/nonogram.mjs` makes the days' puzzles again in another process and compares them). Making one takes a few milliseconds, a sparse lake at Large a few hundred: the rete says so ("Drawing the picture"), and the next random picture is made in a worker while you play this one (`nonogram-worker.js`), so Next is instant.

Difficulty is how far the reasoning has to reach, measured, not the number of clues. The solver climbs a ladder the way a person does: the simple rules first (push every run as far left as it will go and as far right; a cell inside the same run both ways is filled, a cell no run can reach is blank, a line whose runs are placed is done); when they say no more, the complete line logic (every arrangement of the line's runs that fits what's known: a cell they all agree on is settled), on one line, then back to the simple rules; and only when no line says anything, a contradiction (suppose a cell is filled, follow the lines, and if they clash it's blank). It also counts the rounds of simple reasoning a puzzle needs, each worked from what the round before settled. Easy is the simple rules alone in six rounds or fewer; Medium seven to ten, or the complete line logic somewhere; Hard eleven rounds or more, or a contradiction. A puzzle that needs more than thirty contradictions is a slog and isn't dealt. Measured on every picture: countries at Medium are 49 Easy, 86 Medium and 29 Hard (Small leans easy, Large hard); flags are nearly all Easy, as colour gives so much away; lakes are mostly Hard, being thin. The menu's Difficulty is Any, Easy, Medium or Hard; where a region has nothing of a band at a size, the nearest is dealt and the line says so. The bands' cut points come from these measurements, not from play: whether Medium feels medium is for Korbi's thumb to say.

Playing. Fill (or a flag's colours, as swatches) and Cross are the tools; a tap fills or crosses one cell, a drag runs along its row or column, and the first cell decides what a stroke does: on an empty cell it fills, on one of its colour it clears, and a fill never covers a cell crossed out. Undo and Redo take whole strokes. Mistakes (Settings) are Shown, the default: a wrong fill or cross is refused, the cell shows what it really is and it costs 20 seconds; or Hidden, as on paper: nothing says you're wrong, and the picture is done when every row and column agrees with its clue. A hint (+30 s) fills in the next cell the clues force, the simplest way a person would see it, and names the line that says so and why ("from row 4: its runs pushed both ways overlap there"); with mistakes hidden and one standing, it rings the wrong cell instead. A finished line's clue greys; the row and column under your finger light up with their clues; the corner holds a thumbnail of what you've drawn so far. On a phone a Large picture's cells would be too small to hit, so the board zooms (pinch, or the zoom button, or the wheel on a computer) and pans (two fingers, or one along a clue strip), the clue strips staying put while the grid moves under them, so a cell's clues are always in sight. A pinch's first finger lands on a cell like any tap, so a mistake in the first 160 ms of a stroke waits to see whether a second finger follows before it counts. On a computer, X picks the cross, 1 to 7 a colour, Ctrl+Z undoes. The board is drawn on a canvas (`nonogram-board.js`) in the theme's colours by day and night; the finished picture sweeps in in its own colours, land green, water blue, a flag as it flies. Held sideways the board takes the left with all the height and the strip, the line and the tools stand at the right, the tools low.

The rules (October 2026, as in every classic) are a sheet of their own, from Rules in the header or The rules in the menu, in short plain prose: the aim, how a clue reads (and a flag's colours), a worked example (a run of 8 in a row of 10), the pictures and hexes, how to play here (the tools and strokes, zooming, the keys, Mistakes, the hint, Name it), the levels, the score and today's picture, its seconds taken from the engine's own so the sheet can't drift from the game. It scrolls inside itself under its pinned head, opens at its top each time, and the clock and the computer stand while it's open. With a partner's pill in the title on a narrow phone, the pill has no margin and the header's buttons close up a little, so the long name, Rules and the menu's button still fit at 390 pixels.

Name it (Settings, on by default). Before the picture is finished you may name it once: right takes off up to 30, 60 or 120 seconds (Small, Medium, Large) in proportion to how much was still to draw; wrong adds 30. Not named by the end, it asks then, for nothing off. The question comes about a second after the last cell, while the picture is drawn in; until then the result panel says "Solved" and keeps the name back (31 October 2026: it used to print the flag and the name of the picture 1.15 seconds before asking whose outline it was; a match's result sheet likewise names the picture only once you have been asked). Naming is typed, recall rather than recognition, with the names that fit what's typed offered under the box (`typing.js` forgives case, accents, a leading "the" and a slip of a letter; a place answers to what else it goes by: Czechia, Burma, Ivory Coast, the UK, Léman). One you couldn't name goes to the pile, carrying its knowledge-base entity, and comes back in Deck as a card: the picture and four names from its region; and in this game's learning mode (on, as everywhere) as a picture again, before any new one, followed by pictures of what other games found you weak on. A missed Chile in Chart can come back here as Chile's flag.

The computer (Settings): Off, Partner or Rival, at a pace (Easy, Medium, Hard). It solves the way the hints do, the simplest rule first, and is paced like a person: a while to see each step, longer for a harder rule (about 2.5, 6 and 15 seconds at Medium), then a moment for each cell it fills or crosses out; at Medium it draws Italy at Medium size in about three minutes, at Hard in about half that. As a partner it has the columns' clues and you the rows' (theirs show as shaded marks in its colour): it works from the grid you share, and when its columns say no more it waits on you, as a partner would; a picture drawn with it doesn't count for your best. As a rival it solves its own copy alongside you, the strip saying how far it has got, and the result says who was quicker (`nonogram-bot.js`).

Together, on `duel.js` (README, Head to head), from the partner sheet or a Party of up to six, two ways (the menu's Next match). Split, the default for a pair: one grid between you and the clues dealt out, the rows to one and the columns to the other (at a table of more, the rows dealt round and the columns round one seat on, so everyone reads some of each). Neither can finish alone (the tests check that the rows' clues alone never finish a picture, nor the columns'): each must say what their lines need ("my row 3 is 2 5"), and tapping a clue lights that line on the others' screens for a few seconds, to point at it. Each sees the other's cells as they're drawn (the room keeps the cells one by one, so two people drawing at once never collide); a partner away gives their lines to those still here, so nobody's sleeping phone holds the picture up; mistakes, hints and the one go at naming it are the team's, and the time runs from when the table started. The finished picture counts for the pair as a team result. Race, a party's first match: the same picture for everyone, each on their own grid, the others' progress in the line under the strip, the lowest score winning; duel.js does the lobby, the countdown and the result. Your own picture, bests and today's stay as they were.

Hexes (the menu's Grid: Squares or Hexes), the genre's other real grid: a hexagon of hexagonal cells, 91, 169 or 271 of them (5, 7 or 9 from the centre to a side, Small to Large), every cell on three lines, its row and two slants, each with a clue (`nonogram-hex.js`; `nonogram-hexboard.js` draws it, with the square board's calls). The clues stand round three of the six sides, each read toward the board as a row's is: the rows' on the left, the falling slants' (top left to bottom right) off the lower right, the rising slants' off the upper right, so none stands in another's way and a clue's first number is always the run nearest it. On hexes a country or a lake is its outline, the coast one cell wide, and the land inside fills in, lighter, when it's done. That is measured, not taste: with three clues at every cell the simple rules run through a solid shape almost at once (every country drawn filled came out Easy), while an outline, which on squares hardly ever has one answer, has exactly one on hexes for every shape that fills an eighth of the hexagon, and at Large needs real reasoning. Countries at Small are all Easy, at Medium 148 Easy and 14 Medium, at Large 84 Easy, 66 Medium and 12 Hard; lakes run the same way. The few too thin or scattered for a hexagon (Chile, Norway, Japan, Indonesia, Cuba and the Solomon Islands; long lakes such as Baikal, Tanganyika, Malawi and Nasser) aren't dealt there, nor is a flag, a rectangle, which stays on squares (on hexes the Pictures choice has no Flags; a link to one of these gives way to the run you had). The generator is the squares' (the land sampled at 19 points a cell, the cut tried from 40% to 60%, the fewest cells changed), and so are the engine's lines, solver, grading and hints, which take any lines. A drag runs along whichever of its cell's three lines the finger sets off along; the three lines under a finger light with their clues; a hint lights the line it comes from ("from falling slant 5"). The whole drawing zooms and pans, since clues in three directions can't stay pinned to the edges as a square grid's do: the zoom button stands at the lower left, clear of the slants' clues, two fingers pan, and zoomed in, the clues of the three lines under your finger show in a corner, each with its direction and a dot at the end it reads from. A match can be on hexes (the Grid when it's dealt): Split gives each of a pair every other line of each direction, three players a direction each, more round the table within each direction, and the computer as partner reads every other line; the race, bests (kept apart for hexes), naming and the pile work as on squares, and the Deck card draws the hexes as bricks, each row half a brick on, as hexes sit. Today's is always on squares.

Dealing (31 October 2026). The picture for a new game is the next in a shuffle of the pool that doesn't repeat until the pool has come round: those not dealt lately first (the page remembers every picture the game has, so even Pictures: All comes round), at random, then the rest, the one dealt longest ago first. Learning mode's picks (a picture you couldn't name, and ones about what other games found you weak on) used to go in front of everything with nothing counted against them, so one miss was dealt at every single game (Korbi: "always giving me the same one, Switzerland"; reproduced, 60 games, Switzerland 60 times). Now a game offers a pick half the time, and not one shown in the last six games, so a single miss comes back about once in eight games and several share the turns. A day's picture and a match's deal take no picks and no history, so they are exactly what they were (400 days compared). Chart, Quote, Punt and Crossword remember what they dealt in `pile.js` (`dealt`, `freshPicks`, for its No repeats mode); this game keeps its own list, `nonogram:seen`, which already syncs between devices, and could move there so every game's No repeats shares one memory.

Scoring, bests and today's. The score is your seconds on the clock, plus 20 a mistake and 30 a hint, less a right early name's credit or plus 30 for a wrong one; lower is better. Bests are kept for each kind of picture and size (`nonogram:times`, not `nonogram:best`, which the games screen would read as highest-wins). Today's picture (Picture: Today's in the menu, and the games screen's tile while it's still to play) is a country's outline, Medium, of Medium difficulty, with mistakes shown and name-it on, the same for everyone on the day (`today()`); its score counts the moment the last cell is right (`noteDayTime`), and the tile shows it as today's time.

Saved state. The picture in play is `nonogram:run`: its id and size (and `hex` on hexes; a link to it is `#p=c-france&z=medium&g=hex`), the grid (a character a cell), the strokes to undo and redo, the clock, mistakes, hints, the name, the rules it was dealt with and the rival's copy; it's saved at every stroke and every five seconds, so a reload or a partner watching sees it as it stands (the puzzle itself is made again from the picture's id and size). The menu's choices are `nonogram:settings`, the pictures dealt lately `nonogram:seen` (so they don't come round again soon), the counts `nonogram:stats`; a match's run is `nonogram:duel`. The clock and the computer stand still while the menu, the rules or the games screen is open, and while the page is away.

`tests/nonogram.mjs` checks the clues; the complete line logic against every arrangement of 2,500 random lines in one to three colours, and that the simple rules never claim more than it; the ladder's grades against what each rule alone can finish, on 400 random grids; that every picture at Small, half of them at Medium and a quarter at Large make puzzles with exactly one answer, the one drawn, reached within thirty contradictions; that a picture always makes the same puzzle, today's included in another process, and every day of a month has its medium country; the bands; dealing (not lately, the region, learning mode's picks first, the nearest band); the split (each half alone never finishes, both together always do, the lines dealt to every seat); the computer (it finishes, never a cell wrong, paced like a person, Hard twice as quick as Easy, a partner that waits); hints, checking and the score; names; the hexagon (its cells and lines, the cell under a finger, an outline with one answer reasoning reaches for a sample at every size, the bands at Large, no flags, a pair's split, the computer and hints there, a hex run saved and opened again); and a run saved as JSON and opened again. `tests/nonogram-deal.mjs` (under a second) checks the dealer: a due picture comes back, but never within six games and not at every game; with nothing due every picture is dealt once before any twice, the longest ago first, a picture in the list twice counting from its newest showing; a day's picture the same everywhere. `tests/nonogram-regions.mjs` (under a second) checks the regions' data (unique ids and names, no alias that is another's name, closed rings, under 400 KB, the counts), that every one makes a puzzle at Medium, the questions and Deck notes, the names a place answers to, and Okinawa's islands drawn together. The answer's early appearance and the new topic were checked in a browser at phone size (a finished picture's panel every 50 ms, before and after; the menu, the strip and the naming list). Two phones in one browser on the stand-in database played a split match through (each saw the other's cells, the finish counted once for the team and went to the pair's record) and a race (the winner by score, Rematch), on squares and on hexes; the computer played as partner and as rival; a hex picture was drawn by touch along all three directions, pinched, zoomed and panned, finished and named; a missed flag and a missed hex outline came back in Deck with their pictures. Its logo is a nonogram's own shape: the grid with its clue strips along the top and the left, the corner between them empty, a heart drawn on it in oxblood.

Not done: the flags' drawings and the other names places go by live in this game's own files and could move into the knowledge base as facts, for other games to draw on, and so could the 422 regions (a canton `in` Switzerland, a state `in` the United States, which Chart's pins and the learning modes could use; their pictures carry no entity yet); Deck labels the card "nonogram" until the pile's list of games names it.
