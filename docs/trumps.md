# Trumps
The trick-taking games of a Swiss, a French and a Bavarian table (October 2026), four players, against the computer or with friends each on their own phone (Invite players: seats.js runs it, the computer taking the seats nobody has). Four tiles open this page (4 November 2026, Korbi approved the split: `?game=` jass, belote, coinche or schafkopf; plain trumps.html lands on the run in progress; the menu has no Game row, the address says which; 4 November 2026, Korbi: no setting duplicates a tile or a suite setting): Jass (the Schieber, called Chibre in Romandy), Belote, Coinche (Belote's auction, a tile of its own in the Appendix) and Schafkopf, each with its own streak and record (`trumps:<tile>:streak`, `:record`) and its own run, put away under `trumps:game:<tile>` when another tile opens; Learn and Puzzle rush are Schafkopf's, the others' Learn is their rules. The table is set out the game's way round: Jass and Belote pass to the right, Schafkopf to the left; your partner (in the partnership games) sits across, named in the game's colour, and held upright the other two sit at the top corners beside them. Your hand runs along the foot, sorted by suit with the trumps on the right, each rising to the right (`sortHand` in `trumps-games.js`); a Schafkopf hand is sorted by trumps from the deal (Korbi, October 2026: "the colours, then hearts, then U/Os"), as for a Sauspiel while the game is still to be called (the Farben, the Zehner over the König; then hearts; then the Unters and the Obers, der Alte on the far right), and by the game's own trumps once a Wenz or a Solo is called; on your turn the cards you may play are lifted and the rest dimmed, and a tap on one plays it (a tap on a dimmed one says why it can't be played: follow suit, the undertrump, the called ace). A hand's score stands in the middle when it's over: solo it stays until you deal the next (Korbi, October 2026: "the human player should be able to dismiss the hand"), with Next hand under it (The result after the last) or a tap on the score itself, the table holding its clock till then; at a table with others it moves on by itself after a few seconds, so nobody's tap holds up the rest. The trick lies in the middle, each card on its player's side, the card taking it ringed; the bidding is said in bubbles by each player's place, and your bidding sits under your hand. The line across the top has the game, the contract (the trumps and who chose them, the value of a Coinche bid, the called ace of a Sauspiel) and the score.

The cards (October 2026) are as large as the screen allows, your hand the size of the trick's cards: one size for both, the largest at which the trick's cross fits the table's middle and your hand fits along its box with each card showing at least its corner (a little over a third of its width), worked out for as many cards as the deal gives, so the hand keeps its size as it's played out (Jass's nine make its cards a little smaller than the others' eight). They had been sized apart, the hand by its box alone and the trick by the middle, and the hand came out the smaller: 46 pixels wide against the trick's 54 on a phone, with most of the table empty. Upright, the other three sit along the top so the trick has the table's whole width; held sideways, they sit round the trick and your hand has the side column (25rem wide, 18rem on a phone on its side). The others' backs are as large as their places let them be, up to half your cards, and keep their row when a hand is played out. The rest of the page keeps its size and the cards take what's left: sized for the play (your status, or the coach's line, measured as the play draws it), the bidding's taller buttons take their room from the table rather than from the cards, and what a player says and a Jass player's Weis lie over the table under their place (the Weis on their backs), so the table never changes size. A card is now 101 pixels wide on a 390×844 phone (the others' backs 38, from 17), 83 on a 390×664, 157 on an iPad upright and 111 held sideways, your hand and the trick alike. `fitTable` in trumps.js does it, on every draw and when the screen turns.

Full screen (Menu, October 2026) puts the line across the top (the contract and the score) into the header's row, between the game's mark and the menu, so the cards have the other row's room (the game's name and the target go; Learn is in the menu too): 91 pixels on a 390×664 phone. Where the browser can, the page goes full screen too (an app on the home screen already is); the setting stays when the browser leaves it.

The packs are drawn for the game: Jass in the Swiss pack (bells, shields, roses, acorns; Under, Ober, King, Ace, the ten the Banner) or, by choice, the French; Schafkopf in the German (acorns, leaves, hearts, bells; Unter, Ober, King, the ace the Sau); Belote in the French (cards.js's cards). The suits' marks are drawn for the game, not copied from any maker's cards. One numbering serves every pack (trumps-cards.js: a card is its suit times nine plus its rank).

**Jass** (pagat.com's Schieber): 36 cards, nine each. The player after the dealer chooses trumps (a suit, Obenabe, high cards winning, or Undenufe, low cards winning) or pushes (schiebt) the choice to the partner. In trumps the Under (the Bauer, 20) is highest, then the nine (the Nell, 14), the ace, king, Ober, Banner; the pack is 157 with the last trick's 5, and a match (all nine tricks) 100 more. You must follow suit or trump, may always trump, but never under a trump already in the trick unless you've nothing else; the Bauer never has to be played. Weis (runs of three or more, four alike) are counted in the first trick and shown by each player's place; only the side with the best single Weis scores, all of its own. Stöck (the king and Ober of trumps in one hand) is 20. The count: all single, or the Swiss multipliers (acorns and roses ×1, shields and bells ×2, Obenabe ×3, Undenufe ×4; in the French pack black single, red double), to 1000, 1500, 2500 or 3000.

**Belote**: 32 cards, five each and one turned up; in turn take its suit as trumps (the taker gets the turned card) or pass, then a second round for any other suit, then a new deal. Trumps: jack 20, nine 14; 162 with the dix de der; belote and rebelote (king and queen of trumps) 20. Follow suit; if you can't, trump, over any trump already in, unless your partner holds the trick. The takers must beat the others or the others score 162; capot 250. To 501 or 1000. **Coinche**: Belote with an auction instead of the turned card: 80 to 160 or capot in a suit, coinche (doubled) and surcoinche (redoubled); made, the bidders score the contract (multiplied) and, uncoinched, their points rounded to ten; down, the others score 160 and the contract (multiplied). To 1000 or 2000.

**Schafkopf**: 32 German cards, eight each. Each player in turn says whether they'd play and what, a Solo over a Wenz over a Sauspiel; all passing throws the hand in. Sauspiel: hearts and every Ober and Unter are trumps, and you call an ace (a Sau) you don't hold of a suit you do: its holder is your partner, unknown until it's played. The called ace must be played when its suit is first led, may not be thrown away before the last trick, and its holder can't lead another card of its suit (unless holding four). Wenz: the four Unters alone are trumps; Solo: the Obers, Unters and a suit of your choice, you against three. 61 of the 120 wins; the tariff is 10 for a Sauspiel and 50 for a Solo or Wenz, 10 more for Schneider (the players under 31 when they lose, the others under 30 when the players win: 91 makes it, 90 doesn't, as pagat has it; it once counted 90), 10 more for schwarz and 10 for each Laufende (the top trumps held unbroken by one side, three or more, two in a Wenz); in a Sauspiel the losers pay the winners one each, a soloist is paid by or pays all three. 8, 16 or 24 deals; the running score is each player's own.

The computer players bid by counting each hand's strength for each way it could be played (Jass pushes a weak hand to its partner; Coinche bids what its hand looks worth with its partner's help guessed and coinches a high bid it holds trumps against; Schafkopf, at Easy and Medium, plays a Solo with six trumps and three high Obers and Unters, a Wenz with Unters and aces, a Sauspiel with four trumps and an ace to call, calling the suit it holds least of; Hard's is below). They play by sampling: Medium and Hard deal the cards they can't see among the others as they could lie (each hand's size; the suits a player has shown to be out of, a Jass player's trumping in revealing nothing and one not following trumps perhaps still holding the Bauer; Belote's turned card with its taker), play each card they may to the end of the hand in each deal by a rule of thumb (take a trick worth taking as cheaply as possible, fatten a partner's sure trick, else throw the least), and play the card that did best for their side on average, crossing the contract's line counting for more than a point either side of it (Medium 14 deals, Hard 40, under a quarter of a second; Schafkopf's Hard looks further, below). Easy plays the rule of thumb alone, with a wobble, and doesn't know a Schafkopf partner until the ace is seen.

Hard, in Schafkopf (October 2026), plays as the strongest programs for Skat and bridge play: perfect-information Monte Carlo. Before each card it deals the cards it can't see as they could lie (`trumps-deals.js`: each hand's size, the Farben a player has shown out of, what the call says, every card played so far legal from the hands so dealt) and as the bidding and the play make likely (`trumps-think.js`: cards are then swapped between the hands it can't see, eighty times, each swap kept by the Metropolis rule on how likely what was said, led and given is from the hands as dealt). Every seat is taken to bid as Hard itself would until it shows otherwise (Korbi, October 2026: "assume perfect play until proven otherwise"): a deal is weighed by whether each seat, holding what the deal gives it, would have said just what it said, with a hundredth of the weight for one that wouldn't, read then as likely to hold a game worth saying, so a seat that bid otherwise is made unlikely, not ruled out; once a hand's end shows a seat's cards bid otherwise than Hard would have bid them, its bids are read that softer way only (`s.unlike`, kept by the rules). The players' side reads so, a defender softly, as Hard always had (What Hard reads, below). On 1,000 deals bid by Hard, read softly (as it was), the bidding made the real deal, from a seat that didn't say the game, 2.3 times likelier than a deal that only fits the call in a Sauspiel, 24 times in a Wenz and 68 in a Solo, and only about half the deals drawn were ones Hard's bidders could have held; read exactly, 82 to 91% are, so a Solo's defenders deal its player what Hard would have played a Solo on, a Sauspiel's caller the game it would have called, and whoever passed none worth more. Medium and Easy bid by fixed patterns (a Solo on six trumps with three high Obers and Unters): a hand or two shows them otherwise. Hard never looks at the hands it can't see: `tests/trumps-hard.mjs` asks each of its moves through two hands again with the other three hands (and what the state remembers of them) dealt afresh, and gets the same move. Each deal is then played to the end with every card seen, both sides at their best (`trumps-dd.js`, double dummy: alpha-beta over the cards as bits for the players' points, positions at a trick's start remembered with what's known of their worth, runs of cards that can't differ tried once, and a budget of positions past which a deal is left out), and it plays the card that does best on average in what the hand pays: a Sauspiel's tariff, a Solo's or Wenz's five, Schneider and schwarz one more each, the Laufende (which go to whoever wins, and so make winning dearer), and a hair for each card point. In the first trick a deal is searched for whether the game is won and, after each card that loses it, whether in Schneider (Korbi, October 2026: if the game can't be won, keeping out of Schneider is the goal; on a budget of positions of its own and for every losing card or none, so no deal is left out for it and no card is set against another on unequal terms), in the second for which of the lines that pay (31, 61, 91) the points fall between, from the third for every point, and schwarz (no trick at all) is looked for once a side is about to take nothing. Hard weighs a card over 8 deals when it leads the first trick, 12 when it follows in it, 16 in the second and 24 after; a card takes 42 ms at the median and 0.4 s at the 95th percentile on a laptop. It thinks in a worker (`trumps-worker.js`) while the table waits its pace, so the page never stands still, and past a second and a half (a slow phone's first trick) plays on what it has weighed; where no worker runs it thinks briefly on the page's own thread. Against Medium (two of each at a table, every deal played twice with the seats swapped) it came out ahead by 2.1 a seat a hand, a fifth of a Sauspiel, with a standard error of 0.9, over 780 hands: its Sauspiele with a Hard partner were won 79% of the time against Medium's 57% with a Medium one, its Solos 70% against 55%.

Reading the play, tried and set aside (October 2026). Korbi asked that Hard, which never sees the others' cards, should also read what they play, taking everyone to play perfectly: each deal of the unseen cards weighed by how well the cards played so far fit it, a card that would have thrown points away (with every card seen, both sides at their best) counting against the deals where it would have. Built and measured on 1,030 decisions of Hard's own play, each with the real deal beside the drawn ones, it doesn't pay: nobody plays perfectly who can't see the cards, the computer included, so a card that would have thrown ten points or more away turns up in the real deal 7.8% of the time against 10.1% in a drawn one, and the first trick's cards and the second's lead say nothing measurable at all. Weighed as sharply as the real deals bear (exp(−0.08 × the points), at least 0.6), the play makes the real deal only 3 to 6% likelier than an average drawn one; it changed about 2% of Hard's cards from the third trick and 2 results in 110 deals played twice with the readers' seats swapped (+0.095 ± 0.091 tariffs a hand, both of them Solos), while a card took about six times as long to think (304 ms against 50). Read more sharply, it changes more cards but on worse beliefs: the decisions it led to fell further from the real deal's best. The play carries little because the computer players don't signal: they can't, as long as every card is judged with every card seen, where a signal is worth nothing. The code is on the branch `schafkopf-read-play`, not on main. One kind of card does say a lot, read as the convention it is rather than as perfect play: a Sau or a Zehner given to a trick (What Hard reads, below).

What Hard reads (October 2026). Korbi asked that Hard take everyone to play as it does until shown otherwise, read the bidding for all it says, signal and read signals, guess who the partner is and play for its side's points, keeping out of Schneider where the game can't be won. The players' side reads the bidding exactly (above), and in a Sauspiel the play too, until the called Sau shows, by the table's conventions: the caller's partner leads trump while any may be out and a defender a Farbe; and a non-caller gives a trick another holds a Sau or a Zehner it could have kept (schmiert) when the holder is on its side. Hard plays so by its own weighing, since with every card seen the points go to the side that will take the trick: as partner it gives the caller's trick its Sau or Zehner three times in four where it could, as a defender one time in twelve (to a non-caller's trick a defender gives about as often whoever holds it, 30% and 20% of the time: it can't tell its fellow from the partner either); as partner it leads trump about half the times it could have led either, a defender a Farbe three times in four. Each seat is taken to lead as Hard does until its own leads show how it does (counted at each hand's end, as partner and as defender apart: `s.signals`) and to give as Hard does, and a deal of the unseen cards is weighed by how likely what was led and given is from it (`playLikelihood` in `trumps-think.js`). So the caller finds its partner far sooner: from the fourth trick, with the Sau still out, its deals give the Sau to the real partner 75% of the time (42% before; an even guess 33%; in 50 deals). As partner, Hard leads trump wherever that costs at most a twentieth of a tariff, so a person playing with it can tell, and the coach says so of that card.

What it's worth (October 2026), in whole hands played both ways: each Sauspiel bid by Hard, played with Hard as it was at every seat, and again with one side's seats on the new, the side's tariffs compared deal by deal. The players' side reading so came out +0.26 tariffs a Sauspiel (± 0.12, 205 hands) and −0.10 (± 0.09, 167) in two runs: nothing measurable either way. A defender reading so lost 0.21 (± 0.11) and 0.33 (± 0.14), so a defender reads the bidding softly and the play not at all, as Hard always had, and came out +0.11 (± 0.07) and −0.01 (± 0.07) so. Probably its search, which takes every card after the one it weighs to be played with every card seen, gives a game up for Schneider sooner than a fight against a real caller warrants once it reads the caller's strength as it is. Weighed decision by decision instead (each of Hard's cards, and the card each version would have played there, scored in the real deal with both sides playing on with every card seen), nothing measures: the bidding read exactly +0.05 tariffs a hand (± 0.07, 418 hands), the play −0.03 (± 0.08), and even knowing the partner outright from the first card, every deal drawn giving the Sau to the real partner, −0.01 (± 0.08). Who the partner is matters little to a search that plays on with every card seen: to turn a better guess into tariffs would take a search that weighs what each seat will know, not only what is so. Keeping out of Schneider from the first trick gained 0.04 a hand (± 0.03). A card takes a fifth longer to think (60 ms against 50 in Node on a laptop).

It bids by what a game is worth on average: its chance of being won times what a win pays, less the chance of losing times what a loss costs (a Solo's or Wenz's three times over, since all three pay), against nothing for passing; it says the best. The chance and what a game won or lost comes to are `trumps-bids.js`'s, fitted by `tools/trumps-bids.mjs`: a logistic regression of winning on the hand's features as its player sees them (the eight Obers and Unters one by one, the trumps, the Laufende, the trump Farbe's Sau and Zehner, the Säue, Zehner with and without their Sau, Könige, blank Farben, singletons, the longest Farbe and the Farben's points, the called Farbe's length and Zehner, and whether it leads), and least squares for what each comes to, on 29,006 games played out with every card seen in random deals (15,866 Sauspiele, 9,103 Solos, 4,037 Wenz). Every card seen favours whoever would otherwise have to guess, so the chances are then moved to Hard's own play (the log-odds a plus b times the fit's, and what a game comes to likewise): in 238 Sauspiele, 429 Solos and 182 Wenz said near the line and played with Hard on every seat (the Solos and Wenz on hands dealt strong, since random deals rarely give one), the Sauspiel came out as every card seen said (won 55% where it said 55%; 0.08 plus 0.71 times), but a Solo was won 24% of the time where it said 14%, and a Wenz 45% where it said 27% (0.44 plus 0.44 times, 0.68 plus 0.46): a lone player's three opponents can't play as one when they can't see each other's cards. The Hint and the Why sheet show the same numbers for your hand.

The coach (the menu's Coach, Schafkopf; October 2026): Off, Hints, Check or Always, as the other games' coaches. Hints: a Hint under your cards on your turn rings the card Hard would play in your seat, or the game it would say, and says why in a line: what the card does (a trump led, to draw theirs; the called Farbe searched while its Sau is out; a Sau cashed; schmieren on your side's trick; the partner's trump lead, which tells the caller it's you; stechen; a trick taken, or let go to keep what takes one later, the course's motifs) and how often it wins the game over the deals of the cards you can't see against the next best, or, winning as often, what more it comes to on average; for a game, its chance and what it comes to on your score, with what in the hand speaks for it (its trumps and how many are Obers and Unters, its Säue besides, the called Farbe). Check: a Hint when asked, and a word after a card of yours that was behind the coach's by an eighth of a game's base and by two standard errors of the gap over the same deals (each deal's worth is kept, so a real gap is told from the deals' luck), or after a bid behind its own by a tenth of a Sauspiel. Always: its card ringed and its line at each of your turns, unasked. Why? opens its reasons: every card (or game) it weighed, how often each wins, what each comes to on your score (the table's own units: a Sauspiel 10, a Solo's player three times what each of the others pays) and what each does. The coach weighs in a second worker, up to 32 deals in under three seconds, so it never holds up the table; its line keeps its room under your cards while it's on, so the table doesn't change size as it speaks.

At a table (October 2026) the coach is the table's: its host (a party's host) sets it from the menu, it's kept on the table (`house`), and every phone coaches as it says, so the host's Off takes everyone's Hint away; the others' menus say what the host chose. Solo, a phone's own setting governs. The table's code is version 2 for it, so a phone on older code updates before it sits down.

The Bavarian pack (the menu's Cards: Bavarian, Schafkopf; October 2026): the whole pack drawn as vectors (`trumps-deck.js`) after the Bavarian pattern as it is played: the Munich pattern of the nineteenth century, double-ended as today's packs print it, with the rank (U, O, K, A or the number) over the Farbe's small mark in every corner, where a fanned hand shows them. The figures keep the pattern's conventions, which are free to draw; the drawings are our own, not traced from any maker's pack. The Obers: der Alte, the Eichel-Ober, long-haired in a green tunic, with an oval red shield bearing a cross of fleurs and a sabre; the Gras-Ober, the drummer, in a bicorne, a red cross-belt over a yellow front; the Herz-Ober in a blue beret with a red plume, one hand on his hip and one on his sword; the Schellen-Ober in a lace collar and a red doublet buttoned in gold, his sword across him. The Unters: the Eichel-Unter in a green coat faced red, pointing, his hand on a cup hilt; the Gras-Unter, the fifer, moustached, a cockade on his bicorne; the Herz-Unter with a spear held high; the Schellen-Unter swinging a sabre over his head, a round shield on his back. The Könige sit enthroned and crowned, the Gras-König's crown set on a turban as the pattern has it. The Säue: Bacchus as a child astride a barrel among hops, his Maß raised (Eichel); a great leaf, a stalk of roses under a red lily and Bavaria's arms in a cartouche (Gras); a great heart over an urn of roses between acanthus scrolls (Herz); and the wild boar with a hound at his neck (Schellen). The marks are drawn as printed: the acorn's nut yellow and striped red over a blue band and a green cup, the bell's red band with its three holes over a crown of green leaves, the right half of every mark hatched. The small cards repeat all their marks in each half in overlapping columns, the acorns and the leaves lying about a stalk, the Zehner with its X. Shading is hatched, as on an engraved card. A court's or a Sau's upper half is drawn once and shown again turned about the centre. The back is Bavaria's white and blue Rauten. Each card is an SVG of the table's cards' shape, made on the page once as a blob's address for an `<img>` (a hand's markup stays small, a card is drawn once): 26 KB at most, 550 KB in all, drawn by code, so there is nothing to download and nothing to keep for offline play. The table, the course and the puzzle rush draw it; Simple (each Farbe's mark, the rank in the corner) stays the default. It replaces a first drawing whose Unters were stockmen in Lederhosen, which had replaced the scans of J. N. Valter's Bayerisches Bild (Regensburg, 1826) the menu first offered; the side branch `fetch/bavarian-cards` that fetched those scans, and the pictures of the pattern these drawings were studied from, can be deleted.

The course (Learn, in the header; October 2026): Schafkopf taught from nothing, whatever game the menu has, on the course sheet the card games share (`course.js`; README, Dou Dizhu) with a stage of Trumps' own (`trumps-course.js`): the German cards drawn as the table draws them (the drawing moved to `trumps-cards.js`, so the page and the course share it) and sized as the card games' course sizes its own (`course-cards.js`'s `fitCardStage`), the game and who plays it, the trick with who played each card, your hand held as the table holds it (the same `sortHand`: the Farben, then the trumps, rising to the right). Cards are written as Bavarians say them, Farbe then rank ("EO" der Alte, "GA" die Gras-Sau, "H10"), and named in German ("the Herz-Siebener"). The game, in eleven lessons: the short pack and what it counts (120), the fourteen trumps of a Sauspiel, the Farben (the Zehner above the König), following (an Ober or Unter led is a trump led; with nothing of it, anything goes), who takes a trick, the bidding (weiter, i spui; a Solo over a Wenz over a Sauspiel), calling a Sau and which you may, the called Sau's three rules (gesucht, never thrown away, davonlaufen only with four), 61 and what a hand pays (Schneider's two lines, schwarz, Laufende), the Wenz and the Solo; then a first game against Easy. A step is words over cards, a hand to tap through (every trump, or every card you may play to the trick: a wrong one is dimmed with why, "The called Sau can't be thrown away before the last trick"; Hint marks the rest), a card to play (tap it, then Play), or a choice. Where you are is kept (`trumps:course`). `tests/trumps-course.mjs` checks every card written and that the trick and the hand never share one, that every hand to tap through has as many cards to find as its words say, that every card to play is legal and judged right and of the cards the hand may play only the meant ones are, that every whole trick a question shows is taken by the card its answer names, the points, the Sau the hand may call (the rules' `callable`) and what the Sauspiel won 92 to 28 with three Laufende pays (50, by the rules' own `scoreHand`), and taps every exercise through the stage on a page of plain objects. The Rules sheet, Schafkopf's, opens from the course's map, and each game's from the menu.

Playing well, the course's second chapter: counting the trumps (fourteen in a Sauspiel or a Solo, four in a Wenz: how many are still out, and with five out against your Solo, an Ober led), drawing their trumps (the players lead trumps, the others lead Farben, the called one first while its Sau is out; with one trump out, an Ober to draw it before the Gras-Sau; with none out, the Säue stand), schmieren (a Zehner on a trick your partner holds when you play last; a small card when the two still to play could take it), stechen (over the Herz-Zehner that took your partner's Sau; with the Herz-Sau, not the Unter, when its 11 make 61), your partner's trick (the Herz-Unter under his Schellen-Ober, der Alte kept for the last trick, and 60 enough against a game); then a game against Hard. Its positions are from the computer players' own games, shown as they were reached: the tricks played so far (each taker's card ringed, every card's corner showing) and what each side has taken, as far as you'd know the sides (`takenOf`: the players once the called Sau is out, and always your own side). Each card a lesson asks for is the best when the cards you can't see are dealt every way they could lie and each deal is played to the end of the hand with every card seen (`trumps-solve.js`: double dummy, alpha-beta over the players' side against the others, worth counted in games won, Schneider and schwarz, and a hair for each card point), by at least an eighth of a game over every other card. The deals honour each hand's size, the Farben a player has shown out of, the call (the caller holds no called Sau, and still a card of its Farbe until one is played), and now every card played: a deal under which some card played would have been illegal is dealt again, which keeps the called Sau with whoever ran away with it (davonlaufen: its Farbe led and the Sau not come). On a stage with this much on it the hand takes less of the height and the trick doesn't shrink below a readable size, the hand giving way instead (`fitCardStage`'s share and floor, which the other card games' courses leave at their old values). `tests/trumps-course.mjs` checks that each position is one pack with each trick led by whoever took the last and some deal of the unseen cards under which every card played was legal, that a question counting the trumps has the position's count, and weighs every card to play again, 300 deals each: the lesson's card best by an eighth of a game and three standard errors of the difference clear of every card not accepted, every card accepted within that eighth.

Puzzle rush (the menu; October 2026, called Schafkopf puzzle rush until every game's rush took one name; its positions are all Schafkopf's, as its words before a run say): as Dominoes' (README, Dominoes) and on the same sheet (`rush-sheet.js`), with every game's rush settings (README, Puzzle rush), with Schafkopf's 400 positions (`trumps-puzzles.js`: 40 at each rating; Sauspiel and Solo alike, and every Wenz found). `tools/trumps-puzzles.mjs` finds them in the computer players' own games (Medium against Medium), late in a hand (four cards a hand or fewer, sometimes five) where one card is best by a quarter of a game or more on average, four standard errors clear, when the cards that player couldn't see are dealt every way they could lie and each deal is played to the end with every card seen (`trumps-solve.js`, as Playing well is checked). A puzzle is drawn on the course's stage (the game and who plays it, the tricks played, what each side has taken, the trick, your hand): tap a card, then Play. The verdict says how often the best won the game over its deals and how often yours did (or, when you found it, the next best); where both win alike, what each came to on average in games (a game won counts 1, Schneider and schwarz one more each); and what the best card does (a trump led, the called Farbe searched while its Sau is out, a Sau cashed, schmieren, stechen, the trick taken or let go). Ratings are by rank: the computer's rule of thumb missing a puzzle, a closer call, more cards to choose from and to hold make it harder, and the bank is cut into tenths. The sheet writes its words before it draws the stage, which sizes itself to the room they leave (a long verdict had pushed the stage past its box). `tests/trumps-rush.mjs` checks every puzzle could have been reached (one pack, each trick led by the last one's taker, some deal under which every card played was legal), that the cards weighed are exactly those the hand may play, the best the one worth most and clear of the rest, its motifs found again, every rating stocked and every game in the bank; weighs sixteen again in 160 deals of their own, the best still best; and checks a run's order and the verdicts' words.

`tests/trumps-deck.mjs` checks the drawn pack: every card and the back an SVG of the cards' shape, well formed, every number a number, each card its own and small, every id in a card defined once (two parts under one id would clip each other), both ends alike, the rank and the Farbe's mark in the corners, a court's great mark high beside an Ober's or a König's head and lower on an Unter, and the addresses the page uses. `tests/trumps-hard.mjs` checks the search with every card seen against a plain minimax (every card's points, in Sauspiel, Wenz and Solo, some mid-trick; the lines at least so many points; schwarz's search; a whole hand's 61, 91 and 31), the deals of the unseen cards (sizes, a Farbe not followed, the called Sau's player on the caller's side), that the weighing finds the puzzle rush's best in ten of its positions, the bidding's fit on hands whose game is plain (four Obers a Sauspiel all but won, sevens and eights lost, the top six trumps an Eichel-Solo, four Unters and four Säue a Wenz), Hard's bids, moves worked out ahead taken only when legal, Hard through a whole hand, and the coach's words. `tests/trumps.mjs` checks Jass's cards and play (the 157, the Bauer and the Nell, Obenabe and Undenufe, following or trumping, no undertrump, the Bauer's freedom), its Weis and multipliers; Belote's 162, its play and its bidding (taken, the second round, a new deal); Coinche's auction and a redoubled contract made; Schafkopf's trumps, the called ace's duties, the Laufende, a hand thrown in and a Solo's payment; and a whole game of each played by the computer, every move legal, the state coming back whole from the database.
