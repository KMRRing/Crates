# Contract bridge
Contract bridge (October 2026), four at a table in two partnerships, North–South against East–West, against the computer or with friends each on their own phone (Invite players: seats.js runs it, the computer taking the seats nobody has). You sit South with your partner North across the table. Two people brought by a party sit across from each other as partners (Party's `across`); anyone else sits down in the lowest seat free.

A match is Chicago, the four-deal match clubs play when four sit down for an evening: the dealer moves clockwise, and the vulnerability (which raises both what a side's games and slams score and what its contracts cost going down) follows the deals, nobody on the first, the dealer's side on the second and third, both on the fourth. Each deal is scored as duplicate bridge scores it, its game or part-score bonus on the deal itself, and the running score adds them; the side ahead after four deals wins the match, and a run of matches won shows on the games screen. A deal all four pass is dealt again by the same dealer. The rules, the whole scoring table among them, are on the Rules sheet (the menu).

The auction: from the dealer, each player in turn passes, bids (one to seven tricks over six, in clubs, diamonds, hearts, spades or notrump, each bid higher than the last), doubles the other side's last bid or redoubles their double of their own; three passes after a bid end it. Of the side that won the contract, whoever first named its strain is declarer; the player on declarer's left leads, then dummy (declarer's partner) lays down their cards and declarer plays both hands. Thirteen tricks: follow suit if you can, the highest card of the suit led takes the trick unless a trump is played. Made, the contract scores its tricks (20 a trick in the minors, 30 in the majors, 40 then 30 in notrump; doubled twice that, redoubled four times), 300 more for a game (100 or more in tricks; 500 vulnerable), 50 for a part-score, 500 or 1000 for a small or a grand slam (750, 1500 vulnerable), 50 or 100 for making it doubled or redoubled, and its overtricks; down, the defenders score 50 a trick (100 vulnerable), doubled 100, 200, 200, then 300 (vulnerable 200, then 300), redoubled twice that (`dealScore` in `contract-core.js`).

The page: your partner across the top, the opponents left and right on the felt, your hand along the foot. While it's bid, the auction is drawn in the middle as bridge writes it, a column a seat from your left, and every call in it says what it showed when tapped; under your hand, the bidding box: a level, then a strain (a second tap on it bids it), or Pass, Double or Redouble. Once the lead is made dummy's cards lie face up: as a bridge column (a suit a line, high to low) by its seat, or across the top as cards to play when it's yours to play, your side declaring. The trick lies in the middle, each card on its player's side, the card taking it ringed. The line across the top has the deal and who's vulnerable, the contract (tap it for the auction and what every call meant), the tricks each side has taken, and the match. After a deal its result shows on the felt with where its points came from; The four hands (and Review) shows the deal, the auction, the opening lead marked, and the tricks the contract makes with every card seen. Held sideways the auction stays beside the table through the play, and the trick, the auction and the seats grow with the table. The narrator writes each deal, the contract and the result. The game lives in `contract.html`, `contract.js` and `contract.css`, its rules in `contract-core.js` (cards, calls, the play, the score) and `contract-rules.js` (the match on seats.js).

The bidding (`contract-sayc.js`) is SAYC, the Standard American Yellow Card, the system American clubs and most online play teach first, written as tables of rules. Each rule is a call and what it shows: a range of high-card points (an ace 4, a king 3, a queen 2, a jack 1, 40 in the pack), lengths in each suit, a balanced hand (no short suit), a stopper in the other side's suit (a card that stops them running it), or a test of its own. A computer player calls the first rule that fits its hand and is legal; the same tables read every call, so what a call shows is what the rules that make it at that point have in common, and what a player has shown is every one of their calls read together (a seat that opened 1♥ and then passed partner's invitation has shown 11 to 14). The openings: 1NT 15–17 balanced, 2NT 20–21, 2♣ strong and artificial (22 or more, or nine tricks in hand), five-card majors and the longer minor, light in third seat, the rule of 20 in first and second (points plus the two longest suits' lengths), the rule of 15 in fourth (points plus spades), weak twos in diamonds, hearts and spades, preempts at the three and four level. The answers: raises (single 6–10, limit 10–12, preemptive to four, Jacoby 2NT with four trumps and an opening, game forcing), a new suit (forcing; at the two level 10 or more), 1NT 6–10, 2NT and 3NT by points, Stayman (2♣ over 1NT: a four-card major?) and Jacoby transfers (2♦ and 2♥ for hearts and spades, 2♠ for a minor), the feature ask over a weak two, opener's rebids by strength, reverses, jump shifts. Competing: overcalls (a good five-card suit, 8–16), the 1NT overcall (15–18 with their suit stopped), takeout doubles (short in their suit, support for the others, or any strong hand), the negative double (responder's takeout double after their overcall), cue-bid raises, the reopening double, the penalty double of 1NT, and leaving partner's penalty double in. Slams: Blackwood (4NT asks for aces, 5NT then for kings), the quantitative 4NT over notrump, slam tries over transfers. A call no rule makes still reads as natural. Over 2000 random deals bid by four computer players, every call was legal and 1% were passed out; partnerships with 26–32 points between them bid game or slam 93% of the time with eight cards in a major and 90% without, with 23–25 60% and 40%. A call takes a quarter of a millisecond.

The play by the computer players (`contract-play.js`): Easy plays by the rules of thumb (the opening lead's, second hand low and an honour covered, third hand high, the cheapest winner fourth, declarer drawing trumps, winners cashed, partner's suit returned, discards from the weakest suit). Medium and Hard play by perfect-information Monte Carlo, the way the strongest bridge programs play: before each card they deal the cards they can't see as they could lie (each hand's size, the suits a player has shown out of, and what the auction showed: each hand's points and suit lengths, relaxed a step at a time if no deal fits), solve every deal double dummy (with every card seen, both sides at their best: how many tricks each card they may play takes), and play the card that reaches their side's goal (the contract made, or beaten) in the most deals, then the one with the most tricks; among cards as good, the one convention plays (the top of a sequence, the lowest that does the job, a high spot card to encourage partner's suit when holding an honour in it). Medium weighs up to 12 deals in under half a second a card, Hard up to 48 in a second. Every level leads by the rules (`openingLead`: partner's suit, the top of a sequence, fourth best from length, never away from an ace against a suit, the longest suit against notrump): over 150 deals a lead weighed by sampling (Hard's, 48 deals) left declarer as many tricks double dummy as the rules' lead (9.29 against 9.28, the rules' lead the best there is on 110 of them), so it isn't worth a second's wait, and the rules' lead is the one partner can read and the course teaches.

The solver is DDS, Bo Haglund's and Soren Hein's double-dummy solver (Apache 2.0), compiled to WebAssembly by the npm package @bridge-tools/dd (MIT) and shipped unchanged in `vendor/dds/` with its licences and a note of where it came from (`NOTE.txt`): a single-threaded build, so it needs nothing GitHub Pages can't send, started by `contract-dds.js` with the few imports it asks for (the package's own JavaScript isn't used). A deal of thirteen cards a hand takes it about a tenth of a second on a laptop, a late trick a few milliseconds. It runs in a worker (`contract-worker.js`), started once, so the page never stands still: the computer players think while the table waits its pace (solo the table holds their card until it's in; at a table, the phone that runs it waits as long as the level may think and a little more, and only a card still not in by then is played by the rules of thumb), and the hint and the review's every-card-seen line ask the same worker. Offline, the worker and the solver are kept as every page's files are (`tools/offline.mjs` follows the worker's and the solver's addresses). Against each other (`tools/contract-arena.mjs`, every deal bid by SAYC and played twice with the sides swapped, the difference counted in IMPs, international match points, the scale team games score by, which keeps one big swing from drowning the rest): Medium gained 4.5 IMPs a deal on Easy over 30 deals (better on 26, worse on 1), Hard 5.3 (better on 29, worse on none); Hard against Medium came out level, 0.2 IMPs a deal over 40 deals (better on 8, worse on 5, the same on 27): past a dozen deals, more deals weighed tell less than the deals' own luck. Declaring against Easy's defence they took 0.9 and 0.8 tricks a deal more than double dummy (the defence's slips taken); against each other, 0.1.

Hints (the menu's Hints, on at first): in the auction, Hint lights the call the system makes with your hand in the box and says what it shows partner ("1♠: new suit (6+ points, 4+ spades; forcing)"; a pass by its reading, "up to 12 points", or "no bid fits this hand"); in the play, the card Hard would play in your seat (48 deals, a second and a half), ringed, with how often it makes or beats the contract over the deals weighed and the next best card's figure; on the opening lead, the rules' lead. Tap any call in the auction for what it showed, as the system reads it.

The course (Learn, in the header; October 2026): bridge from nothing, on the course sheet the card games share (`course.js`; README, Landlord) with a stage of its own (`contract-course.js`): examples of cards and calls with their names, the auction as a bridge column with your call to make, a bidding box, dummy and your hand, the trick. 28 lessons in nine chapters: the game (thirteen tricks, trumps, the auction, dummy and the opening lead), valuing a hand (high-card points, balanced hands), opening the bidding (one of a suit, notrump, strong and weak openings), answering partner (a major opened, a new suit, a minor opened, opener's second bid), notrump bidding (Stayman, Jacoby transfers), competing (overcalls and doubles, the negative double, slams and Blackwood), declarer play (counting winners, drawing trumps, the finesse, ruffing in dummy, setting up a long suit), defence (opening leads, second hand low and third hand high, signals), the score (what a contract scores, Chicago); then a first match. A step is words over cards, a hand to tap through (every honour, every card that may follow), a call to make in the box (judged against the system's own call for that hand and auction), a card to play from your hand or dummy (Hint marks it), or a choice. Where you are is kept (`contract:course`).

Puzzle rush (the menu; October 2026): as the other games' rushes, on the same sheet (`rush-sheet.js`) with every game's rush settings (README, Puzzle rush), with 399 positions (`contract-puzzles.js`: 283 declaring, 116 defending) from 400 of the computer players' own deals. `tools/contract-puzzles.mjs` plays deals the computer players bid by SAYC and play at Medium; from the seventh trick on, each decision with a choice is weighed over 16 deals of the cards its player can't see, dealt as that player could know them (each hand's size, the suits shown out of, what the auction showed) and each solved double dummy; one whose best card makes or beats the contract in 25 points more of the deals than any other is weighed again over 160 fresh deals and kept if it still is, four standard errors clear, deal by deal against the next best. Cards that did as well in every deal (touching cards, small cards alike) are all as good. At most two are kept from a deal, a trick or more apart, a defender's beside a declarer's where there's one, each with three cards or more to choose from. A puzzle is its deal, its auction and the cards played so far, the seats turned so its player is South (declaring, from your hand or dummy's, or defending), put back together by the rules (`contract-rush.js`) and drawn on the course's stage: the contract with its auction in a line, dummy, the trick, your hand; tap a card, then Play. The words say what your side has and needs, and the cards still out (what a player keeping count would know). The verdict: how often the best and yours (or, when you found it, the next best) made or beat the contract over its deals, and what the best does (takes the trick, lets it go, trumps it, draws trumps, cashes a winner, throws the card that isn't needed). Ratings are by rank: Easy's rule of thumb and Medium missing it, a closer call, more cards to choose from and to hold make one harder, the bank cut into ten. The sheet's page part is `contract-rush-ui.js`.

`tests/contract.mjs` checks the duplicate score of part-scores, games, slams, doubled and redoubled contracts, overtricks and undertricks by vulnerability, and where a result's points came from; the calls (what may be called, a redouble only by the doubled side, the end of the auction, a passed-out deal) and the contract and its declarer; following suit and who takes a trick; SAYC's call in sample positions and what calls show (1NT, Stayman, Jacoby 2NT, the negative and the takeout double, a pass in first seat, an unknown call read as natural); 60 deals bid and played by four computer players, every call and card legal, every card once, each scored as the table says; the solver against a plain search of small endings in every strain, mid-trick too, and on deals whose answer is known; the deals of the unseen hands (each hand's size, the known cards kept, a void known); whole deals played by sampling and a cold grand slam taking all thirteen; the opening leads; the Chicago match (four deals, the dealer clockwise, the vulnerability by deal, the running score, a passed-out deal dealt again); a match through the database's round trip; a whole deal at a table of two people and two computer players on the stand-in database, both phones seeing it the same; and the course: every call it asks for is the system's own and no other is accepted, every card to play is in the hand that plays it and judged right, every card judged right takes as many tricks as the best with every card seen where the lesson gives all four hands, every quiz's answer an option and every score it quotes the scoring's own; and the puzzle rush: every puzzle a position its deal reaches (one pack, the auction and every card legal), South's card (or dummy's, South declaring), the cards weighed exactly those it may play, the best ahead of every card not as good by 25 points of the deals, what the best does found again, the stage and the verdict judging alike, every rating stocked, and a puzzle of each rating weighed again over 60 deals of its own, the best still ahead.
