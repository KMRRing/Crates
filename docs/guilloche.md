# Guilloché
The tenth idle game, rebuilt (23 October 2026; README, Idle): `guilloche.html`, `guilloche.js` and `guilloche.css`, the machine and its levels in `guilloche-engine.js` (no page: the tests run it in Node), the watch drawn by `guilloche-3d.js` (WebGL; `guilloche-flat.js` draws it flat where there is none) to the specs in `guilloche-watch.js`. It replaced Rosette, a Block Blast with cuts repeated round a dial, because Korbi wanted "the more engaging guilloche game ... someone able to play the guilloche game should have a good understanding of how they are made", a simulator of the machine and not a puzzle that borrows its look. (The proposal he pasted is not kept with the project, so the design below is mine: what it keeps of his wording is that playing teaches how guilloché is made, and that it is calm and takes patience rather than speed.)

How a rose engine cuts. The plate turns; a cutting point rides in and out of a set radius as it turns, pushed by a rosette, a disc with a wavy edge that a rubbing block follows. One pass is a ring with as many waves as the rosette has lobes, as deep as its throw. A pattern is many passes: between one and the next the rosette is turned a little, the index, so each ring's waves sit a little round from the last, and the crossings are the lattice a guilloché is. Turned a whole lobe in all (passes times index is 360 degrees), the rose closes. A feed that steps the cut outward on each pass makes a ribbon; a second rosette riding on the first adds its waves to every pass (a basket); and a dial is several cuts, each in a zone of its own radius. In the engine a pass j of a cut is r(θ) = R + j·feed + throw·rosette(lobes·θ + j·index) + throw2·sin(lobes2·θ) on a plate of radius 1, the rosette's edge a wave, scallops (arches meeting in cusps: the barleycorn grain) or points; sampled at 720 points, a pass is drawn as the line through them. The straight-line engine slides the plate under the cutter instead, so a pass is a line (a pitch apart, at an angle, with a second set across for pyramids), and a rosette on its bar makes every line a wave. The same machine turned a little between strokes from the centre out cuts rays, and short lines in square blocks, each block's square to the next, a basket weave. The graver is a V, so how deep it goes is how wide its groove is (`grooveWidth`: each step of Depth half again as wide as the last); cut deep, grooves a little apart meet in ridges and leave no flat between them.

The game (set up, then cut, since 27 October 2026). The target plate is shown faint on the dial, a line down the middle of each of its grooves, bolder the deeper the target is cut (the menu turns it off), and held still under a finger for a quarter of a second the target shows alone, engraved, depth and all. The machine has the knobs the plate's level uses and nothing else: Lobes (2 to 24), Throw (1 to 10 steps of a fiftieth of the plate's radius), Rosette (wave, scallop, point), Passes (1 to 40), Index (-180 to 180 degrees in fives), Feed, a second rosette's Lobes and Throw, the straight engine's Pitch, Angle and Cross, the radial cut's Rays (12 to 120 in sixes) and Sweep (-90 to 90 degrees in tens), the weave's Block, and Depth (1 to 6). A knob is a slider with a step each way and a hold that runs; each shows its value; in the corner of the plate the rosette of the cut in hand is drawn as a disc with the wavy edge those lobes, throw and shape give it, with its rubbing block, so what a knob does is seen where it comes from. Turning a knob engraves nothing: the path the cutting point would take is drawn over the dial as a fine line (cobalt on a light dial, gilt on a dark one), and Cut, the plate beside Undo and Clue, cuts it, the needle running the zone's passes one after another and the grooves opening behind it (about a second for one pass, two and a half for forty). A plate with more than one cut has a tab for each (centre, band, rim); each zone is set and cut in turn, and its tab is underlined once it is cut, in oxblood until it is cut true and then in verdigris with a tick. Cut a zone again after changing a knob and it is faced off and cut anew. The line under the plate says how near the setting in hand is, that it is set as the target ("Cut it"), or what a cut came to, and the plate is done when every zone is cut as the target's. The panel keeps as many rows as the most any cut of the plate has, so nothing changes size when you change cut.

The twenty-two plates (from 27 October 2026; fifteen from 24 October, eleven before that), each opening when the one before has a star (or one of its own has been engraved): the rose (passes and index, always a rose that closes, so passes times index is 360, a showpiece first), straight lines (pitch, angle and depth), clous de Paris (a second set of lines across the first, cut deep enough to meet: pitch, angle and cross), waves (a wave on the straight engine's bar: lobes, throw and depth), sunburst (rays and depth), the wave ring (lobes and throw: one pass), scallops and points (the rosette's edge), barleycorn (lines with a rosette's wave running along them, each line's set along by the index, so they zigzag; its pitch and angle are fixed), watered silk (waves set a few degrees along line by line: lobes, throw and index), fish scale (scalloped lines, each half a wave along from the last: pitch, lobes and throw), flinqué (rays with a wave on them, in step: rays, lobes and throw), flames (rays that bend: rays and sweep), basket weave (pitch, block and angle), the ribbon (feed), the woven ring (a second rosette; called Basket until the basket weave came), two zones (a rose in the centre and a scalloped ring), field and medallion (a clous de Paris field with a hole, a rose in the hole and a scalloped rim), three zones (rose, ribbon and a pointed rim), the master dial (three cuts, two with a second rosette), then the registers, the chronograph and the grand (see Registers below). Every plate is made from a seed, a different one every time; the hidden setting is drawn from the level's ranges so that each knob differs from where the machine starts and a step either way of any one of them makes a cut that is not the target's (`makeJob` checks every neighbour and draws again if one is too near, up to sixty times).

What is checked is the engraving, not the settings, zone by zone. A cut is a picture of G (192) squares across, each holding the number of times a pass crosses it, so a pass more or less shows even in a dense rose where the passes overlap; a zone is the target's when its picture differs in less than one hundredth (`alike`, 0.99) and it is cut to the same depth (a picture of where the grooves run cannot show how deep they go, so each step of depth apart takes a fifth off the likeness, `cutMatch`), which in practice means the same cut: any setting that engraves the same zone counts. (A first version compared plates as covered or bare, forgiving a square either way; the passes of a dense rose cover the whole band, so one pass more, or two lobes more, was "the same", and it was dropped for the counts.) That is strict, so for the line under the plate there is another measure, `near`: the same comparison with each plate's counts spread over a few squares round them, so a ring cut a step of throw out reads as nearer than one with other lobes. It is for telling a hand which way to go, never for finishing.

The grade is stars, by taps against par, as in Loom: par is the knobs to set and a cut for each zone, a tap each (three for the wave ring, five for the rose, eighteen for the master dial); up to half again par and two is three stars, up to three times it and four two, any finish one. A tap is one gesture: a drag along a slider, a hold on a step, or a press is one however far the knob goes (so a long drag costs no more than a press), and Undo takes back a gesture (not a cut: a zone cut wrong is cut again). When the last zone is cut true, the plate's stars count at once (its best and the stars in all; random mode moves on from there, README, Random mode), the markers and hands are set, the glass goes on and the camera draws back to show the whole watch, and a dialog gives the taps, par, passes cut and clues, and what the plate was about.

Saved state is under `guilloche:`: `guilloche:run` (the plate in hand, its seed, every cut's knobs, what each zone was last cut with (`cut`; a run kept before 27 October has none, so its plate opens with nothing engraved, or as it was set if it was finished) and the cut in hand), `guilloche:jobs` (the stars by plate), `guilloche:best` (the stars in all, for the games screen) and `guilloche:opts` (the faint target, sound). Sounds are made as they play. There is no clock; a cut or the watch being put together stands still while the menu is open and finishes when it closes.

`tests/guilloche.mjs` also checks the straight cuts (a line every pitch, level at angle 0 and upright at 90, a second set square to the first at 90, lines stopping at a hole all along them, a straight line kept as its two ends and a wavy one as its waves need, a wave no more than its throw off a line, a plate of lines nearer its target a knob at a time), rays (from a small hub to the rim, a ray's share of a turn apart, the first at twelve; sweep bending each by that much at the rim, either way; a wave in step pushing every ray the same way at a radius), the basket weave (short lines, level or upright, inside the plate; each block Block lines a pitch apart, square to the blocks beside it), depth (each step half again as wide; lines in the right places a step of depth off near but not the target's; a dial's zones at depths of their own; no rose-engine groove a hairline), setting up and cutting (a new plate bare; knobs engraving nothing; Cut engraving the zone in hand as set, right or not; nothing new to cut when it's cut as set; a zone faced off and cut anew, a tap for the cut; one zone true not the plate, and the line naming the zone still to cut; every zone true, the plate done; what was cut kept through JSON; a lesson's cut free) and the levels' order by name. It checks a pass (it starts at twelve o'clock on its radius and closes on itself, the throw is how far it rides, the lobes are its waves, each rosette shape rides the same distance, the index turns and the feed steps pass by pass, a second rosette adds its waves, a cut never runs off the plate); plates as pictures (the same engraving alike to a pixel, a step of any knob is not, one pass more is not, `near` forgives a little); every level over twenty-four seeds (the same from a seed and another from another, every knob in range and away from the start, every neighbour told apart, a rose's passes times index 360); setting a knob and undoing it (a tap, clamped, a hold's further steps one tap, a knob not free refused, a finger's batch back last first); the stars; and a run kept through JSON.

**Set up, then cut; depth; textures** (27 October 2026). Korbi: "the straight line ones are already rendered before cut. user should make the lines first, then cut the guillochet. depth should be a more common feature, the rosette ones are generally too thin. and look up some actuall guillochet patterns beyond these simple circular ones. the "texture" ones are beautiful, there's wavy, radial, complex "woven" looking ones - all of which don't seem to be represented yet". Three changes. First, the knobs used to engrave as they turned, so a plate of lines stood fully engraved before anything was cut and the cut at the end only played it again; now a knob sets the machine up, its path is a fine line, and Cut engraves, zone by zone, as a guillocheur works area by area (`cutPlate` in the engine; `run.cut` keeps what each zone was cut with). Second, depth is the graver's: a V, so a deeper cut is a wider groove, drawn as real V's composited by depth (under The watch), so a deep cut leaves ridges and no flat between them, the faceted look of a real dial. Every rose-engine plate is cut deeper than the old hairline (depth 3 or 4; the old groove was about depth 1, and the rose's throw now runs 4 to 8, from 3 to 6, for fuller roses), the zones of a dial to depths of their own, Paris and barleycorn deep enough to meet, and Depth is a knob on straight lines, waves and the sunburst. ("Depth" read as how deep the graver goes; the lessons also call a wave's throw its depth, and the throw is unchanged.) Third, textures from real dials, looked up (Oracle of Time's "Guilloché dial styles", Monochrome's technical perspective on guilloché, Breguet's pages on guilloché, and OrnamentalTurning.co.uk's notes on rosette profiles and phasing, where "watered silk" is very fine divisions, a small shift between cuts): waves (vagues), sunburst (soleil), watered silk (moiré), fish scale (écailles), flinqué (the rays cut under an enamel), flames (flammé) and basket weave (panier), seven plates on three kinds of cut new to the engine: the straight engine with a wave on its bar, rays (`rays`, with `sweep` and a wave), and the weave (`basket`). Each has a lesson, a watch (ocean and steel, copper sunburst, pearl and platinum, jade and gold, cobalt enamel, ember and bronze, sand and titanium) and clues for its new knobs; the old Basket plate, a second rosette on a ring, is the Woven ring now. Progress is kept by plate: a plate with stars stays open, the new ones open in turn from the one before, and the plate in hand is found again by its name (set afresh where its knobs changed, as on straight lines, which gained Depth).

**Straight lines** (24 October 2026). Korbi: "too many of the starting levels are increasingly complex rosette engine types. Instead it should start with one to show off how cool it can be, but then quickly show off straight stuff as well - maybe a Paris one to see how those pyramids are made". The straight-line engine is a second kind of cut (`kind: "lines"` in a level, `linePasses` in `guilloche-engine.js`): not a ring but a family of parallel grooves, one every `pitch` (a fiftieth of the plate's radius a step, 2 to 12) in a direction `angle` (0 to 175 in fives), each a chord of the plate, with `cross` a second family at that angle to the first (0 is none, then 15 degrees at a time to 90). At 90 degrees two sets of V-grooves leave square pyramids between them: clous de Paris, hobnail, which is how the pyramids are made, and it is why the Paris level sits third. A rosette's wave (`a`, `k`, `shape`, `index`) can be run along the lines, each line's wave turned on by `index` as a rose's passes are, which is a barleycorn's zigzag. A cut can have `holes` (centre and radius; the lines stop at the edge, so a medallion can sit in the middle of a field, as the medallion plate does) and a `fill`, the share of the pitch a groove takes in the picture, which only the drawing uses. The levels now run rose, straight lines, Paris, then the rings. Progress made in the old order is kept: a plate you have engraved stays open whatever stood before it, and a run kept for a plate under the old order (it has no plate name, `id`) is let go rather than put on another plate.

What's checked for lines is not by picture alone: a step of pitch or angle shifts every line, so as a picture a plate is 0 percent like its target until the last tap, which tells nothing. So the line under the plate, for a cut of lines, is by how far each knob is from the target's (a quarter of its range off is none alike), the cuts of rings stay by picture, and a plate of several cuts is the mean of its cuts. Whether the plate is done is still by picture, exact. The 3D watch drew lines with a shader of their own (a field that sloped into the nearest line of each set), and from 24 October cut them a line at a time behind the needle (Korbi: for straight lines "the actual cuts aren't really shown - the animation seems disconnected from the product"); since 27 October every kind of cut is drawn as grooves in the one way described under The watch, and the needle runs each pass as the engine has it, so a groove grows from its start behind the tip, the lines fill in one after another, and the pyramids appear as the second set crosses the first. Each of the new plates has a watch: ice and bronze (lines), antique gold hobnail (Paris), teal and titanium (barleycorn), olive and platinum (medallion), and the first plate's, salmon and rose gold, is what an unknown plate is given.

**Registers** (23 October 2026). Korbi: later plates should "allow some more complex watch face designs that have different types of Guilloche at different areas of the face". A cut can now sit away from the centre: it has `at`, where its centre is, and `size`, its radius, in plate radii, and everything about it (its radius, throw, feed) is cut to that scale, so a register, a small dial of its own, is set exactly as a whole plate is (`passes` in the engine; the cut's settings carry both). Three plates after the master dial use them: Registers (a pointed rim round a rose in the left register and a ribbon in the right), Chrono (a scalloped rim and three registers: a rose, a woven basket and a ribbon) and Grand (a barleycorn rim, a hub and three registers, five cuts, a second rosette in three of them). Each is a chronograph's layout, and each register is told apart from its neighbours by its kind as well as its count and depth, which is what reading a face is. A level may name its tabs (`names`: Rim, Left, Right, Base, Hub) instead of Centre, Band and Rim; each register is edged with a fine ring of its own, in 3D and flat, and the ring that a clue or lesson draws goes round the whole register. The test checks that a register's pass is the plain pass scaled and set at its centre, that the fullest cut a register can make stays inside it, that the registers do not touch each other, and that the rim clears them. Each has a lesson and a watch (burgundy and bronze, glacier and steel, aubergine and gold). Their stars still open each plate after the one before. A register's grooves are fine, so the picture's cells count for less there: a step of any knob was told apart in all twenty-four seeds tried, but the margin on Grand is thin (the worst neighbour is 0.985 alike against the 0.99 that finishes a plate), so the knobs of the smaller registers are the first to look at if players find a plate "nearly right but not".

**Lessons and clues** (23 October 2026). Korbi: "add clues and lessons". A plate that has not been taught opens first on its lesson (once; "Skip the lesson" is always there, and the menu's "Lesson for this plate" opens it again): the same machine with no target, and a short run of steps, each saying what to set on which cut and then what that did ("This is one pass of a rose engine. Turn Lobes up to 6", then "Six lobes on the rosette, six waves in the ring"). The step's knob glows (colour only, nothing moves), the step is done when its knobs are where it asked, and Next appears then; the last step is "Cut the plate", which leaves the lesson for the real plate. A plate counts as taught once seen or once it has a star. Only knobs the plate leaves free are used, because the fixed ones of the dial plates cannot be turned.

A clue (the button under the plate, with its cost beside it) is given in three tiers about the knob that, set right, would make the engraving most like the target (`worstKnob`: each knob in turn put to its answer, the best gain of `alike` taken). Tier 1 says where to look (that cut's tab and that knob glow); asking again about the same knob says which way and roughly how far, or, for the rosette's shape, what its edge looks like; the third says the setting. A clue about another knob starts again at tier 1, and touching a knob clears the clue about it. Stars are capped by the deepest tier asked, `CLUE_CAP = [3, 3, 2, 1]`: no clue or a where-to-look still earns three, a direction two, the answer one. The plate's stars, and the clues used, are kept with the best and shown in the dialog at the end.

**The watch.** Korbi: "completely overhaul the visuals so it's a beautiful 3d rendering of what the user is creating and different levels have different watch face colours and cases". The plate is now the dial of a wristwatch lying on a walnut bench, and each plate is a different watch (`WATCHES` in `guilloche-watch.js`, pure data the tests check): silver sunburst in steel, champagne in gold, salmon in rose gold with a coin-edged bezel, midnight blue in platinum, racing green two-tone with a stepped bezel, slate in titanium in an eight-sided case with screws, ivory in platinum, and onyx with a fluted gold bezel. Each has a dial finish (sunburst, satin, lacquer or grain), case metal and bezel, hands, markers and a strap (leather, crocodile, rubber or suede) of its own.

What you cut is real relief: each pass is a groove of V section as wide as it is deep, three vertices a point (its two edges and its floor), each wall lit as polished metal against a studio of softboxes, so the glints run along the lattice and sweep as the watch is tilted. Where grooves cross, what shows is the lowest surface, as on the plate, where one cut takes away what another left: the grooves are drawn straight after the dial, opaque, with the depth test turned round (the farther surface wins), so the deepest cut at each point is the one drawn, and crossing V's leave ridges and pyramids by themselves (a clous de Paris is two sets of lines and nothing else). That holds because the camera never looks flatter than the walls slope (it stops at 50 degrees above the dial; the walls are at 28): a ray meets the cut surface once, and every groove surface it passes on the way is in air another cut took away. The V is drawn a quarter as deep as it is lit, so the grooves don't swim as the watch tips. The target's ghost lies a hair under the dial's face and is drawn only where it is the farther surface, so it shows on the uncut face and your own grooves hide it; the machine's path is drawn over everything. Ribbons are rebuilt at most once a frame, since a dragged knob sends many changes a frame and a flinqué is tens of thousands of points. The rest is built in the same renderer: a lathe-turned case, lugs, crown and crystal, the dial's finish worked out in the shader (rays, rings, grain), the strap's stitches, scales and ridges, ridged hands with shadows on the dial. The suite's three.js bundle has no perspective camera, shader material or texture, so perspective is set on an orthographic camera's matrix and the shading goes in through `onBeforeCompile`. While a zone is cut, the cutter's needle rides the groove with a glow; when the last is cut true the hands, markers and crystal come in and the camera draws back to show the whole watch before the dialog.

Handling: a drag tilts and turns the watch; two fingers pinch to zoom and, moving together, slide the picture (a mouse: the wheel zooms, the right button or shift slides); a double tap puts it back; a hold (without moving) shows the target alone. All of it works on the finished watch too, which is what "Look at it" in the dialog is for: zoom runs to six times (it was lost once the watch was drawn back, which was a bug), so the grooves can be looked at close. The tilt stops short of straight down (86°, the idle sway included): looking straight down the camera has no "up", and the watch flipped to the other side at that one point (Korbi, 23 October 2026). The rosette is drawn in a gauge in the corner, a second small scene over a backdrop of its own. The cut a clue or lesson is about is ringed on the dial. A device without WebGL gets `guilloche-flat.js`, the same dial in its colour inside a metal ring, the engraving, the target ghost and the rosette, with the hands and markers drawn when finished (it has no tilt).

Saved state gains `guilloche:lessons` (the plates whose lesson has been seen). The plate in hand (`guilloche:run`) now also keeps the deepest clue tier asked and the clue given, so a partner watching sees them; `guilloche:jobs` still holds only the stars.

Not yet: the pumping engine's axial passes, the honeycomb (nid d'abeille), drapery and tapisserie, textures in the zone dials (a sunburst register, a flinqué centre), and your own free design on the engine with a plate to keep. Seen at 390×600, 390×664, 390×844, 820×1180 and 1180×820, in Codex day and night and in Office, in software WebGL (swiftshader), where the animation is too slow to judge: the cuts and the assembly were looked at frame by frame, not at speed. Played by taps and drags in a browser, not yet on a phone: how smooth the shaders run on a phone's GPU (a flinqué is the heaviest, some 80,000 vertices a ribbon), the slider's feel at a thumb's width and the orbit gesture are untested.
