# Dandan
Forgetful Fish (October 2026, from Korbi's list, https://tappedout.net/mtg-decks/daves-dandan/, read on 19 October 2026): a way of playing *Magic: The Gathering* with no deck-building, against a computer player or at a table with a friend on their own phone (README, Seats). It is a communal format: there are not two decks but **one library of eighty cards** that both players draw from and **one graveyard** that every used card goes to, so whatever you counter, discard or mill is the other player's to draw, flash back or fetch. The list has exactly eighty cards in twenty-five kinds (it names a sideboard of Fire // Ice, Mission Briefing and More or Less, which the format doesn't play and the game leaves out).
**To come** (Korbi, 2 November 2026: "separate app for Dandan is best"). Dandan is to be an app of its own: the largest game here (about 6,400 lines of page code, a test that takes 237 s) and one whose engine is to be perfected. Not built.


The cards are in `dandan-cards.js`, ten Dandâns, sixteen Islands and a deck of blue instants and sorceries around them. Their *cost and type* were read from the magicthegathering.io card database (Deem Inferior, which it lacks, from Scryfall) and their *rules text* from each card's Tappedout page, the same site as the list; nothing was written from memory, and the source of each is in the file. The text on screen is our own wording of it, as Cartel's and Hong's rules are; the game uses no Wizards of the Coast artwork, mana symbols or logos (a cost is drawn as small plain pips, blue and red in the suite's enamels, the generic cost grey), and the card names and the format belong to their makers.

The rules were checked card by card and against the format's own (October 2026): every card's Oracle text and rulings on Scryfall, and the format as Wizards of the Coast set it out with its Secret Lair Dandân deck (March 2026, "From the Chaos Vault"): one library and one graveyard, "your" library and graveyard being the shared ones; the owner of a spell or a permanent is the player who cast it (so Repulse returns a Dandân to its caster's hand, and Narset's Reversal a spell to its caster's); when both players draw at once the cards are dealt one at a time from the active player (Memory's seven); and a mulligan costs nothing the first time, and again for a hand with fewer than two lands or fewer than two spells, which may be shown and thrown back free as often as it comes (the free ones don't count); each mulligan after the first puts one more card on the bottom at the keep (the London mulligan, with the first one free). The rules in short: 20 life each, the first to play skips their first draw, a land a turn, seven cards in hand at the end of yours. The Dandân (4/1, two blue) is the only card that does damage and is the whole game: it **can't attack unless the defending player controls an Island, and is sacrificed the moment its own controller controls none**. So the game is about Islands: your own keep your Dandâns alive, theirs are what let you attack, and the deck is full of ways to bounce, fetch, bend or lose one. Memory Lapse counters a spell by putting it on top of the *shared* library, so the next player to draw gets it (the one who countered it on the opponent's turn, usually); Mind Bend swaps a basic land type in a permanent's text for good (a Dandân told to look for a Swamp is sacrificed at once, an Island told to be a Mountain taps for red); Commit and Deem Inferior put a permanent second from the top; Memory, from the graveyard, shuffles both hands and the graveyard into the library and deals each seven. Mana is spent from lands that untap only on their owner's turn, so mana left open on your own turn is the counterspell on theirs.

What the game does for you: priority and the stack are the real ones (both pass in turn, the last spell cast resolves first), but you are asked only where you could act: your own main phases, the attack and the blocks, the end step when you hold something castable, whenever there is a spell on the stack you could answer, and at an upkeep when the card about to be drawn is worth acting on (you know what it is and could draw it first, bury it or shuffle it away, or it is the other player's draw and you hold a Brainstorm, to put two cards of your choosing on top for them, or a Predict, to mill it) or when you hold an untapped Flood Plain (below). Tap a card to see what it does and what it costs, and a spell that needs a target lights the legal ones on the table; mana is paid from the lands that fit, choosing for you the ones you will need least (a dual land last), what a land makes and a cost doesn't use (Izzet Boilerworks' red, paying for a blue spell) floats in your pool, shown beside your lands, until the step ends, Menu, Hold priority when I cast, lets you cast, or fetch or crack a Clue, twice before the other player may answer, and a card you cycle always holds it (below), and Menu, Choose which lands to tap, has you choose the lands that pay (below). The shared library shows how many cards it holds and, when a card on top is known to both (a countered spell, the top three Halimar Depths showed), which; a card you saw go into the other player's hand (drawn from a top you both knew, bounced, found by a search that reveals it) is counted beside their hand size and named in the library's sheet, as a player at a real table would remember it. While a question is up (the mulligan, the cards to put on the bottom, discard or put back, the order of the top three, a surveil) a card tapped opens in a sheet of its own, its full text large, with the question's choice for it ("Put on the bottom", "Put back on top") as the sheet's button; a card in a question's list (a land to fetch, a card to name) has a ? beside it that does the same. A question with a list (which land to bounce, which card to name for Predict) is a sheet that can be put away to look at the table and brought back with the Answer button.

What the rules review of October 2026 put right: the upkeep had no stop at all (so a card countered onto the top could never be drawn first in its owner's upkeep, and a Brainstorm could never hand the other player two chosen cards); mana a land made and a cost didn't use was lost instead of floating to the end of the step; Mystic Sanctuary's card was chosen as the trigger resolved instead of as it went on the stack, where the other player can answer it knowing the target; a permanent that left the battlefield and came back was still the old object to a spell aimed at it and to its Dandân's sacrifice trigger; a copy from Narset's Reversal whose target had gone wasn't offered the one other target there was; a bounced card, a card drawn from a top both players had seen and a card found by a search that reveals it were forgotten at once, where a player at a table knows where they went; and (found on 20 October) a Mystic Retrieval flashed back could choose itself as its target, though it is on the stack by the time its target is chosen (`targetLists`' `self`; another Retrieval in the graveyard it may choose).
The window before the draw (30 October 2026, Korbi: "There's a window before draw where flood plain can be triggered to fetch. The user should be prompted to pass here before the draw to have the option to fetch"): an untapped Flood Plain, with an Island or Plains card still in the library, stops the game at every upkeep from the second turn, your own and theirs, with the player whose turn it is asked first and the other after, and the Pass button reads "To your draw" or "Let them draw". Before this the upkeep stopped only for a seat that knew the top card or held a Brainstorm or Predict, so a Flood Plain alone was never offered: the fetch, which shuffles the library, had to be made at the end step before, or not at all. The stop doesn't depend on what you know of the top card (a stop that came only then would be a hint), nor on Show revealed cards. The computer players pass it without a thought (`botMove` in `dandan-bot.js`), and a game played out in the head, by Hard and by the coach (quiet), leaves it out (`wants`, `fetchWindow` in `dandan-engine.js`), so the computer's play and the coach's verdicts are as they were. Cracking it there puts the Island onto the battlefield and shuffles the library, and the draw follows from the shuffled library (`tests/dandan-window.mjs`).
Every upkeep where you could act (31 October 2026, Korbi: "before draw, if user can do anything, including cast predict or any instant speed action, they need to be able to choose to not do that. just happened that I could have cast predict but game continued to draw automatically"): from the second turn, an upkeep asks a seat that could do anything at all (`anyUpkeepMove`: any instant, a card to cycle, a Flood Plain, a Clue), your own and theirs, the player whose turn it is first, and the Pass button reads "To your draw" or "Let them draw" as before. Until now it asked only with a known top card to act on, a Brainstorm or Predict held for their draw, or a Flood Plain, so a Predict held for your own draw with the top card unknown, or a Quicken, was passed over for you. It is for the person only: the computer players and the look-ahead leave it out (as the Flood Plain's window is), and the computer is asked only where it has a known card to act on or a Brainstorm or Predict for your draw, and passes anywhere else, so its play and its speed are as they were. The one thing that is not "anything" is a Flood Plain with no Island or Plains card left in the library to fetch: cracking it would only shuffle, so it asks no more than it did (`emptyCrack`; `tests/dandan-window.mjs` found it when my first version asked there, and the push's run failed on it).

Where it still plays differently from the paper game, and why (also on the Rules sheet, in the menu):
- There is no stop at the beginning or end of combat or in the draw step, and an upkeep is a stop whenever you could do anything before the draw (above): what could be done in those steps is done as well at the declarations either side of them or at the end step, and each stop is a tap for nothing. The one play lost: at the beginning of combat, Mind Bending your own last Island so that a Dandân can't attack you (yours dying with it).
- Combat damage among several blockers is assigned for you (enough to kill each in turn); you choose blocks, not the split.
- Predict names no player: it mills the one library whichever it names. Mind Bend changes basic land types; no permanent in this deck has a colour word for it to change.
- Mystic Sanctuary's "may" is answered when its card is chosen, as it triggers: choosing none is declining.
- Holding priority is the cast flag in the menu above and not a separate step.
- Flashback (Mystic Retrieval) and the aftermath half of Commit // Memory are cast from the shared graveyard, so either player may; they are exiled afterwards as printed.
- The opening hands are dealt alternately from the first player, and the first player settles their mulligans before the second decides (paper has both declare in turn each round; with one shuffled library it comes to the same). The loser of the last game goes first, a lot decides the first.
- State-based checks (a Dandân with no toughness left or no Island, a player at 0 life or drawing from an empty library) run once a resolving spell has finished asking its questions, not between them, so a half-resolved spell is never judged.

The engine (`dandan-engine.js`, `dandan-core.js`, `dandan-spells.js`) is pure functions on a plain JSON state, as Seats asks: `deal`, `toMove`, `act`, `moves`, `over`, `restore` (the database drops empty lists, so a state read back is made whole), with `dandan-rules.js` the module `seats.js` runs. The state keeps what each player knows (`kn`, the library cards each has seen, by position; `kh`, the cards each knows to be in the other's hand), each player's floating mana (`pool`, emptied at every step), what each player's play has shown the table (`obs`, every pass read, and in a game from before them `tells`, below, with `draws` counting the cards each has drawn), and an object number on each permanent (`oid`) that a target and a trigger carry, so a card that left and came back is not the one they meant; `act(…, { quiet })` keeps no log and copies nothing, for the games the computer plays out in its head; in a real game the engine keeps a copy of the game at each pass that can show what a hand lacks (`passPoints`), which `dandan-rules.js` reads once the move is made. Every random draw is from the game's seed and the move's number. The cards' effects are one table (`RES`), the questions a seat can be asked (mulligan, bottom, attack, block, discard, put back, order, surveil, search, pick, owner, bend, name, retarget) are `pending` in the state, and `moves()` lists a seat's legal moves one to a card name, which is what the page's buttons and the computer player's choices both come from, so neither can offer a move the rules refuse.

The computer players (Menu, Computer player: Easy, Medium, Hard, Hard by default; rebuilt in October 2026, when the first ones were found Mind Bending a lone Island on the first turn, reworked again on 20 October, when Hard cast Mystic Retrieval from its hand with Korbi's red on the table and a Piracy Charm in the graveyard, and lost its Dandân to it, and rebuilt on 21 October around a search of both players' choices and a reading of every pass, Korbi asking that it stop being taught habit by habit and assume the other player plays well, in the hand it deals them and in the play it expects of them). All three play from what their seat may know: their hand, the table, the graveyard and exile, the library cards they have seen go there, and the cards they have seen go into the other hand; the rest are the cards they haven't seen, in the other hand or the library at random.

Medium (`dandan-policy.js`) plays by the habits of a good player of the format, worked out afresh at each decision, with no look ahead. It weighs everything in cards (an average card in hand is 1): what a card held is worth by itself and to the hand it's in (a land by how many lands its holder has), a Dandân on the battlefield by whether it can attack (the other player controls its land type) and how safe it is (its last Island), a spell on the stack by what it does for whoever cast it, and the chance the other hand holds a card, counted from the copies not yet seen and the size of that hand. The format's turns of the screw are in it. The library is shared, so a countered spell goes on top for whoever draws next, which is you on their turn and them on yours: it counters their spells on their turn, and holds its own threats back from open mana on its own. Cards put back on top are chosen for who will draw them (Brainstorm's two, Halimar Depths' three, a surveil, Mystic Sanctuary's card), and a known card on top is drawn first in the other player's upkeep. Lands untap only on their owner's turn, so on its own turn it keeps two open for a Memory Lapse it holds (one more for a Piracy Charm or Mind Bend while their Dandân could attack), and spends what's left at the end of theirs. Removal waits for a Dandân to attack, or for the end of their turn, unless they're tapped out on its own turn (nothing can counter it then, which their turn won't be), and the more their next attack would hurt, the sooner it answers. On its own turn Gone Missing or Metamorphose has them draw their own card back instead of a new one, and Deem Inferior is a kill (its owner bottoms it rather than hand it over as the second card). Mind Bend is for a Dandân or for the last Island under one, never for an Island alone. Islands cut both ways: its own keep its Dandâns, and are what let theirs attack it, so with no Dandân of its own it prefers a land that isn't one. It attacks and blocks by the race: what they could swing back with, and a block not made when taking the hit and swinging back wins the game. Easy is the same player made careless: it taps out on its own turn, casts what it can when it can, counters whatever it can, aims removal at anything, attacks with everything, blocks only a lethal attack, and plays a random move one time in eight.

The shared graveyard. A spell cast from the hand ends in the graveyard both players use, and a Mystic Retrieval there is anyone's with a red source and three mana (Thundering Falls, Izzet Boilerworks, an Island Mind Bent into a Mountain). So Medium weighs every cast with what it leaves the other player there (`gift` in `dandan-policy.js`): the best card their Retrieval would find on their next turn, flashed back with their red or cast from their hand (at the chance they hold one), a removal spell they could cast the same turn counted at what it would do to our best Dandân, against what they could find already. Our own Memory Lapse with two left open through their turn halves it (the Retrieval may be countered), and a cast on their turn counts it at six tenths (our own turn comes first). The same arithmetic runs the other way: taking away the card they'd find, or flashing the Retrieval back ourselves so it's exiled, is worth what it denies them; and a Retrieval for a removal spell with the mana left to cast it at once (an instant any time, a sorcery in our main phase) is worth what that spell does now, not what a card in hand is worth. So Medium no longer casts the Retrieval into their red, flashes theirs back for the Charm and kills the Dandân with it the same turn, and takes the Charm out of their reach when it has red of its own. Two smaller habits changed with it: a sorcery-speed removal spell (Gone Missing, Deem Inferior) is no longer held back for a better moment, which for a sorcery only comes after their attack; and a land is weighed by the mana it adds when that mana is wanted, to cast now or to keep open through their turn (a tapped land, or an Izzet Boilerworks that has to return an untapped land, counts against it then). These positions are rare, and Medium against the Medium before them won 1,015 of 2,000 games; its other weights (a Dandân's worth, a land's taken away), tried higher and lower over 2,000 games each, moved nothing beyond chance either: Medium's results hang on its habits more than on its numbers.

Mana (21 October 2026; in Korbi's game Hard Metamorphosed his Island just after his Izzet Boilerworks had returned one to his hand, and he put that one straight back down: Metamorphose lets the player whose permanent it takes put a land or creature from their hand onto the battlefield). Medium counted any land taken away at 0.45 of a card, early or late. What mana is worth was measured instead with the judge (below), over Medium's games: a land taken from the battlefield, against a spell taken from the hand. The n-th mana of a player's lands is worth 1.75 cards up to three, 1.55 at four, 1.35 at five, 1.05 at six, 0.7 at eight, 0.45 at ten and next to nothing past that (`MANA_LOSS` in `dandan-policy.js`); a land card in hand, put down in place of one taken, 1.3 early to 0.2 late when it's the only one, 0.9 to 0.17 when there are others (`LAST_LAND`, `SPARE_LAND`). So slowing the other player's mana is worth a great deal until their lands are all down, and nothing much after. Metamorphose or Gone Missing on their land is weighed by it (`landAway`): the mana it takes, an Izzet Boilerworks two; drawn next by us, the land is gone from them for good and ours in place of a card at random (worth less to a hand that holds a land already); drawn by them, they play it again in place of a new land and lose their draw, about a card by the judge whatever the turn. Metamorphose's offer is counted: a land in their hand (certain when it's one they were seen to get back, else at the odds from the unseen cards and a turn they ended with no land played, `landOdds`) undoes the mana taken but for what the land put down doesn't make up, and costs them the land from their hand; a Dandân goes down rather where an Island will stand under it, priced apart as before. And an Izzet Boilerworks of theirs waiting on the stack to return a land (`manaDrop`): taken in answer to its trigger, the Boilerworks costs them its two mana while they still return a land for it, and that land isn't yet in their hand to put back down; an Island taken then can leave the Boilerworks to go back itself. In Korbi's position Medium now takes the Boilerworks in answer to the trigger (scored 1.6 cards; an Island 0.2), and at the end of that turn, with the Island in his hand, the Boilerworks again (1.9; the Island 0.04, less than passing); Hard's weighing agrees (63% to win against 56% for the Island and 55% passing). Their land is taken at the end of their turn when their play showed no land in hand, and no card is spent on one once they have ten lands down. Medium against the Medium before it won 1,020 of 2,000 games, and Hard against the Hard before it 53 of 100: no difference beyond chance, the positions being rare. Easy still counts a land as a land. Fixed with it: offered Metamorphose's choice with a Dandân in hand and no Island to keep it, the computer put nothing down even holding another land; it puts the land down now.

Hard (`dandan-think.js`) looks ahead, searching both players' choices. At each decision it takes the moves worth weighing (Medium's own choice, passing, and the other moves Medium scores within a card of it, seven at most; for a question, its reasonable answers: every set of attackers up to three, each block, every order of three cards, the pairs a Brainstorm could put back), and plays each out to the start of the turn after next in up to 64 deals of the cards it can't see. Below each move, the decisions that follow it, the other player's and its own, are a tree shared by all that move's deals (information-set Monte Carlo tree search): at each, the player to move picks among its four likeliest moves (Medium's own first, then passing and the best-scored others) by how each has done for that player so far, an upper confidence bound (the average, and a margin for the moves tried less) counting a move only in the deals where it was there to make (a card the other hand doesn't hold in that deal can't be cast in it). So each side comes to play what does best for it there; before 21 October Medium's habit moved both seats, and only the other player's first main phase was searched. A decision's choices widen as it's visited (at first only Medium's move, then the next, as the square root of its visits), each deal grows the tree by one decision and Medium plays on from there, and a decision's moves are kept by who decides and where as well as by the move, since in another deal the same line can reach another seat's decision first. (The deals know Hard's own hand, so the other player's choices are made against it: every move looks a little worse for Hard than it is, all alike.) Where each line lands is judged by the chance of winning from there (the judge, below), which is the rest of the game as these players would play it. Played out ten turns instead, Medium's habits moving both seats for the eight past the tree, it played worse (25 of 70 games against the Hard before it): those turns give the judge's estimate with far more noise, and what sees further is better play inside the projection, which is what the tree adds. A deal keeps every card the seat knows where it is and deals the rest by what their play has shown (below); what the other player knew of the library stays with the places they knew. The deals and the rest of the game's luck are the same for every move, so the moves are compared on the same footing, and a move that falls clearly behind stops being played out: by twice the standard error of its gap, deal by deal, plus a hundredth, the spread taken as at least 0.06 (over the first few deals two moves can come out alike by chance, and in one position the best move was dropped that way after six). The best is chosen among the moves still in the race, never one dropped on a lucky few deals. Medium's own move stands unless another beats it by more than the deals can explain: what two turns can't show, a land drop kept back for nothing above all (the judge counts a land in hand nearly as much as one down), is in Medium's habits. Letting the search decide alone played as well against the Hard before it (58 of 100 games either way) but now and then kept a land back on a gap that was chance (10 of 52 cards its passes were read to rule out were in its hand, all but one a land); with Medium's move standing, none of 26. Its mulligans, its cards to the bottom and its names for Predict are Medium's. The seed comes from what the seat sees, so the same position gets the same move on any phone. On the page it thinks in a worker (`dandan-worker.js`), with the runner held until the move is in (seats-ui.js's `hold`), so the table never stops; at a table, the phone that runs it thinks on its own thread. On this machine a decision takes 59 ms at the median, 268 ms one time in ten and 0.7 s at worst, about what it took before: a play-out down the tree costs half of one of the old (Medium's line and the other player's four answers), so twice the deals fit. A phone is perhaps four times slower, and 2.5 seconds is the backstop.

Thinking (Menu, Thinking, shown while the computer is Hard or the coach is on: Quick, Long, Deep, Deepest; Quick by default; 21 October 2026, Korbi asking that what Hard thought then be the least, and that it could be set to think much deeper; the coach with it from 22 October): the deals each move is played out in, four times as many at each step, Quick's 64 (the Hard measured here), Long's 256, Deep's 1,024 and Deepest's 4,096. More deals are a deeper search, not only a surer average: each deal grows each move's tree by a decision, so the lines that matter are searched further for both players (at Quick's 64 a few decisions down, at Deep's 1,024 twice as far), and the moves are told apart on smaller gaps. With them came a guard: a pass at the end of the main phases never outweighs the land Medium would play. Where Medium played a land, the search kept it back in 3 or 4 of 96 positions at every depth, by a point or two of the judge's (which counts a land in hand nearly as one down); kept back at the first main phase it can still go down after combat, but at the second the drop is lost for good, and at Deep, where the margin Medium's move needs to be overruled shrinks with the deals, 5 of 86 turns ended with a land kept back against Quick's 2 of 87. The look stays at the start of the turn after next at every setting, the tree now reaching all of it (it stopped there anyway): played further, more of what is judged is Medium's habit, which is noise to the judge (above). On this machine a decision takes, at the median, one time in ten and at worst: Quick 55 ms, 0.3 s and 0.8 s; Long 0.12 s, 1 s and 3 s; Deep 0.26 s, 4 s and 15 s; Deepest about four times Deep. A phone is perhaps four times slower, which is what the menu's line under the choice says to expect, and the backstop on the time grows with the deals (2.5 s, 12 s, 40 s, two minutes), so a slow phone stops short of the deals rather than keeping you waiting longer. Most decisions come out the same at any depth (over three games Medium's move stood at 470 of 548 at Quick, 464 at Long and 460 at Deep): it is the close ones that more deals settle. Against Quick, over seeded games each played from both seats, Long won 45 of 100, no better within the luck of 100 games, Deep 60 of 90 (67%, give or take 5), thinking about two minutes a game here to Quick's ten seconds, and Deepest 14 of 20 (70%, give or take 10), thinking eight or nine minutes a game: no surer a gain than Deep's, at four times the wait. The coach thinks at the same setting (22 October 2026, Korbi finding the coach at Deepest no deeper than before; `coachBudget`): Hard's deals there and its backstop on the time, never fewer than its own 96 deals (so at Quick it is as it was, and never the weaker of the two), over its wider nine moves, and in about Hard's time (at Deep, over a game's decisions, 0.96 s on average here to Hard's 1.1 s: what the two moves more cost, pruning wins back). A change of setting while either is working something out starts it again at the new depth (their workers are stopped and new ones asked; what they owed is answered as stale, not as a failure), and a Hint asked for is asked again. At a table the phone that runs the computer thinks on its own thread, at Quick; each phone's coach at its own setting.

How they measure, over seeded games with each side taking each seat in turn (scratch runs, not kept in the repository; since 20 October each deal is played twice, the seats swapped, so its luck falls to both alike): the Hard of 21 October (the tree, every pass read) beat the Hard before it in 57 of 100 games; the tree alone, in 58 of 100, and letting the search decide alone in 58 of 100 (55 of 100 with every pass read too): 228 of 400 over the four, 57% give or take 2.5; played out ten turns, 25 of 70. The Hard of 20 October (the other player's answers tried, the network judge, Medium's graveyard) beat the Hard before it in 70 of 100 games and Medium in 36 of 40; trying the answers alone was worth 47 of 80 games, the network judge alone 31 of 60. Before that, Hard beat Medium in 99 of 120 games, and the computer player that came before (the old Hard) in 91 of 100. Medium beat the old Hard in 234 of 300 and Easy in 237 of 300; Easy beat a player of random legal moves 195 times in 200, Medium all 200. A game between Medium players runs about 25 turns. Some things that were tried and didn't pay: looking three turns ahead instead of two (55 of 100 against two, at half as much time again), twice the deals (48 of 100), and eleven more things for the judge to read (the log loss moved in the fourth place). How well any of them does against a person is not measured.

What their play shows (the reads, `dandan-observe.js` and `dandan-reads.js`; on 21 October widened from four hand-written tells to every pass, Korbi asking that the hand dealt the other player be one consistent with good play at every decision they have made, not a random one). In a real game the engine keeps a copy of the game at every pass made at a moment that can show what a hand lacks: an opposing spell on the stack, the end of one's own main phases, the end of the other player's turn, an attack about to be damaged; whether the player was asked there or passed over for having nothing to do (the other player can't tell those apart either). After each move `dandan-rules.js` reads them (`readPass`), from the other seat's side: for each card it hasn't seen (all the lands as one), a hand holding it is dealt as that seat would deal it, and the best move it gives, by Medium's scores, is set against the pass. When that move looks worth two fifths of a card more, both are played out from the passing player's side in up to six deals (the reader's own hand dealt as the passer would deal it, so nothing the passer couldn't know decides it), Medium moving both seats, and judged: with a spell on the stack the card Medium likes most there, to the start of the turn after next (a counter's worth shows when the card it put on top is drawn), elsewhere the two it likes most, to the start of the next turn; the card is ruled out of the hand held then when its move comes out ahead by more than 5 points of the chance of winning and the deals' error. Moves that come out alike show nothing: a player may have had a reason. A land not played by the end of the main phases is ruled out by rule: the lost drop shows over many turns, not one (to the judge a land in hand counts nearly as much as one down), and nothing is gained by keeping one back. What is read is kept in the game (`obs`: the seat, the cards it had drawn then, the names ruled out, and what the pass let by, for the coach's words), and stops counting once the whole hand could have been drawn since. A read takes 2 to 13 ms here on average, by the game, and a few tens at most (most passes show nothing: their cards couldn't be cast there, or Medium wouldn't), on the page's own thread, a move's passes with it. In Medium's games, where the reads can be checked against the hands really held, not one card ruled out was held (75 in four games), nor in Hard's (26 in three). They are read by Bayes' rule: a hidden hand is as likely as before, times the chance a player holding it would have shown what the table saw, which is 1 when it holds none of what a read rules out, and otherwise the chance that all of it came with the draws since (k such cards among n in hand, m drawn since: C(m, k)/C(n, k)), plus the chance the player held it back anyway (its slip). The kept hand: two to five lands in a seven, before and after the free first mulligan (a hand with fewer than two lands or spells is thrown back for nothing), at least one in a six; the lands it has had (in hand and played) must fit that, for its first four draws. Hard's deals deal the other hand by them (a deal kept with that chance, dealt again up to 24 times, else the likeliest), and Medium's odds on a card in their hand read the same (the chance from the cards drawn since, or the slip); the coach says so where it decides a move ("They let your last Dandân resolve with a Lapse's mana open, and haven't drawn since: they very likely hold no Memory Lapse"; "Your last attack got through with their blue open, and they haven't drawn since: they very likely hold no answer to it"). The slips are set for people: a card a pass rules out is held anyway one time in seven, a land one time in twenty, a hand kept outside the bounds one time in ten. Medium never slips (in 2,000 games against itself, 42 of 17,644 turns ended with no land played had one in hand, none of 7,284 passes on a Dandân a Lapse, none of 5,370 unanswered attacks an answer, 2 of 4,000 kept hands were outside two to five lands). A game begun before the reads of passes keeps the four tells the engine kept until then (a hand kept, a turn with no land, a pass on a Dandân with a Lapse's mana open, an attack let through with blue open), read the same way; between computer players those made no difference beyond chance (Hard with them against the Hard before them won 48 of 100 games): what they read is a person's play. Writing the tells found that the weighing shared the game's draw counts and tells with its deals, so the deals' games added to them: each deal now has its own, and a test checks the weighed game is left exactly as it was.

The judge (`dandan-eval.js`): the chance a seat wins from the start of a turn, as Medium against Medium would play it on: a score of the situation learned from results, by a small neural network (a value network). It reads 100 numbers off the position. For each side: the hits it can take (life in fours, and how close to the last), its Dandâns on the table, those that can attack and those on their last Island, its lands, Islands and having none, red, the mana its lands make and what of it is open, Clues, and its hand, by size and card by card (each of seventeen spells and seven kinds of land). And what both share: whose turn it is, the library's size, the graveyard card by card, the turn. The network scores a position from one side (the 100 numbers, two hidden layers of 32 and 16, rectified); a seat's chance is the logistic of its score less the other's, so the two always add up to one. It was trained on 1.3 million positions, the start of every turn of 45,000 games of Medium against Medium (one move in twelve and a half one of its three best at random, for variety), each with its game's result, by Adam on the log loss, with a tenth of the games kept out to check it: there its log loss is 0.542, against 0.582 for the logistic model of thirty-five features it replaced (fitted the same way earlier in October) and 0.693 for a coin. Bigger networks did no better (0.543 with layers of 64 and 32, 0.544 with 128 and 64): the results are as noisy as the game. Its weights are rounded to signed bytes with a scale for each output (6 KB of base64 in the file), which moves a chance by a third of a point on average and leaves the log loss as it was. Hard plays about as well with it as with the old model (a first network of the kind beat it in 31 of 60 games of Hard against Hard); what it changes is the figures, a Dandân, a land or a card now worth what it is worth in that hand at that point of the game, and so the chances the coach shows. A game played out in the head has dealt the other hand, so the judge reads both.

The coach (Menu, Coach: Off, Hints, Check, Always, Review; Hints by default), as Hold'em's. Hints: nothing until you ask; Hint, in the coach's line above the buttons, gives its move for the decision you're at with the one thing that decides it and its chance of winning ("Cast Memory Lapse on their Dandân · Countered now, their Dandân goes on top, and you draw it · 73% to win"), and Why? after it: every move it weighed, its own first, each with its chance of winning, ± (the standard error: how far an average over that many deals may stray by chance), the deals it was played in, and in how many of them the coach's move did better and worse. Check: no Hint (22 October 2026, Korbi: the decision is yours, and only then set against the coach's; until then Check had Hint too): each move of yours is weighed the moment you make it, and after one it would not have made, its word in oxblood, so it isn't taken for advice: how much worse it reckons yours, and why ("Casting Mind Bend on their Island: about 10 points worse than playing Island (63% to win against 72%). An Island turned into a Swamp still makes mana for them: keep Mind Bend for a Dandân, or for their last Island under one."). Always: the same, and at each of your decisions its move without asking, ringed on the table: the card in your hand, its target, the button, the row of a question's sheet. Off: nothing, and no Hint. A move counts as agreeing when it is the coach's or as good as near as the deals can tell (no more than 3 points worse, and not two standard errors clear of it), so a close call is never called a mistake; a word stays through the moves that follow it (a spell and the questions it asks) until another replaces it or the turn after next, and a game ends with how often it agreed with your decisions, Where it differed listing the three that cost most. Its reasons are worked out from the table, the move and the format's rules of thumb (`dandan-coach.js`, each about one thing, two about the same thing being one): who draws a countered card, the free mana at the end of their turn, the two kept for Memory Lapse, their open mana against a spell cast on your turn (with the chance they hold a Lapse), the last Island under a Dandân, a Mind Bend on an Island that kills nothing, a land taken from them by their mana ("Izzet Boilerworks makes two mana: taking it slows them the most"; "They hold the Island you know of: Metamorphose lets them put it straight down, so their mana doesn't drop"; ten lands down, no matter), a Dandân attacking you, the swing back, the race, a known top card drawn first, and the shared graveyard ("The Mystic Retrieval stays in the graveyard: their red flashes it back for the Piracy Charm, and your Dandân dies to it", "It brings back the Piracy Charm with the mana left to cast it: their Dandân is answered this turn"). Then what the deals showed (`evidence`): each deal remembers the other player's answer down the searched line (the first thing they do in their own main phase, or else their first move at all; a land is no answer) and where it left the game two turns on (a Dandân of yours on the table, one of theirs, both lives and hands), and the word cites the answer when it was the same in many of the deals and seldom after the better move ("Their best answer to yours, in 20 of the 64 deals: they cast Mystic Retrieval for Piracy Charm with flashback"), and the biggest difference in the better move's favour when it is big enough to say ("Two turns on, you have a Dandân on the table in 23% of the deals after yours; after playing Island, in 63%"), three reasons at most; the Why sheet the same for the coach's move against yours or the next best. Where neither applies, the word says how often each came out ahead ("Played out, passing came out ahead in 41 of the 64 deals and behind in 9"). It weighs a decision as Hard does, at the menu's Thinking (above: at Quick in up to 96 deals, 177 ms on average here and 1.1 s at worst; deeper, as many deals as Hard, and as long), wider (nine moves), in its own worker; the figures are Medium's chances, a model of these computer players and not of people, which is the point at this table and nothing to rely on at another. A partner's frame, watched from the games screen, coaches nobody; at a table with a friend each phone's coach is its own, and it sees only what its seat does.

The review (Menu, Coach: Review, and Menu, Review your play; 29 October 2026, Korbi: "in dandan -> settings add a review mode where 1) the coach explains the most common mistakes 2) you can review the plays that the coach disagreed with (5% or more)"; `dandan-review.js`). Review is a coach that shows nothing while you play: no Hint, no line above the buttons, no word after a move, only the weighing of each move of yours the moment you make it, as Check does, and what it finds kept under `dandan:review` (Check and Always keep it too; Hints and Off weigh nothing of yours, so there is nothing to keep). A move is kept as a play when the coach's came out 5 points or more ahead of it (`GAP`; the coach's own margin for calling a move a difference at all is 3 points and two standard errors, so the 3-to-5-point ones are weighed and counted as decisions but not kept as plays). Each play keeps its game, turn and size, the coach's words about it, and the table in a line (the turn, both lives, the hand held); the newest forty are kept, and the counts by theme go on past them (about 18 KB when full and 36 KB at the very most, against the 300 KB a mirrored key may be). The sheet (Menu, Review your play, with the number of plays kept) opens with how many decisions were weighed in how many games and how many were 5 points or more worse; then Your most common mistakes, the most frequent first: what it was about, how many plays and how many points worse on average, and the rule of thumb behind it, with Show these plays; then the plays, the costliest first or the newest first, of one theme or all; and Clear the review. What a mistake is about is the theme of the first thing the coach said against your move (every reason it can give has one, `THEMES` in `dandan-coach.js`: who draws the top card, casting into open mana, keeping the mana for Memory Lapse, taking their lands, Islands under Dandâns, attacking and blocking, and so on, each with a title and a rule of thumb written once and said under every play of that kind); a difference with no reason of its own is a pass where a play came out ahead, a play where keeping the card did, or the rest. The chances are the coach's, so they are Medium's model of the computer players and not of people, as above.

If it's only one land or only one non land then can also mulligan for free"): the first mulligan costs nothing, seven new cards and none to the bottom, so the Mulligan button says "free" on the first and "bottom 1", "bottom 2"… on those after it (and Keep, the cards it would put on the bottom). Cycling answered, and lands tapped by hand (23 October 2026, Korbi: "In dandan, cycling is an action that I should get to respond to. And: "when I have multiple different lands, there should be a 'manual tap' mode where I select what lands will be tapped. This is importantly since [Izzet] Boilerworks for instance should be pinging back tapped lands."). The first: a cycled card's cost is paid and the card discarded as the cost, and the draw (or islandcycling's search) goes on the stack, where the other player may answer it as before; but the one who cycled passed priority at once, and so could answer only if the other player, answering first, gave it back. The engine's `hold` (a move's flag, until now a cast's: the page sent it when Hold priority was on) now holds for a cycle and for an ability (a Flood Plain's fetch, a Clue) as well, `holdOrPass` in the engine, and the page always sends it for a cycled card (the Hold priority switch decides for casts and abilities, as it did for casts). With priority held the game stops at the cycler wherever it has a move to make (a Flood Plain to fetch with: the fetch resolves first, so the shuffle comes before the draw; another card to cycle: it draws first, which is how the top card is taken before it is shuffled away) and goes on by itself where it has none, so a cycle with nothing to answer it with costs no tap. Reading it as an answer to the other player's cycling needed no change: with the ability on the stack and a move at hand, the game has always asked. The second, the manual tap (Menu, Choose which lands to tap; `opts.manual`, off to begin with, kept in `dandan:opts`): the game by itself pays a cost from the lands that leave you the most (`payPlan`: a dual land last), which is what a hand wants most of the time and the wrong thing for a card like Izzet Boilerworks, which returns a land you control: the land to return is the one already tapped for the spell, and the lands to keep untapped are the ones for a Memory Lapse. With the switch on, a spell, a cycled card or a cracked Clue that costs mana waits for the lands to be chosen where there is a choice to make: the untapped lands differ (a Boilerworks and Islands, say) and some would stay out of the payment (with all of them needed, or all alike, there is nothing to choose, and it is paid as before). Conceding (Menu, Concede; 27 October 2026, Korbi asking for it): the game is lost there and then, whoever's decision it is, as a player of Magic may concede at any time, even while the computer thinks; the page asks first. The end of a game (29 October 2026, Korbi: "add win rate statistics, a chart that shows the game advantage/disadvantage, at the end of dandan"; then, the same evening, "the chart shouldn't just be after every turn but after every action, every resolved action should get a dot on the chart (and it should be by turn as well) but the user should be able to see which decision screwed them"): the sheet that opens when a game ends says who won and why, how the game went, where it turned, and the record. How it went is a chart (`dandan-advantage.js`) of the judge's chance (above) that you win, at the start of each turn and after each action that settled: a line between certain defeat and certain victory, verdigris where you were ahead of even and oxblood where you were behind, ending in a dot where the game did (green, red, or grey for a draw). A turn's start is a larger hollow dot with a tick and its number under the line, and the actions of that turn are small dots spread along it up to the next; "By turn" (above the chart, "Every action" the other; not saved, and only on a game that has actions noted) draws just the turns' starts, the chart as it was first. Touch the chart and drag along it, or step with the arrow keys, for a point: its turn, whose move it was, your chance before and after and the points it moved, and the words of the log for what happened (the first four lines). Under it, in words: how many turns you began ahead of, the lowest you won from (or the highest you lost from) at any point, and the biggest swing, the biggest single move. Forty-turn games thin the turn labels (never one touching "End") and shrink the dots.

What an action is: the engine notes a dot (`noteAction`, in `settle`) whenever the stack is empty, no question is open and the game is on, and the log has moved since the last entry (not counting a turn's own line). So a land is a dot when it comes down, a spell once it has resolved with everything cast in answer to it, an attack when it is declared, each block, and combat damage; a pass that only moves a step says nothing. A spell is noted after it resolves because that is where the judge is sure: scored against how 200 games of Medium against Medium ended (log loss, lower is better), its positions after an action settled score 0.517, a turn's start (where it was learned) 0.539, and one with a spell still on the stack 0.550. The dot belongs to the seat that began the action (`s.by` in the state: the seat that made a move with nothing on the stack; a pass never begins one, and an answer, even a counterspell, doesn't take it over; combat damage is the attacker's), so a spell cast into a Memory Lapse is the caster's move and costs the caster, with both spells in its words, and a Dandân that dies to a Piracy Charm in answer to your cycling is yours too. The turn's start is noted as it was (the first decision of the turn, by `noteTurn`, where the judge was learned), so the draw and the untap are the step from the last action to it, and not a move.

Where it turned (under the chart): your moves that cost the most (at most three, each a fall of 4 points or more in your chance after an action of yours, the worst first) are ringed and numbered on the chart and listed under it with the points lost and what the log said; under them the other player's moves that hurt you the most (the same, not ringed). A row tapped is shown on the chart and its words under it. Two things it can't tell and says so: the judge sees both hands, so a play into a card you couldn't know of falls too, and a pass is not a move, so what it let through is the other player's. Nothing here is drawn for a game from before actions were noted (only turns' starts), whose chart is the old one.

Kept in the state's `adv` (`dandan-rules.js` calls `noteTurn`; never in the games the computer plays out in its head, `quiet`, which notes nothing) so it is the same on both phones at a table and survives a reload and the Result button, which opens the sheet again. Each entry is one number, because the database drops nulls from a list and a list of pairs would lose its place. A turn's start from before is turn × 1000 + percent; since actions, (turn × 1000 + percent) × 400,000 + (kind × 2 + seat) × 100,000 + the log's place (its line counter, to find the words by; the log keeps a thousand lines), which can't be taken for the old kind (under 400,000,000), so a game begun before is read as it was and gets no dots for the rest of its turn. It is always seat 0's chance, and the other seat's is the rest. A game over before its second turn (a concession) has nothing to draw and the sheet says so. The judge is Medium's and sees both hands, so the line moves on cards neither player knew about: it says how the game stood, not how it looked from your seat (the sheet says so under the caption).

The record under it: games won of played, and the same by who it was against (Easy, Medium, Hard, or a friend at a table; games played before they were told apart are "Earlier games"), and the last ten results as dots, newest at the right (`dandan:record` keeps twenty, W, L or D, and which game it counted last). A game counts once, by when it began: until now the page counted a finished game again each time it was opened (a reload, another phone), and the record and the streak both drifted. The Menu's line reads "Won 7 of 15 games (47%)". On a short phone (390 by 664) the sheet scrolls with its buttons kept at its foot; their class was `dd-end-go`, and Kontor styles any class with `-go` in it as its main button, which made the whole row one green plate, so it is `dd-end-acts`.

Saved: `dandan:game` (the game, as every seat game keeps it, so another of your devices or a partner watching sees it as it stands), `dandan:opts` (the computer player's level, the thinking, the computer's pace, the coach, hold priority, choosing the lands to tap, whether revealed cards are shown, full screen, the view), `dandan:record` (games played and won, and by opponent, the last twenty results, and the game counted last), `dandan:review` (what the coach kept of your moves, above) and `dandan:streak` (the run of wins, which the games screen shows, `noteStreak`). A watching frame (`IN_FRAME`) reads the partner's game and its writes go nowhere. Playing together: Invite players, or a party (README, Party), seats two at a table (`rooms.js`, and Party's `ELSEWHERE` in `party-core.js`), the computer in the seat nobody has.
