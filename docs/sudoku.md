# Sudoku
Fill the grid so that every row, every column and every region holds each digit once (October 2026). Four rules, chosen in the menu (Rules, one of): **Classic**, the regions the boxes; **Diagonals** (Sudoku X), the two long diagonals units too, their cells shaded; **Jigsaw**, the regions pieces of any shape; **Killer**, no digits to start with, only cages, each with the sum of its cells and no digit twice in it. Each at two sizes (Size): 9×9, and 6×6 for a few minutes (boxes two rows by three columns, digits 1 to 6). The grid is drawn on the digit grid (below), which Calcudoku and Kakuro share. There is nothing to choose from the knowledge base: the genre's content is its digits. Letters in place of digits (Wordoku) were weighed and left out: they slow the scanning the puzzle is made of, and a hidden word turns a logic puzzle into an anagram.

**How a grid is made, with one solution.** `sudoku-solve.js` is an exact solver: candidates as bit masks, it settles what's forced (a cell with one candidate, a digit with one place in a unit, a cage's sums) and tries the cell with fewest candidates, counting solutions up to two; a pared-down 9×9 takes it well under a millisecond. Every grid starts as a random solution (the same solver filling an empty grid in a random order). For the digit grids its digits are then taken away in pairs, each the mirror of the other through the centre (a printed grid's convention; a jigsaw's pieces aren't symmetric, so its digits go singly), for as long as the solver still finds exactly one solution. Jigsaw's pieces are made from the boxes by swapping a cell on the edge of one piece for a cell on the same edge of its neighbour, each piece kept in one piece, 81 swaps; a cut with fewer than a third of its cells moved out of their boxes isn't a jigsaw, and not every cut can be filled at all (about one in four can't), so a solution is searched for before it's used. Killer cuts its solution into cages grown from random cells to sizes drawn from the level's odds (two to four cells, mostly two and three for an Easy grid), never a digit twice in a cage; a cell left on its own pairs up with a neighbour or joins a cage; then, wherever the solver finds two solutions, the cage of a cell where they differ is split in two (the longest first, so it doesn't leave single cells), until one is left. A single-cell cage is a digit given in all but name: an Easy Killer keeps up to four, a Medium two, a Hard one. The same seed always makes the same grid (`sudoku-grid.js`'s random numbers are integer arithmetic), which is how today's grid and a match's are the same on every phone, and how a link (`#s=SEED&r=RULES&z=SIZE&l=LEVEL`, or `#d=DAY`) brings one back.

**How hard a grid is, and the levels.** Difficulty is the hardest technique a person needs, never the number of givens. `sudoku-logic.js` is a human-style solver that always takes the easiest step it can find, each technique a function that finds one step and says why, in the tiers solvers commonly agree on (HoDoKu's, for one): rank 1, singles (the last cell of a unit, a hidden single, a naked single); 2, locked candidates (pointing and claiming, and the same between any two units that cross, a jigsaw piece and a row, a diagonal and a box), naked and hidden pairs and triples; 3, naked and hidden quads, the X-wing, swordfish and jellyfish, the XY-wing, XYZ-wing and W-wing, the short single-digit chains (the skyscraper, the kite, the turbot fish) and simple colouring; 4, X-chains of three to six strong links and XY-chains. Killer adds its own: a cage's sum read exactly (the ways it can add up, given what's left in each cell), a digit a cage must hold lying in one unit (rank 1 and 2), and the rule of 45 (a unit adds up to 45, so the cells its cages leave over add up to the rest: a single unit at rank 2, two or three rows or columns side by side at rank 3). A grid that needs more than all of these (a forcing chain, a guess) is never dealt. On a 9×9 the four levels are the four ranks: Easy (singles, and it keeps 36 digits, so it's gentle as well), Medium, Hard and Expert. A grid is pared to its last digit, then graded; one harder than its level gets a digit back where the solver first needed more than the level allows (or where it got stuck), until it's down to it; one easier is thrown away and the next seed in the stream tried. Of 200 pared classic grids, 62% needed singles only, 12% Medium's techniques, 13.5% Hard's, 6.5% a chain, and 5.5% more than any technique here; so a Medium takes about ten tries and an Expert about twelve, Hard about nine. A 6×6 almost never needs anything between singles and chains (97% of pared ones need singles only, and nearly all the rest jump straight to fish and short chains), so its levels are Easy (singles, 16 digits kept), Medium (singles only, but pared until no digit is to spare, so there are single places to find rather than several) and Hard (anything beyond singles). Killer's cages settle so much by their sums that its grids come out Medium whatever the cages' sizes and hardly ever need a chain: Easy (sums and singles), Medium (the rule of 45, locked candidates, pairs) and Hard (two or three rows at once, fish, wings), a Hard one made from a Medium cut by joining neighbouring cages (up to six cells), while it keeps one solution, until it needs a Hard technique. So Expert is offered on a 9×9 without cages, and the level menu has no Expert for a 6×6 or Killer. A grid that can't be found at its level in the tries allowed (it hasn't happened in testing) is dealt as the level it is, never as a harder one. Every grid is made in a worker (`sudoku-worker.js`), so a hard one never holds the screen; on the machine here, on average, Classic and Diagonals take tens of milliseconds to a fifth of a second, Jigsaw up to half a second but its Expert one to three seconds, Killer a fifth of a second to a second; a phone is slower. One spare grid of each kind is kept ready on the device, made in the background after each deal, so New grid is at once; the rete shows only when there's none.

**Today's grid** is a classic 9×9 at Medium, the same for everyone (`today()`'s date its seed), counted the moment its last digit goes in: the games screen shows today's time (`noteDayTime`, kept in `sudoku:today`), and opens on it while it's still to play. It's always played alone, with the computer off, so the times compare.

**Hints teach.** Hint costs 30 seconds. A wrong digit comes first (every step after a wrong one is built on sand), struck through; then pencil marks that leave out the digit their cell needs; then the easiest step from the grid as it stands, counting your pencil marks where you've made them, so it never tells you what you've already worked out: named and explained in the line under the grid ("In box 2, the 8 fits only one cell: every other is ruled out by its row, column or box"; "X-wing on 4: …"), its cells lit, and its target picked out. A digit's cell is selected for you to write it yourself; candidates a step strikes out go from your pencil marks there. Anything the hint says comes from the same solver that grades the grids, and the tests check every one of its steps against the solution.

**The computer** (3 November 2026, Korbi: "'against the computer' makes little sense"; "probably drop from all": no rival that races you, on any puzzle) is the same solver at a person's pace (`sudoku-bot.js`): each step takes the time a person needs for its technique (a hidden single eight seconds or so, an X-wing a minute, an XY-chain nearly three), shorter when there are many singles on offer and longer with one left, with a little luck either way; at three paces, Steady (a newspaper solver), Keen (a regular) and Sharp (a competition solver). In the menu (Computer: Off, Partner): **Partner** gives you and it a share of the givens each, as a pair at a table gets (below): its share hatched on your grid, its digits arriving in copper as it finds them, working from what it can see (your digits included), and waiting for you when that isn't enough. A grid solved with it isn't your best: it's a team's time.

**Playing together.** A duo match (asked for from the partner sheet) or a Party's table of up to six (a race first; Together is in the menu), on the head-to-head table (`duel.js`: the lobby, Ready, the countdown to a start everyone shares), in either of two ways, switched from the menu (Race instead, Together instead), which deals a new match for everyone at the rules, size and level of whoever asks. **A race**: the same grid for everyone, each on their own phone, the others' digits to go in the line over the grid; the lowest time with hints wins; the pair's record counts the wins. **Together** (a pair's match starts this way): one grid for the table, and each player sees only their share of the givens; the others' are hatched cells (in Killer, every cage is outlined on every screen but a sum only someone else holds shows as "?"). What anyone writes is everyone's at once, in that player's colour, and anyone may write anywhere; the cell each of the others has selected is framed in their colour on your grid, with their initial, so two don't set off for the same one (written to their seat as they move, at most every 150 ms); pencil marks stay your own; hints are the team's (30 seconds each, the hint shown to whoever asked); the team's time runs from the start to the last right digit, recorded as the pair's best. The less able player can't just watch: they hold givens the other needs. How much each holds was measured, not guessed. Half the givens each is too little: a pair split half and half got stuck after one round of placing what each could, in every Medium grid tried. So every given is dealt to one player, round each region in turn, and then, a few at a time and at random, givens are shown to everyone, until the relay finishes: each player works out all they can from what they see, at the grid's own level, what they find goes to the others, and round again. As few are shared as that takes: about a third of the givens on an Easy grid and two thirds to three quarters on the harder ones, so each player still holds givens only they can see, a dozen on an Easy grid and three to five on the harder ones, and each finds a good share of the digits (in the relay, the one who finds fewer still finds a quarter to a third of them). The tests check that neither share alone has one solution (each has many), that the two together see every given, and that the relay finishes, so the two can finish without a word (saying "what's your middle cell in box 4?" is quicker). A table of three to six gets its shares the same way, round the table. The players who began at the start hold the shares; one who sits down later shares one of theirs; a share whose player has been away for 20 seconds is lent to everyone else (shown in grey, or its sums shown) until they're back, so a phone that sleeps never leaves the table stuck. The split is worked out from the grid and the table's size on each phone, in the worker, the same everywhere. Computer players don't sit at a table: a seat nobody has is no share at all, and a share that goes unheld is lent, which serves the table better than a computer filling it in.
**Co-op** (3 November 2026, `docs/coop.md`, `sudoku-coop.js`, `digits-coop.js`): a paired partner who watches a solo grid can ask to join it, and both then write on one grid with every given shown to both: not Together above, which shares the givens out on `duel.js` and is never this. Digits and pencil marks are shared (a layer of digits and one of marks for each digit, so two players marking different digits in one cell both stand, and a mark never overwrites a digit); selection is each player's own and your partner's cell is framed as in Together; undo leaves what your partner changed since; a hint is the team's. No computer partner, no Show the solution, and a shared solve earns no best, today's time or Random round (today's grid solved together stops being today's in the host's run).

**Twists**, weighed. Kept, each because it changes the reasoning: the 6×6 for a few minutes; Diagonals, a mild change of scanning that costs the engine two more units; Jigsaw, where a piece's shape is half the puzzle; and Killer, the variant newspapers print most, with a reasoning of its own (sums, the rule of 45). Left out: Windoku and other extra regions (Diagonals' change again, with more clutter), anti-knight and anti-king (modern, and a generated one is rarely more than a duller classic), Kropki dots, thermometers, arrows and sandwiches (hand-set genres whose generated grids are rarely elegant), 16×16 and Samurai (they don't fit a phone), and Wordoku (above).

**The digit grid** (`digits.js`, `digits.css`, `digits-edit.js`; `tests/digits.mjs`) is the board, its pad and its keys, designed for all three number grids from the start: any grid from 4 to 12 a side, rectangles too (Kakuro's); a value per cell shown as one of the game's digits (so a Calcudoku of 0 to 5 can have a 0); regions with heavy rules between them; cages, outlined in dashes just inside their cells, each sum in its first cell's corner over the dashes, as printed Killers have them, or drawn as heavy rules (Calcudoku's, `cageRules`); blacked-out cells, and Kakuro's clue cells split by a diagonal with an across and a down sum; diagonals shaded. It draws and it listens: the game keeps every bit of state (what's in the cells, the selection, Notes), so a saved run is the whole picture and a partner watching sees it, and the board says what was tapped (`onInput`: select, digit, erase, undo, redo, notes, or a game's own tool). Its API is at the top of `digits.js`. A selected cell lights its row, column and region (or what the game says its peers are: a Kakuro run), every cell with its digit, and that digit in every cell's pencil marks; repeats, wrong digits, a hint's cells and its target, a partner's hatched givens and lent ones are marks a game sets, and where the others at a table are (`cursors`) is framed in each one's colour. `digits-edit.js` keeps the entries: writing a digit (again on the same digit rubs it out; its pencil mark goes from the cells that see it, a setting), pencil marks, rubbing out, every pencil mark at once, and undo and redo of the last 300 moves, each move undone whole, all as plain JSON.

**Controls.** On a phone: tap a cell, then a digit; Notes writes in pencil (the keys show their digit small in the corner, as a pencil mark is); a digit tapped with no cell selected lights where it stands. Each key shows how many of its digit are still to place (and dims when none are). Undo, Redo, Erase, Notes and Hint are under the keys; Fill in every pencil mark and Show the solution are in the menu. The keys are a row of nine on a phone, taller on a tall phone (the grid can't use the height: it's as wide as the screen already), lower on a short one so the grid keeps its room; at 390 by 600 nothing scrolls. On a keyboard: the arrows move, a digit's key writes it (with Shift, in pencil), Backspace or Delete erases, N switches Notes, Ctrl+Z undoes and Ctrl+Y redoes. On an upright tablet the phone's layout is drawn larger. Held sideways, the grid takes the left with all the height and the clock, the line and a three-by-three keypad stand beside it, the keys at the bottom. The menu has the rules, size and level (picking a level deals a grid of the rules and size chosen), the computer and its pace, Mark repeats (on by default: a digit twice in a unit marked in oxblood), and Clear pencil marks as you write.

**The rules sheet** (October 2026, as in every classic, Korbi asking for one): Rules in the header, or The rules in the menu, opens the rules in short plain prose: the aim, the four rule sets at both sizes, a worked example (a hidden single, and Killer's two-cell cages of 3 and 17), how to play here (the taps and keys, Notes, what the game marks and fills in, the hint), the levels with each technique said in a few words, the time and today's grid. It scrolls inside itself under its pinned head, opens at its top each time, and the clock stands while it's open, so reading costs nothing. Writing it found the level notes calling triples Hard where the solver ranks them with pairs, at Medium; they say so now.

**Scoring and what's kept.** A grid's time is the time it was on screen: the clock stops while the menu, the rules or the games screen is open, and while the phone is elsewhere, and the grid hides behind a plate meanwhile, so the stopped clock buys no thinking. A hint adds 30 seconds. Bests are by rules, size and level, lowest first, in `sudoku:times` (not `sudoku:best`, which the games screen reads as highest-wins); the games screen's figure is today's time. The run in play is `sudoku:run`: the grid itself (its givens, solution, regions or cages, as strings, so it opens at once and a partner watching sees it without making it), your entries with their undo, the selection, the Notes switch, the time and hints, and with the computer its share and its next digit; the settings `sudoku:settings`, the spares `sudoku:spares`, the way a table plays `sudoku:together`, and a match's own run `sudoku:duel` (duel.js keeps it), so a reload anywhere comes back as it was.

`tests/sudoku.mjs` checks the rules as units, the exact solver on known grids (one solution, none, more), about fifty grids of every rules, size and level (one solution each, at their level, mirrored where they should be, the cages' sums and pieces right, every step of the human-style solver agreeing with the solution, the solver finishing each), the jigsaw and Killer pieces, grading and hints on known grids, the computer (the paces in order, a partner's next digit right), grids and entries through JSON and the database, the splits (each player holds givens the other lacks, neither can finish alone, the relay finishes, for a pair and a table of three), the table through the database's round trip (a digit in for this match only and never on a given, who placed it, the last right digit solving it with its time, the team's hints, a share nobody holds, a latecomer's share), and today's grid the same each time it's made, with nothing in the engine but its seed (no `Math.random`, no clock).

Not done: there's no mistake limit or check-as-you-go mode (a hint finds a wrong digit, at 30 seconds); a Killer is drawn from generated cuts, whose sums can look less shapely than a setter's; computer players don't take seats at a table (above); an Expert Jigsaw can take a phone several seconds to make the first time (the next is already made). Whether Medium is the right daily, and whether the pace of the computer's Keen matches a regular, is for Korbi's thumb to say.
