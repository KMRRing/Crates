# Almanac

Puzzle, logic and knowledge games for the phone, alone or with a partner, online or off. It began as Crates, the first game below, and keeps that address; on the home screen it's Almanac, a sextant for its icon (the manifest, every page's home-screen title, and `icon.svg` with the PNGs drawn from it, `icons/` and `icon.png`).

Play: https://kmrring.github.io/Crates/ (on iPhone: Share → Add to Home Screen)

## The look

Brass instruments on an almanac's page, with an Art Deco hand: the precision of a sextant or an astrolabe (brass on navy, engraved scales, enamelled detail) set the way the 1930s set gold on black, in wide geometric capitals and fine double rules, as in an ocean liner's chart room or Griffith Observatory. Light is the page: parchment (`--yard`), cream paper for cards and dialogs (`--sheet`), navy ink (`--ink`), brass for lines, frames and scales (`--brass`). Dark is the instrument itself: a night-navy dial, parchment lettering, bright brass. The four crate colours are enamels: ochre, verdigris (copper's green patina), cobalt and oxblood, and every game's own accent is retuned to an enamel of its colour, with its soft fill mixed from it and the paper (`color-mix`), so both schemes follow from one value.

All of it lives in `style.css`'s tokens; a game names colours only for what is its own. Headings (a game's name in its header, dialog titles, menu headings, crate names, the tiles on the games screen) are Archivo at its widest (`--wide`, 125%), in spaced capitals: the font the suite already serves offline, so the look costs no download. A header sits on a graduated rule, a hairline with ticks every nine pixels, like an instrument's scale. Buttons are brass-framed with square corners; a primary action is a plate, navy lettered in gilt on the page and brass lettered in navy on the dial (`--plate`, `--plate-ink`), with a gilt hairline inside its edge, and every game's main action button is that plate. Tiles are paper with a brass edge, and a picked tile wears a brass frame. A game's stage (the card it plays on: Chart's map card, Quote's question, Manifest's floor, a tasting note in Brut) is a panel with square-ish corners framed in a double rule, a brass edge and a hairline four pixels inside it, drawn as an inset shadow so it sits under the content and never crosses a map or a board; Cartel's felt table has a gilt rim and a gilt line inside it. Category labels are tags, not pills: small rectangles in the game's enamel, lettered in the same wide capitals; boxes, options and inputs take a tile's corners, and the only round things left are dots, discs and round icon buttons. Every game's logo is its object drawn as the instruments are (README, the games screen): navy, brass, parchment and its enamel. Each logo's contour is drawn at its width times `--wire`: 1 in a game's header and on the games screen, 1.5 on the selected game (the one you're in, or a tile under your finger or pointer), the contour thickening as it's chosen while the engraved detail keeps its fine weight.

The logo is a sextant whose frame is the A: its brace the crossbar, its graduated arc the foot, the index arm reading the angle (`logo.svg`; the app icon is it on a navy tile). Loading is drawn the same way (`loading.js`). Whenever a page or a long task loads, the astrolabe's rete turns large in the middle of the screen, the task's words under it (`rete.svg`): while a page loads (it fades in only after a moment, so a page that's ready at once never shows it; it lets taps through, so a page can never be locked behind it), while Delta deals a hard board, while Crates sets up or fetches a synced run, and while Almanac updates itself. A short wait inside a line or a box shows the sextant, small, its arm swinging (`sextant.svg`): a dictionary lookup, a reconnecting room, a toast that says it's still working (one ending in "…"), and a picture on its way. `busy(label)` returns the function that ends a wait; `sextant()` is the small one as an element. Both drawings are animated SVG (SMIL), so they run as plain image files, offline, with nothing in any page's markup.

## Crates
Sixteen words, four hidden crates. Group the four words that belong together, then name the country or commodity behind them.

Topics are tapped round three states, the same control as Punt's: out, in, more (twice as often). An old "Less" counts as in; the presets (Balanced, Trader, Culture night) use in and more.

## Boards
Every board is drawn fresh from a word bank: 69 countries and 90 commodities. Clues and answers map many-to-many: Copper points to Chile, the DR Congo, Zambia and eight more; the Nile to Egypt and Ethiopia; Commonwealth to the UK, Poland and Lithuania. Each clue-answer pair has its own explanation and its own difficulty from 1 to 3, because the link is easy for some answers and hard for others (about 5,500 pairs over 3,700 clues). Hong Kong's clues sit under China. Clues also carry topics. The generator picks four answers, then four clues each, and only keeps a board that has exactly one solution given every answer each clue points to. Each crate must also name exactly one answer: the only answer in the whole bank that all four of its clues fit. That matters for close relatives such as FAME and its variants (UCOME, RME, SME, PME, TME): clues true of all biodiesel sit under all six, so a crate needs at least one clue specific to its answer. No tile contains the name of an answer on its board, so White gold never appears alongside Gold. Red herrings (clues that also point to another crate on the board) follow the difficulty: none on Easy, at most one on Mixed (on roughly two boards in five), one or two on Hard (two on roughly one board in five). Each herring fits exactly one other crate, and no two herrings link the same pair of crates. A solved crate lists its red herrings but names the other crate only once that one is open too; from then on the herring shows in both crates ("Amazon also fits Brazil" in Peru, "also fits here, but belongs to Peru" in Brazil). Crates are coloured yellow to red by the average difficulty of their clues.

The dropdown in the header picks what the boards deal: Countries, Commodities, or Mixed (each board one or the other). It is the suite's own dropdown rather than the phone's: a button in Crates' logo colours (a deep tint on a dark screen, where the light one glared), as wide as its longest choice so it never changes size, opening onto a card with a tick on the current choice and a line under each saying what it deals. A hidden select still holds the choice, so links (`?cat=`), rooms and synced runs read and set it as before. Any game can take it up (`dropdown.js`, `dropdown.css`): link the stylesheet, call `dropdown(select)`, give the select `data-accent` with the game's logo prefix, and its options `data-note` for the lines under them.

## Settings
Menu (No. button) → Settings. They apply from the next board.
- Preset: Trader, Balanced or Culture night.
- Difficulty: Easy, Mixed or Hard.
- Topics: Off, Less, Normal or More for each. Countries and commodities have their own lists, and Settings shows the ones your pool deals from. Country topics (19): Film, TV & games covers films, series, video and board games; Craft & style covers textiles and weaving, tailoring and menswear, shoes and watches. Commodity topics (10): Origins, Production, Uses, Specs & science, Markets, Trade & logistics, Policy, Companies, History, Culture & language. Money & economy is what an informed newspaper reader knows (currencies, central banks, household-name banks and indices, famous crises, sovereign funds); Finance is what someone working in markets knows (benchmarks and crude grades, bond markets, trading jargon, rogue traders and blow-ups). Benchmarks also carry their sector topic, so TTF comes up under Energy as well.
- Regions and sectors: switch whole groups of answers on or off (for example only European countries, or no chemicals). Commodity sectors: Energy; Biofuels & low-carbon; Metals & minerals; Grains & oilseeds; Softs, fibres & livestock; Chemicals; Circular & recycled; Credits & freight.

If the settings are too narrow to build a board, that board falls back to Balanced and says so.

## Learning modes (solo)
Settings → Learning mode has four settings:
- Off: boards are dealt at random, so a clue can come back.
- Learn: missed clues come back (below).
- No repeats: no clue is dealt twice (under any answer) until you've seen every clue your settings allow; then that category starts over. It counts every board you've played, not just those since you switched it on.
- Clue learn: only tiles you open with ? come back, 3 to 8 boards later in a new crate, even if you skip that board. Solve one without opening it and it's learned. Nothing else on the board is recorded. Tiles you opened in Learn mode count here too.

Learn mode in detail: every clue-answer pair is its own card: knowing that Copper points to Chile doesn't mark Copper→Zambia as known. For each pair on a board the question is: did you tie that clue to that answer?
- Yes: its crate was found and named, or a "one away" guess put it among the three that belonged together (in a one-away with Matches, Rotten eggs and Gunpowder, those three count as known even if the Sulphur crate is never found).
- No: it was the odd one out or the one left out in a "one away", you opened its clue, its crate was found but not named, or its crate was never found and nothing showed you placed it. A pair in a plain miss (two and two, or worse) proves nothing either way.
- A missed pair comes back 3 to 8 boards later in a crate of the same answer, next to companions it wasn't missed with, and usually on a board without the crates it was missed next to. In a Mixed pool, pairs of the other category wait for a board of their own kind, so they can take a little longer.
- A pair you get right retires to the bottom of the deck. Unseen pairs are preferred, so the same clue turns up under its other answers as you go.
- Solved crates flag clues "↻ back soon" or "✓ learned" (in Learn and Clue learn). Progress stays in the browser (or follows a synced run); Settings has a reset for the learning cards and, in No repeats, a way to start the clues over.

## Slate
(Called Glyph until October 2026: glyph.html forwards to slate.html, and saves, rooms and the switcher id still say glyph, so nothing in play broke.)

Tap the title (Crates or Slate) to switch games. Slate lives at slate.html.

Fill a small crossword so every across and down run is a real word. Coloured fields carry secret letter rules, and the colour family says what kind:
- Blues and greens judge each letter on its own (a tick or cross on the letter).
- Yellows and oranges judge neighbouring pairs, read left to right and top to bottom (the mark sits on the join).
- Purples and pinks, dashed, judge the whole field once every cell is filled (one mark for the field).

When a board ends, the result shows placements, checks, clues (rules revealed) and time (time on screen for solo boards; from dealing to the end in a together game). On Easy, checks, suggestions and rule reveals are unlimited; otherwise two of each per board. Check and Suggest judge against every winning board, not our fill: any fill of real words from the full list (repeats allowed) that keeps every rule, including the whole-field rules that can't be judged yet. Check asks which letters must change for a winning board to exist, keeping all the others: letters nearest the highlighted word are protected first (rings counted outward through crossings; no nearer letter is marked while changes further out would do), then fewer letters, then typed letters before placed ones. Those letters get an orange outline until they change, and while the cursor is on one its key is outlined too; a clean board says "All of this can still win". The answer is exact when the search finishes, and says so when time ran out first. Typing: tap a cell and type; the cursor moves along the row (the default) or, after tapping the same cell again, down the column, and Place (bottom left of the keyboard) puts the word down. Letters typed but not placed belong to their word: moving within it (a tap or the arrow keys) keeps them, moving to another word drops them. Retyping one letter of a word and pressing Place counts as a new placement. Delete empties the cell under the cursor (typed or placed) and the cursor stays; on an empty cell it steps back one and empties that, so repeated presses walk back along the word. On a keyboard the arrow keys move the cursor, as in the New York Times crossword: pressed across the word being typed where another word crosses, the first press turns to that word; otherwise the cursor goes to the next cell that way, over gaps, and typing then runs that way wherever a word does. A complete word that isn't in the word list can still be placed: you're told, it's underlined in red, and like a frame it isn't judged. Placing letters that don't complete a word is allowed, to build a frame: they show greyed with a dotted underline, aren't judged, don't count as placements, and the first Clear removes them; a letter is judged once it's part of a complete real word. While the cursor is in a single-letter field, the keyboard shows the letters that field has taken (green) and rejected (red). In a pair field, once a neighbour in the same field has a letter (placed or typed), the keyboard shows the letters known to go with it (green) and known not to (red), from the pairs tried so far, each read in the field's direction (left to right, top to bottom); with two lettered neighbours, green needs both known to pass and red needs one known to fail. With the field's rule revealed, every letter is coloured. Clear (between Fields and Check letters) escalates: the first press empties letters that aren't part of a complete real word, the next also words that break a field's rule, the next the whole board; clearing never counts as a placement. Fields opens a sheet with everything each field has passed and failed, and a ? per field: two per board (the "2 × ?" beside Close counts them down) reveal a field's rule (for a single-letter field the keyboard then shows every letter green or red). In a together game a revealed rule, and that field's marks, are shown to both players. New board is in the menu. The board and keyboard size themselves to the visible screen, clear of Safari's bars. Our fill uses only very common words; any fill that obeys every rule and makes real words wins too. Every rule is revealed when the board ends.

Boards come in 36 shapes (12 per level) in which every horizontal and every vertical run of two or more cells is a word of three to five letters: there are no two-letter runs, so rows stacked on each other are always joined by real down words. Each board turns or mirrors its shape at random. Easy shapes have five or six words, Medium six to eight, Hard eight to ten with rows stacked into blocks. tests/shapes.mjs checks every shape in every orientation. Difficulty comes from the rules, each rated easy, medium or hard; every level mixes all three field types. The rule catalogue (slate-gen.js): vowels, consonants, alphabet halves and thirds, straight or curved letters, enclosed spaces, keyboard rows, typing hand, mirror symmetry, odd alphabet positions, rarer letters and hidden keywords for single letters; ascending, descending, vowel-consonant, same class, same shape, same keyboard row, same hand, within three letters and double letters for pairs; no duplicates, no vowels, exactly one vowel, a repeated letter, one shape, rearranges into a word, one keyboard row and an even alphabet sum for whole fields.

Together: Menu → Play together sends a link (or a four-letter code). Both players place words on the same board and share everything: the placements, the checks and clues, and every field's marks. Rooms are shared with Crates (see Rooms).

Words: SCOWL word lists (sizes 10-20 for our fills, up to 70 with British and American spellings for what you may place), packed in slate-words.js.

Suggest: a word for the highlighted slot that lies on a winning board keeping every letter entered, placed or typed; our fill's word where it can be, else common words first. It's typed into the slot for you to place with Enter. If no winning board keeps your letters it says so ("No winning board keeps your current letters", or "No solution found" when the search ran out of time) and isn't spent: Check shows what has to change. A full slot has nothing to suggest. Both are worked out off the page (`slate-worker.js`, `slate-solve.js`): a depth-first search over the full word list, the slot with fewest words left first, with the rules propagated as letters go in (a single-letter field narrows its cells, a pair narrows the partner of a placed letter, a whole field narrows as letters arrive and exactly at its last cell); Check goes ring by ring outward, learning small sets of letters that can't all stay and dropping the fewest that break them all. `tests/slate-solve.mjs` plays simulated boards and checks that clean boards (ours or another winning one) are called clean, that the orange letters are enough and each is needed, and that Suggest's words win. Definitions: double-tap a cell to see the complete words through it defined; the double tap leaves the cursor and any typed letters as they were. The dictionary is Slate's own (`slate-defs.js`), so it works offline: up to three senses for each of the 10,510 words players may place, from Open English WordNet 2025 (CC BY 4.0, credited in the dialog), the first sense of each part of speech first. An inflected form shows its base word's senses (BILLS: plural of BILL), and a word that is also a form of a common word says so (FOUND: also the past tense of FIND). The 1,500 or so words WordNet lacks (function words, slang, abbreviations, dialect and archaic words) were defined for Slate; 35 of the rarest have no entry. It is 877 KB (329 KB as served), loaded on the first lookup rather than with the board, and replaced dictionaryapi.dev, a free service that was often down and lacked common words. One entry per line, so edit it in place; `tests/slate-defs.mjs` checks every fill word is defined and every pointer lands on a definition.

On a narrow phone the four buttons under the board share the row equally, so the last one never runs off the edge.

What's left of Check and Suggest on a board that limits them shows as a small brass badge on each button's corner, not as "(2)" in the label, which ran out of four buttons to a row.

## Your run on several devices
Menu → Your other devices → Sync this run. The run gets an 8-letter code and a link; open the link on your other device (or type the code into Join there) and both devices follow the same run: the board you're on, your history, settings and learning cards. Changes go up a moment after each move and when you put the page away, and a device that was left open with old progress catches up instead of overwriting newer progress. Each change also carries the size of the word bank it was made with. A device still on an older version of the app reloads itself onto the new one instead of dealing boards of its own (it couldn't read boards that use newer words), and a device that is only following, untouched, doesn't run the clock or write anything. "Stop syncing on this device" keeps a local copy and lets go of the run.

## Delta
A field of hexes with gaps. Some hexes hold plain numbers, some hold operations (+4, −2, ×3, ÷2), the rest are blank. Three rules:
1. Join the numbers in pairs. Drag from a number to draw a path; paths can't cross, share a hex or pass through another number.
2. A path runs from the number you draw it from: the value starts as that number, each operation on the way changes it in turn, and it must arrive exactly at the other number. Values can become fractions (5 ÷ 4 = 1¼). Which end to start from is yours to work out.
3. Every operation is used exactly once. Blank hexes are optional.

Drawing through another path cuts it there; tapping either number of a path removes it. Arrows show the way each finished path runs.

Levels:
- Easy: two pairs, + and −, the pairs are marked; the running value follows your finger and finished paths are marked right or wrong at once.
- Medium: three pairs, adds ×; running value and immediate marks as on Easy; two clues per board (a clue shows two numbers that belong together).
- Hard: a small, crowded field (the 37-hex hexagon with five gaps), adds ÷. No running value: you work the values out. Paths are only marked right or wrong once every number is joined.

Numbers and operations keep the mental maths light at every size: times tables on small numbers, round numbers times small ones, shifts by 10, 100 and 1,000, round steps on big round numbers (+300 on 1,200), and simple halves, thirds and quarters. Big numbers (up to 100,000) only come from those easy steps, so a big number is a hint at the operation that made it (13,500 wants the ×1,000), never a sum like 259 × 13. Medium adds the big numbers; Hard also has fractions, shown as 9½ or ¾. Operations are often reused across the board, so another pair could take one: that feeds the lures below.

Boards are built backwards from random paths, then checked by a solver in two stages: first every pairing and share-out of the operations the arithmetic allows, then whether those paths can actually be drawn at the same time. A board is kept only if exactly one can be drawn (the one it was built from) while the arithmetic alone allows others, and only if it is full of lures: single paths that add up and could be drawn on their own but belong to no answer, because they block another pair or use an operation another pair needs. Lures must touch most of the numbers (all six on Hard), so the "this works, but it cuts off that" moments are all over the board. The same seed always gives the same board; if a seed's first tries don't meet the bar, the lure and decoy targets relax step by step, never the one-answer rule. `tests/delta.mjs` checks every level.

Boards are dealt in a worker, and a spare board per level is dealt in the background and kept between visits, so New board and changing level are instant even on Hard (a Hard board can take a few seconds to find).

Together: both players draw on one board (`together.js` handles the room side for Delta: joining, seats, moves as transactions, presence).

## Punt
Trivia from the Crates bank with a bookmaker. Each question shows a clue, a country or a commodity, and two or four options (never three: they'd leave one dangling in the 2×2 grid); sometimes more than one is right ("China" goes with many commodities): then the question says how many, and you must pick all of them to win. The house prices that as the chance of picking exactly the right set, knowing some and guessing the rest. The house offers odds; you pick an option and stake a share of your pot (slider or 0/5/10/25/50%/all-in), or pass. A right pick pays stake × odds; a wrong one loses the stake. You start with 1,000. Runs are a standard length (12 or 15 questions by level), 100 questions, or endless (dealt 20 at a time; end it from the menu). The score that compares runs is the average return a question, compounded: the steady rate that would have turned 1,000 into the pot (a pass is 0%, a bust −100%). The best per level and length is kept.

Runs are dealt in balanced batches so they're comparable: a fixed-length run is one batch, an endless run a batch of 20 at a time. Within a batch the mix of question kinds, option counts and clue difficulties is exactly the level's, and the house's mispricings are an even spread of its noise (normal quantiles, shuffled), with one scale factor per batch so a typical player's return per unit staked is exactly 1 − margin, rounding and the 1.05× floor included. Runs differ in which questions they get, not in how generous the house happened to be. The house prices each question from its clue's difficulty (its guess at a typical player's chance: knowing the clue, or guessing among the options), then strays from that by random noise and takes a small margin. So sometimes it pays far too much and sometimes far too little: the skill is spotting a generous price on something you know, and sizing the bet to how sure you are. After each question it shows the fair price and what it paid.
- Easy: 12 questions, mostly two options (sometimes four), easy clues, a sloppy house (big mispricings) with a 3% margin.
- Medium: 15 questions, two or four options, a 5% margin.
- Hard: 15 questions, four options, hard clues, more questions with several right answers, a sharp house (small mispricings) with a 6% margin.

Questions come in two kinds: a clue with answers to choose from (any answer carrying the clue is right; wrong options come mostly from the same region or sector), and an answer with clues to choose from. A clue that names one of the options is never used. The same seed always deals the same session, so a link (#s=…&d=…) lets two people play the same questions. `tests/punt.mjs` checks every level.

Maths: a fourth entry in the level dropdown deals from `maths-bank.js` instead of the clue bank: 1,108 multiple-choice questions from GCSE to first-year university with a finance tilt, each with a stage (GCSE, IB SL, IB HL, Y1 Uni), a difficulty from 1 (routine GCSE) to 10 (hardest first-year university), four options, one or two right answers and an explanation. Choosing Maths shows two pickers under the header, stages and difficulties, each a sheet of toggles that counts how many questions match; a new choice starts a new run. The bank loads only when Maths is chosen. Questions come in the order the seed shuffles the filtered pool (round again once it runs dry), priced like any batch from a typical player's chance of knowing a question of that difficulty (90% at 1, falling 8 points a level). Links carry the choice (`&st=` stages, `&df=` difficulties), stats and bests keep Maths as its own level, and flags work on maths questions too.

Refining: a fifth entry, on `refining-bank.js`, the same machinery as Maths: 139 questions on how a refinery works (basics, units, specs, renewables, economics) and, in two stages of their own, the chemistry of gasoline and diesel blending: molecular classes and octane, reforming and alkylation reactions, RVP and distillation, EN 228's limits, oxygenates, stability, cetane and cold flow, EN 590, what each component brings and costs, difficulties 1–10, with explanations, and the same two pickers. It pairs with the Refinery game: the game teaches by routing, the questions check what stuck. Quote has a Refining category too (two dozen refinery numbers), and Chart's trade places include refineries and hubs.

Reasoning: critical reasoning on `reasoning-bank.js`, 41 questions in trade and market settings: which statement weakens or strengthens an argument, what it assumes, what would help evaluate it, the flaw by name (false dilemma, argument from ignorance, equivocation, affirming the consequent), what follows and what doesn't, correlation against causation, base rates, selection and Simpson's paradox, regression to the mean, overfitting, sunk costs and expected value. Five stages for the picker.

Words: a word, pick its synonym among four, 236 of them from advanced to obscure with a gloss each, with a trade and law tilt (contango, demurrage, estoppel, subrogation). Cities: every capital (197), which country a notable city is in (about 200 cities: ports, refinery towns, industrial and financial centres), and what a city is known for (115 questions: BASF's Ludwigshafen, the ECB, Cushing, the Canton Fair, Rosario's crushers), stages by region with the weight on Germany, the UK, China, the rest of Europe and Latin America. Flags: 197 countries (the UN's members, Vatican City, Palestine, Kosovo and Taiwan), the flag as its emoji, distractors from the same region at a similar obscurity, in three stages from well known to the Pacific micro-states. Patterns: 61 what-comes-next questions, each standing on its own and most with a drawing: number series and the odd one out as boxes, functions as pairs, letter and word series, and figures (a painted cube cut 3×3×3 in isometric, a die, points on a circle and a grid, a clock, a folded sheet, shape rows, a chessboard). A bank question may carry an `svg`, which Punt shows under the prompt. All four use the Maths machinery and pickers.

The textbook banks, each on the same machinery, with explanations on every question: Economics (`eco-bank.js`, 73: markets and firms with drawn supply-and-demand diagrams, macro and money with the yield curves and the Phillips and Laffer curves, trade and finance, the history of ideas); Physics (`phy-bank.js`, 62: motion and energy, electricity and waves, heat, gases and nuclei, the modern picture, formulas in LaTeX); Chemistry (`chm-bank.js`, 58: atoms and bonds, reactions, organic chemistry and fuels, industry and the environment); Code (`cs-bank.js`, 68: data and algorithms, code to read in Python, JavaScript, SQL, regex and JSON shown as a snippet, object-oriented and functional paradigms, systems and the web); Philosophy (`phil-bank.js`, 60: the ancients, the moderns, ethics and politics, knowledge and mind); Religion (`rel-bank.js`, 59: Islam, Judaism and Christianity, India and East Asia, and what to know at the table and in the calendar). Switzerland (`swiss-bank.js`, 44, written October 2026 for the move to Geneva): Geneva (the Escalade and Mère Royaume's soup, Calvin's Protestant Rome, the Reformation Wall, the Jet d'Eau, Dunant and the Red Cross, international Geneva, CERN, the Geneva Seal, the private banks, the trading houses), the Swiss state (the Federal Council and the magic formula, initiatives and referendums, the double majority, the Sonderbund War, neutrality, the cantons, the UN and the EU) and Swiss life and money (the languages, the Gotthard, Swiss made, chocolate, banking secrecy, the franc shock, Credit Suisse, the militia, fondue); wrong options are real alternatives where they can be (Tell's apple and Winkelried's spears beside Mère Royaume's soup; the Paul Scherrer Institute beside CERN).. Art (`art-bank.js`, 225) shows the painting: who painted it, where it hangs, which movement, over 69 famous works, plus the art world's stories without pictures. Wine (`wine-bank.js`, 162) is Brut's cards. Movements as ideas (written October 2026): knowing which pictures belong to a movement isn't knowing the movement, so each of the 29 movements has a card in kb/entities.js (where and when, what it rejected, what it sought, how to spot it, what lay behind it, what came next) and the build asks four questions from it: which movement was reacting against this, which was seeking that, how to spot it, what lay behind it. Wrong options are other movements and their own lines, nearest in time, so every wrong answer is true of something; on 'against', never one of the same family (the Renaissances, the abstractions), whose reasons overlap; never a movement the question names, and no question echoes its answer's name. A miss shows the card.

Rich questions: any prompt, option or note may carry maths between `$…$`, rendered to MathML by Temml (`vendor/temml.min.js`, MIT, loaded on first use; `rich.js`); a question may carry a drawn chart (`svg`), a code snippet (`code`, shown in a monospace block) or a picture (`pic`, the English Wikipedia page title of a painting or landmark, whose lead image `pics.js` fetches from Wikipedia's summary API at display time and the service worker keeps). Deck shows all three on review. Quote dates the same 69 paintings from their picture, and Chart shows a museum's famous painting on its card. Maths is told from money as Pandoc does: an inline $ opens maths only before a non-space and closes it only after one, never before a digit, and \$ is always a dollar, so 'spent $3 million … needs $2 million' stays two prices (`mathParts` in `rich.js`; `tests/rich.mjs` also checks that no maths span in any bank reads like a sentence).

Pictures (paintings in Punt, Quote and Chart, and Deck when it brings them back) are named by their English Wikipedia page and fetched at display time by `pics.js`: Wikipedia's own API gives the page's lead image (`prop=pageimages`, fair-use images included, since most 20th-century paintings are) at a standard thumbnail width, the asked width rounded up to Wikimedia's ladder (120, 250, 330, 500, 960, 1280, 1920 px). Since 2026 Wikimedia refuses thumbnails at any other width, which is why every picture once went blank: the old code built 640-pixel URLs by hand. If a thumbnail is refused anyway, the original file is shown instead; only a page without an image is remembered as such (a failed fetch is tried again next time), and the old cache, which had remembered the refusals, is dropped. The service worker keeps fetched pictures, including thumbnails from thumb.wikimedia.org, for offline play.

Flags: after any question, "Disagree with this answer? Flag it" lets you say what's wrong (a right answer marked wrong, a wrong one marked right, an unclear clue, something else) with an optional note. A flag is kept on the device first, so it works offline, and sent to the database (crates/flags/punt) as soon as there's a connection (`question-flags.js`). Menu → Flagged questions lists this device's flags or everyone's, with a button that copies them as text to hand over for review. The database only accepts flags once the rules in `firebase-rules.json` are published (Firebase console → Realtime Database → Rules → paste the file → Publish); until then they wait on the device, nothing lost.

Stats (Menu → Your stats, also from the end of a run; this run, or all runs at the level on this device) score how well you judge what you know, two ways. Expected outcome, the view for a fixed run: how often your bets were right against how often their prices needed you to be, the edge a bet at equal stakes (what each chip earns, ignoring compounding), and the return on the chips you actually staked. Kelly, the view for the long run (Kelly is only optimal as the number of bets grows without limit): your bets grouped by stake size (a bigger stake says you're surer), how often each group came off, what Kelly would stake at that hit rate and those prices, your stakes as a multiple of Kelly's (over- or under-betting), and the growth a question you got against Kelly on your own hit rates (with hindsight). If you pick an option and then pass, the pick is kept, so the stats also show how your passes would have gone. Every settled question is kept on the device (the latest 5,000).

Together: one shared pot. Each of you stakes up to half of it on your own pick, so you can back the same option or hedge against each other; a question settles once you've both bet or passed.

The page fits the screen: the slip takes the room left and scrolls inside itself when a question is long (its torn edge stays put), the stake in words sits beside the odds, and at the reveal the result sits under the slip with its notes scrolling inside, so Next stays in reach while the slip keeps the right answer in sight.

The level dropdown in the header is the suite's own, as in Crates (`dropdown.js`): a button in Punt's logo colours, and the levels on a card with a tick on the current one, scrolling inside itself on a phone. A hidden select still holds the level, so links and rooms work as before.

The slip never scrolls. A painting takes only the room the question and its answers leave (contained, with a shadow on its own edges), down to a small picture on the shortest screens; the stages and difficulties pickers of a bank and the running average are in the menu (Content and About), bank questions carry no stage-and-level marker, and there's no "choose a stake, or pass" line. Pass is as narrow as its word, and the Bet button carries the rest: the stake, the odds and what comes back if right ("Bet 100 · 1.3× · 130"; "Pays 1.3×" before a stake; "Waiting for Sarah" in a duo). Checked over full Art runs at 360×640, 375×600, 390×664 and 390×844: nothing scrolls inside the slip.

Name it turns a clue round: its own hint, with the country or commodity it belongs to and its topics ("Name it · France · Culture & arts: A masked genius haunts the Paris Opéra"), and the clue to name, typed: recall rather than recognition, nothing to choose from, the strictest test of having learned something. Three modes (Menu, Content, Name it; a change deals a new run): Off; Known, the default, where two in every seven questions may be name-it, but only for clues this device has already recognised the other way round (a crate solved and named in Crates, or a right answer you staked on in Punt's clue and answer questions: `known.js` keeps that record, keyed by the knowledge base's entities), clues not named yet coming first, and until there are some those slots are ordinary questions; and All, every question name-it, any clue. Being able to pick something out doesn't mean being able to produce it, so recognising comes first and naming is the test after. Typed answers are priced from the house's lower chance of recall (30%, 12% or 5% by difficulty, against 60%, 35% and 15% for knowing it among options), so they pay longer odds. Case, accents, punctuation, a leading article and a part in brackets don't matter, and a slip of a letter in a name of five letters or more (two from nine) still counts, the reveal showing the right spelling (`typing.js`, shared with Deck and Parley, tested in `tests/typing.mjs`). A name you miss goes to the review pile as a name-it card, and Deck asks it the same way: the hint, a box to type in, Check. Naming one right marks it named in the record; missing it unmarks it. In a match the questions are dealt from the starter's record, and a device with name-it off chooses from four of the answer's clues instead of typing.

A subject bank's options (Maths, Art and the rest) come in a new order every run, with "all of the above" and its kind kept last; they used to keep the bank's order, which gave answers away.

A pass is a bet of 0%: Pass waits for an answer like Bet does, the answer is kept, and every count treats it as a bet (the stats' hit rate and Kelly view, with a "passed (0%)" band; the Ledger, where a pass believed no more than the price's own chance, 1/odds, and shows as "Passed (0%)").

A run deals from topics, not a level: Countries and Commodities (Crates' clue bank, each its own topic) beside the subject banks (Maths, Refining, Reasoning, Words, Cities, Flags, Patterns, Wine, Art, Economics, Physics, Chemistry, Code, Philosophy, Religion). In the menu each topic is tapped round out (dashed), in (white) and more (highlighted, dealt twice as often), with three presets (Balanced, Trader, Culture night, as in Crates); one difficulty range (1–10) applies to every topic, and each subject bank keeps its own stages. Every question is on the same 1–10 scale (a clue question's from how obscure its clue is and how many options it has) and a run is priced as one batch (`mixQuestions` in punt-gen.js), so a country question pays like any other. Easy, Medium and Hard are gone as choices (their runs still play to the end). A run's link carries its topics (`&t=countries.1,maths.2&df=…`); in the pile a question keeps its own topic's key.


Wrong options are real alternatives, never padding. In Philosophy each wrong answer is another thinker's or school's actual idea, stated correctly (Plato's Forms beside Aristotle's form-in-matter, Democritus' atoms and Heraclitus' flux; Zeno's Achilles beside his own arrow, the sorites and the liar), so a question asks which idea is whose; in Reasoning the wrong answers are plausible considerations that don't do the job (most strengthen an argument the question asks you to weaken). In Refining each wrong answer is something real that belongs to another unit, stream or spec: what HVO brings to a diesel pool sits beside FAME's, LCO's and coker gas oil's own profiles; cloud point beside pour point, CFPP and flash point; gasoline's induction period beside existent gum, copper strip and the Rancimat (FAME's test). None is secretly true of what's asked: LCO's density, which really is too high for EN 590, isn't offered against it. Options are of matching length: once the right answer was the longest every time (four times the wrong ones'), which gave it away. `tests/fair-options.mjs` holds those banks (Philosophy, Reasoning, Refining) to it (the right option the longest in at most 40% of questions, on average at most 1.2 times the wrong ones' length); a bank joins it once its options are evened out. Still to do: religion, chemistry, economics, code and physics have the same tell; wine a milder one.

The odds run on a scale from 1.1× (the easiest question) to 3× (the hardest), by how hard the house thinks a question is for a typical player (its fair price, 1/chance, with the house's spread of opinion); the scale's ends sit at fair prices of 1.16 and 1.54, tuned so a long run of Balanced questions averages about 2× (measured: 1.98×, the easiest twentieth at 1.1×, the hardest at 3×). It's a game's scale, not a bookmaker's: there's no house margin any more, a typical player comes out ahead on a long run, and the skill is in the stake. A typed answer pays more, by how much rarer it is than a pick, up to 4×.

No subject question gives its answer away. Every bank was audited (442 of 3,067 questions did: the right option clearly the longest, or the only one with a formula or a number) and those questions rewritten, every option as long, specific and plausible as the answer: often a neighbouring idea (Veblen for Giffen, contango for backwardation, Bellman-Ford for Dijkstra, the expected shortfall for VaR, jihad as a false fifth pillar) or the answer with one thing changed; detail that only the answer carried moved to the explanation. `tests/banks.mjs` holds every bank to it.

## Cartel
Coup and Liar's Dice built from dice. Everyone has gold dice (lives and roles; two each) and plain dice (money and weight in the bidding; three to start, at most eight), all hidden. A face is both a number and a role: 1 Fixer, 2 Banker, 3 Trader, 4 Auditor, 5 Legal, 6 Regulator. Only gold dice give powers; every die counts in bids, 1s (Fixers) as any face.

Tap any gold die you can see (yours, or one of theirs that's been shown) for what its role does. There's no Pass: taking a die is always at least as good, and rerolling a face-up die covers the rest. A turn goes: before your move, if you like, claim Fixer to reroll one of your dice (free, once; say one the table has seen); one move; after it, if you like, ask a player a question about their whole hand (free, once; they learn the same about yours); then a bid. The question comes after the move, so it shapes the bid but can't make a steal or a sanction safe; the Fixer comes before it, so dice that land face up from the bank or a steal stay seen for a round. Moves: take a die from the bank (it lands face up, so taking costs secrecy); reroll any of your dice secretly (gold ones change your roles); hit (pay 7 dice and they lose a gold die; with a full hand of 8 you must); or claim a role your gold dice show, true or not: Banker takes 3 dice from the bank, Trader steals 2 from a player, Auditor looks at all of a player's dice, and Regulator has two abilities: an inquiry (learn how many dice on the table show a face) or a sanction (pay 4 dice and a player loses a gold die; the dice are spent even if it's blocked, as Coup's assassin pays). Using a role leaves the gold die as it is. Legal blocks steals and sanctions (Coup's Captain and Contessa in one). Any claim, blocks included, can be challenged by the player it's aimed at (the next player, for claims aimed at no one; the blocked player, for a block), and whoever is wrong pays the other 3 dice: the bluffer if caught, the challenger if the claim was true (the proof is shown); short of 3, they pay what they have and lose a gold die. A claimant whose claim (or block) is proven then chooses what becomes of the proof: keep it shown, and nobody will challenge that role while it stays, though everyone knows one of their gold dice; or put it back, rerolled unseen, so their gold dice are hidden again (and it may come up as another role). Then the claim takes effect, or the block stands. The bid raises "at least N dice show F" or calls it; the referee says only whether it held, and whoever was wrong pays the other 3 dice. A player who can't pay in full loses a gold die as well, and with none left is out. Information persists: nothing is revealed or rerolled unless someone makes it so.

Balance (October 2026, thousands of AI games; `RULES` in the engine switches each rule so variants can be compared). With questions as an action and challenges costing 2 dice, asking was a trap (a player who asked a lot won 9% against three Quants, where a fair share is 25%) and bluffing didn't pay (never bluffing won 30%, heavy bluffing 13%); Banker was 43% of all actions. Questions as a free move get used about 14 times a game with no effect on balance. Coup-style stakes (a lost challenge costs a gold die) halved the gap between honest and bluffing play (24% against 16%) and cut Banker to about 30% of actions, but felt too drastic; challenges now cost 3 dice (a gold die only if you can't pay), which plays like the old stakes: about 23 turns, Banker about 42% of actions, never bluffing 27% against heavy bluffing 9%. `RULES.goldStakes` switches the Coup version back on. Seats are fair (25% each), the opening roll barely matters (except two 1s on the gold dice, back when the 1 had no power: about 1 player in 36), the lead changes about 7 times a game and the winner had been last at some point in most games. Roles (400-game studies; win rate when a gold die shows the role at the start, 25% fair): with Auditor and Legal blocking steals, Banker 21%, Trader 25%, Auditor 31%, Legal 29%, Regulator 23%, and steals landed only 28% of the time; with Trader blocking steals instead (now the rule), Banker 28%, Trader 25%, Auditor 30%, Legal 26%, Regulator 23%, and steals land about 38% of the time. Holding a role is worth most when it blocks something (a bluffed block is risky; any other ability can be bluffed as easily as used), which leaves Regulator, with nothing to block, the weakest to hold; a cheaper sanction, an inquiry counting every face, and Regulator blocking Banker didn't change that. The AIs now value an inquiry by what it does to the turn's bid (with a bid standing, the exact count of its face turns the call or raise into a sure thing), so they use it about 2.5 times a game and win about 80% of the calls they make after one. An inquiry was almost as easy to bluff as to use (only about 15% of claims get challenged), so holding a real Regulator added little; now the referee answers only a real Regulator (an Auditor's look can still be bluffed: the other player shows their dice, and can't check), which brings the Regulator to 24% and every role with a power within 24–30% (Legal highest), and the 1 (then Wild, with no power) 20%. Then the free reroll moved from Legal to the 1 (renamed from Wild to Fixer, so every face has a power) and the steal block from Trader to Legal (so Legal, left with only sanctions, isn't thin): two independent 400-game samples put every role at 23–26%, the most even of everything tried. Still open: called bids hold only about 30% of the time, because the forced raise each turn leaves the last raiser stuck. The question moved after the move and the Fixer's reroll before it (October 2026; `RULES.askAfterMove`, `RULES.fixerFirst`): on the same 400 seeds with four Quants, the old order and the new one play alike (30.4 turns either way; every role 21–27% before and 20–29% after, the Auditor up two points and the Banker down two; Banker 23% of actions in both). The computer players never used the old order's tricks (asking whether a target holds Legal before stealing from them, or hiding a die that had just landed face up), so the change is aimed at human play.

Keeping or putting back a proven die (October 2026; `RULES.provenReroll`: "may" is the rule, "keep" the old one, "must" Coup's, where a proven card goes back in the deck), 400 games each on the same seeds, four Quants and mixed tables: game length, the Banker's share, how often players bluff and every role (20–30%) didn't move beyond chance. A player whose claim was proven won 32% (four Quants) and 36% (mixed) when the proof stayed shown, 37% and 38% when it went back: a shown gold die is more a liability than an asset, the safe repeat claims it buys being only 3–4% of claims while the whole table knows one of your roles all game. Challenging pays a little less (35% of challenges caught a bluff with four Quants, 31% with the proof put back; 50% and 43–45% at mixed tables), which takes 3–5 points off the Sceptic. Choosing and always putting back played alike; the computer players keep a proven Banker (income nobody will challenge) or Legal (a block nobody will test) and put back the rest, about three proofs in four.

Two gold dice, not one: with one you'd hold a given role only 1 time in 6, so challenging would nearly always pay and bluffing would die out (with two it's about 1 in 3), and a single bad payment would knock you out.

You play against two or three computer players (choose them in the menu). Each keeps a Mind that follows the game exactly as its seat sees it: every die's owner, every face seen (public, audited or its own), every answer, proof, caught bluff, call verdict and inquiry count for as long as each stays true, and everyone's habits (how often they're caught bluffing, how often they challenge, how honest their bids have been). It samples tables consistent with all that, weighted by what bids suggest, and decides from the probabilities: its action, its free move, whether to challenge, block (honestly, or a bluffed block when the thief seldom challenges) or challenge a block, and how to bid. Personalities: Quant (rational: plays the probabilities, tuned to what the tournaments reward), Wildcat (somewhat random), Gambler (risk taker: bluffs over half its claims, calls late), Hedger (conservative: hardly bluffs, builds and hides its hand), Sceptic (suspicious: challenges and asks a lot). In 200-game tournaments with shuffled seating the Quant and the Hedger win about a third of their games each, the Sceptic and Wildcat about a fifth, the Gambler least; `tests/cartel.mjs` plays a tournament and checks the rules one by one on hand-built games. Your own view is built the same way, so the table shows exactly what you could know: a dark cup is a die you can't see, a dashed edge is one only you know, a dot is one everyone has seen.

Computer turns come at the pace chosen in the menu: Fast (each move shown for 0.7 s), Steady (1.6 s) or Tap to continue (each move stays until you tap anywhere on the table). Once you're out, the rest of the game plays itself quickly.

`cartel-engine.js` is the referee, `cartel-ai.js` the players, `cartel.js` the table. Playing together (two people and the AIs) comes next: the referee would run on one device.

Targets are chosen on the table: when a move needs one (a steal, an audit, a sanction, a question, a hit), the seats that can be chosen glow, the chosen one is ringed and labelled, and tapping another seat changes it; the move panel keeps only the move.

Your turn's panel: on your move, four tiles (Take, Reroll, Claim, Hit); Claim opens six cards three to a row, the Fixer's free reroll first (dashed), then Banker, Trader, Auditor and the Regulator's inquiry and sanction, each role's name green when your gold dice show it and red when claiming it would be a bluff, with what can block it in small print on the button. On your bid: the faces, the count, and Ask, Call and Bid side by side; Ask opens the question (its kind, a face or a total, and a button that names the player and the question). The screen never scrolls: the turn panel keeps one height whatever it shows (the tallest move, a claim, fits it on a 664-pixel phone), and the table takes all the rest, so it keeps one size all game (326 px on a 664-pixel phone, 482 px at 844). Each seat's dice take two rows and shrink as hands grow, yours one row; what you know is one line along the table's near edge that scrolls sideways; the panel scrolls inside itself only on the smallest phones.

Each seat keeps its player's whole last turn until their next turn starts, so a round played fast can still be read: their move and what came of it ("Trader on you: stole 2", "Banker: a bluff, caught", a quiet Fixer reroll riding on the move's line), their question, with both answers when you're one of the two ("Asked you: how many 4s?" then "Quant 2 · you 1": what each learned about the other's hand; between two others the answers stay theirs, so there's no line for them), their bid or call, and any gold die lost. The log is seven lines tall; when a turn has more than fits (a long line wraps onto two), whole lines drop, so nothing is ever cut off part-way: a Fixer reroll first, then the bid (the middle of the table shows it too), then a question together with its answers; the move and any loss stay longest. Your own last question and both answers sit under your hand; a call's verdict sits where the bid stood until someone bids again.

## Spot
Sums fly across a dark field: thrown up from below in an arc, flung in from a side and falling away, or dropping faster and faster with a sway, each on its own path. Three lives; every 5 right in a row raises the score multiplier by half, up to ×3.

The pace is a budget of difficulty: every sum has a cost (1 for a small addition, 2 for a times, 3 for a division, +1 for bigger numbers, +1 under a modifier), and the run has a budget of difficulty a second that rises slowly (0.45 at the start, 1.3 at four minutes, then gently on). A cheap sum is followed quickly by the next; a dear one buys a few seconds, so you might get four easy sums in a row or one division with time to think. Lifetimes scale with cost (5.3 s for a small addition, 12 s for a big division) and shorten by a fifth over four minutes. Times tables fade in from 12 s, division from 35 s, bigger numbers from 60 s; the level shown goes up one every 30 s.

Modifiers: from 45 s, a badge in the header shows an operation to apply to every result before anything else (+1, +2, +3, −1, −2, +10 or ×2), changing every 20 s; a bit over a third of the time there is none. Each sum is tagged with the modifier it came under, so a change mid-flight is never ambiguous. Alone, the shelf shows modified answers, and the unmodified answer is always planted among the fakes. Together, each player has their own modifier, and a pair is two sums that make the same number after each player's own modifier, so the numbers you call are the modified ones.

Alone: the answers wait on a shelf of eight round tokens below, among fakes. Tap an answer before its sum runs out; a wrong tap or a sum that runs out costs a life. Fakes are the slips you'd make: off by one or ten, swapped digits, a neighbouring times-table answer, adding instead of multiplying, forgetting the modifier. No two sums on screen show the same value, no fake matches one, and no number shows twice on a shelf. Play a random run or today's run (the same for everyone that day); bests are kept.

Together there's no shelf, only sums, and each of you sees only your own. Most come in pairs: a sum on one screen and a sum making the same number on the other, the second arriving 0.6–4 s after the first. You call out what yours make; your partner taps the one of theirs that makes a number you called, now or when it arrives (a tap clears the pair, even a partner still to appear). Some sums have no partner: tapping one costs a life. A pair that leaves both screens untapped costs a life. Lives and score are shared, and no number means two things at once.

How it's built: everything comes from a seed and the taps. `spot-engine.js` makes the timeline from the seed alone (the budget, the modifiers per seat, the pairs) and replays the taps over it to get the state at any moment (`play()`), so two phones with the same seed and taps see the same game, and only taps travel through the room (each with its time on the database clock, which `sync.js` provides as an offset). The page replays the run every frame; flight paths come from each sum's id. Solo runs pause while the app is in the background, and the screen stays awake while you play. `tests/spot.mjs` checks the timelines, the pace, the modifiers, the pairs and the replays.

## Quote
Make a market on a number you don't know: a bid and an ask. Every question carries documented tiers, the radius a market may have around the truth for each grade: SS (exact, where exactness can be expected), S, A, B; wider is a C, a miss. Marie Curie's birth (1867): the 1860s is an S, 1850–1880 an A, 1850–1900 a B, 1800–1900 a miss. A market's radius is the distance from the truth to its farther edge, plus the miss again when the truth lies outside, so a confident wrong market grades harder than a wide right one and a near miss (1870–1880) still earns an A. The payoff is stepwise by grade (300 at exact, 250 at the S edge, 180 at A, 100 at B, 0 at twice the B radius, then a loss growing towards −300) with a continuous run between the steps, so a touch tighter earns a touch more, all scaled by the question's difficulty (1 to 5: a 1 pays 0.7 of the curve, a 5 pays 1.3). The card shows the tiers; the shortcuts set a market of the S, A or B radius around what you've typed; the verdict names the grade. Only a B or below banks the question to the pile: an A is adequate knowledge. Tiers and difficulty are in `quote-bank.js` (`tiers`, `d`), derived from each question's range (years: S 2.5× the range, A 2.5× S, B 4.5× S; magnitudes: S 1.5×, A 3.5×, B 7×) and meant to be edited by hand. Know a figure exactly? Give it as both bid and ask: a market on one figure, drawn as a dot on the tape, and the only way to an SS on a question that can be known exactly.

Every question has its own range for "close" (`tol` in the bank): ±1% on Everest's height, ±15% on a city's population, ±40% on Picasso's output; 2 years on a modern date, 20 on an ancient one; whole units on a count. The card says it ("close means ±15%"). The payout is continuous: inside the market it is 300·e^(−v/2), with v the width in ranges, so a point market exactly right pays 300, one a range wide about 180 and one five ranges wide about 25; outside, that fades by e^(−u) with u the miss in ranges while a loss of 300·(1 − e^(−u/3)) grows in its place, so a miss by a third of a range still pays about 60% of exact, a miss by a whole range about breaks even, and far out costs up to 300. It's continuous across the market's edge. Widths and misses are in doublings for magnitudes (log2 of the ratio, the range converted the same way) and plain units otherwise. Shortcuts under the fields set a market of half, one, two or four ranges around what you've typed. After you quote, a tape shows your market and where the truth fell, with a note on the basis of the figure.

Together (Menu → Play together): two phones taking turns. One makes the market without seeing anything else; the other then sees it and hits the bid (sells to the maker at it), lifts the offer (buys at it) or passes. A trade settles between the two by how far the traded price was off the truth, in the question's ranges, on the same smooth curve (up to 300 each way); a pass settles the maker against the house as when alone. Ten questions, five markets each, higher book wins; the closing bell shows both books and each question's result.

Refining sets (Menu → A refining set / Today's refining set) draw ten questions from the refining category alone, with their own bests and links (`&f=refining`): European fuel specs (EN 228, EN 590, EN 15940, EN 14214 limits), the octane, vapour pressure and density of each blendstock, cetane and cold flow by component, energy and oxygen contents, conversions, RED III and the THG-quota, fuel taxes and duty, buy-out prices and crack spreads: 111 questions, so a player who crushes them has the numbers of the European road-fuel trade.

`quote-bank.js` holds the questions (286 across trade, cities, art, wine, people, geography, markets and refining), `quote-engine.js` settles markets and picks sets (seeded, at most two questions from a category), and `tests/quote.mjs` checks both.

The page fits the screen: once a market is settled the bid and ask boxes and the width shortcuts make way for the result (the tape shows your market against the truth), and a long note scrolls inside. Widths are rounded where they're made (0.1 × 3 used to show as 0.30000000000000004).

A question shows its unit only: the grade widths ("S ±3 ppm · A ±7 · B ±15") and the width shortcuts gave away the scale of the answer, so you work out your own range. A range that holds the answer never loses money, however wide, and always earns something: past the curve's B, 30% of B's payoff out to twice the B radius, shrinking as the range widens (100 to 1,500 on Switzerland's GDP of 885 earns about +15). Its verdict is yellow, "Wide, but in range", never red; red is for a range that missed. Below A it still goes to the review pile. A range that misses never earns, by however little: it's a C and loses at least 10, more the further the answer is beyond it against the question's B radius (a year beyond on a ±15-year question loses about 14; five beyond about 34), up to the old losses for big misses.

## Manifest
A stack of coloured containers (columns of varying height) drops into one isometric stage, container by container with a bounce, holds, and is gone; then you're asked about it. A run is twelve rounds, or until three lives are gone.

The order book: each round opens with the orders for its stack, the questions to come posted by kind and not by detail, each with a price. How many of one colour (which colour isn't said), how many in all, how many on tier 2, the colour at a spot on tier 2, the top of a column, the most common colour, a spot with the stack seen from the side or from behind (from round 3), and which of four stacks is yours, turned (from round 5). You choose what to watch. The stack comes in by the round's view (tier by tier; all at once, inner containers hidden; slice by slice; or two tiers and then the rest) and holds. Ship it ends the hold early: the share of the hold you didn't use pays up to half as much again on the round's answers. Then the questions come as cards, each with its answers in view, so one tap answers it; a question about a place on the stack carries its own picture beside its answers (the floor with the spot marked, or the stack turned and grey with one container lit), so two such questions never share one stage. Answer any; pass any by leaving it (the button below ends the round). A right answer pays its price, a wrong one costs a life, a pass costs nothing. Questions only ask about what the view showed you (visibility is settled exactly, by drawing the cubes back to front onto a small raster).

Prices grow 12% a round and follow your record for each kind (a hit rate that weights recent answers): a kind you usually get right pays down to 0.6 of its price, marked ↓; one you usually miss up to 1.4, marked ↑. The strategy that pays keeps moving.

## Survey

## Blend
The blender's job. An order comes in, a product with its specification and a sales price: summer or winter E10 (EN 228 class A or F), Super Plus 98 E5, winter E85, summer or winter B7 (EN 590 class B or F), arctic class 1, paraffinic diesel (EN 15940), Jet A-1 with SAF, VLSFO 0.5% (ISO 8217 RMG 380), ECA marine gasoil 0.1%. You have a tank farm of components with real properties and prices (Eurobob, reformate, alkylate, isomerate, FCC naphtha, light straight-run, butane, ethanol, ETBE, toluene; straight-run gas oil, hydrocracker diesel, LCO, kerosene, arctic and summer HVO, RME, PME, UCOME, heavy gas oil, GTL, diesel slops; jet kerosenes and HEFA; residues, marine gasoil, cutter stock) and set each in steps of 5% until the blend adds up to 100 and every line of the specification passes, at the best margin you can. Renewable content earns the compliance credit (UCOME double). Par is the exact best blend on the 5% grid, found by trying every one. A spec line turns green or red as you go; tap it for what the property is and which components move it; tap a component for its note and full properties. Hint names the best 5% move: the one that fixes most of the shortfall, or, once you pass, the one that earns most. Certify when every line passes; the order is done within €5 of par.

The maths is the practice: most properties blend linearly by volume, octane by blending values (ethanol's RON of 120), vapour pressure by blending RVP (ethanol's 130 kPa at low blends), and flash point, cold flow (CFPP, freeze point) and viscosity by index, so the component that is worst in that property dominates: 5% of slops at a flash point of 24 takes straight-run diesel from 66 to 45 °C, and 20% kerosene takes it to 54; lubricity saturates with about 2% FAME. The orders teach the constraints that bite: the vapour-pressure ceiling on summer E10 against ethanol, the floor in winter and butane's 358 kPa, aromatics and olefins caps against reformate and FCC naphtha, the 5% ethanol limit on Super 98, LCO against cetane and polyaromatics, kerosene against cetane, viscosity and flash point in winter diesel, the density window against HVO, palm ester's +10 °C CFPP, a slops cargo's flash point, the arctic class, EN 15940's aromatics ban, the aromatics floor that caps HEFA in jet, sweetening residue to 0.5% at distillate prices. Figures are textbook-typical. `blend-engine.js`; `tests/blend.mjs` checks every order has a passing blend, the blending maths, and that hints lead to one.

The page fits the screen: the order in two lines (its story behind About), a compact margin card with the verdict beside Hint and Certify, the specification as chips in the spec's order (red off spec; tap one for the limit, this blend's value and why), and the tank farm taking the rest and scrolling inside itself, each tank's figures on one line (tap its name for the rest).

## Pipes
Oil is already on its way. The board is laid with pipe tiles in random rotations (straights, bends, crossings whose two channels don't mix, pumps, and rock); tap a tile to turn it a quarter clockwise. After a planning countdown (22 s on level 1, down to 12 s) the flow leaves the wellhead: gas crosses a tile every 2.8 s (down to 1.5 s), crude at two thirds of that pace and, when it shares the board, eight seconds behind; each tile entered is locked. A dead end, the edge of the field, a pipe that's already full or the wrong terminal is a spill. Pressure lasts 8 tiles after the wellhead or a pump and only a pump refills it, so every board has a direct line that runs dry and a detour through a pump that doesn't: the detour leaves the line at a bend and rejoins it at another, the same tiles turned the other way, so choosing the pump is choosing the route. A delivery pays its netback: the price on the terminal, less 10 a tile of pipe and 40 a pump the flow passes, plus time (the tenths of a second left of a 90-second window once the oil goes). A terminal's price is the cost of its designed route plus the level's margin, so a longer route than needed nets less. On levels 3 and 4 there are two terminals: a near one, and a far one that always shows the higher price but costs more pipe and pumps to reach; its premium is set to beat that extra cost by 80–180, or to fall short of it by as much, so either can be the right pick, and the note after delivery says which was. Fill it now is there from the first moment, countdown included: the oil goes at once and the whole route fills in about half a second (flow time runs 80× fast, in 50 ms steps, so two products meet a crossing in the same order they would at speed), and the time that would have taken counts for the time bonus as if pumped at ×4. A route that isn't ready spills just the same, so it's for when you're sure; otherwise the flow runs at its own pace. Three lives; a spill costs one, pays nothing, and the level comes back as a fresh board.

Levels: 1–4 one product on a 5×7 board; from level 5 two products from two wellheads to their own terminals, which must cross and may only do so at a crossing; from level 9 crude and gas meet at a blender (inlets left and right, outlet below) and the blend goes on to a blend terminal. Boards grow a size every four levels to 7×9; the pressure budget is 8 tiles; levels 3 and 4 have two terminals. Every level is carved from real routes before it's scrambled: a line per product, a detour through a pump on every product line it can fit (every line on one-product levels, at least one on the others), pumps wherever else the pressure budget needs them, and the generator accepts the board only once running it proves that every designed route delivers, every direct line stalls for want of pressure, and (one product) no route at all reaches the far terminal without a pump. Random runs and today's run; bests by score and level. `pipes-engine.js` builds levels and moves the flow; `tests/pipes.mjs` checks 180 levels for all of that, that either terminal can be the better pick, that scrambled boards don't work by luck, the netback, and the pressure rule.

The page fits the screen: the board takes the height left and keeps its shape, and the buttons and the note keep their room whether or not they're showing, so the board never changes size mid-level.

## Refinery
A flowsheet puzzle that teaches what every refinery unit eats and makes. Each level gives you a crude with its assay (API, sulphur, the cut yields), a set of units and a price deck; the crude unit's cuts appear as streams and you route each one to a unit or a product pool. Units turn their feed into outputs by yield tables (the vacuum unit by the crude's own residue split, the hydrocracker swelling the barrel), hydrotreaters carry the feed's octane or cetane through and set its sulphur, the reformer makes hydrogen and everything else that treats or cracks with hydrogen uses it; the shortfall comes from the hydrogen plant, or at three times the price without one. Pools blend what they're given by volume and must meet their spec (gasoline RON 95 and 10 ppm; diesel cetane 51 and 10 ppm; jet only jet-grade streams; fuel oil 0.5% sulphur) or they sell as something cheaper: off-spec gasoline as naphtha, off-spec diesel as fuel oil, sour fuel oil as high-sulphur fuel oil. The margin is revenue less crude, bought-in renewables, operating cost and hydrogen, in $/bbl of crude, shown against par, which a solver finds by local search with pair moves (pools have cliffs: two streams often have to move together). A level is done within $0.6/bbl of par with gasoline, diesel and jet in spec (fuel oil falling to high-sulphur fuel oil is priced, not a failure). Help: every option in the destination picker shows what it would do to the margin, judged with any streams it creates sent to their best places (so heavy naphtha to the hydrotreater is judged with the treated naphtha going on to the reformer), and choosing it routes those streams the same way; the Hint button names the single change worth most, with the follow-on routings, or, when no single change helps because two streams have to move together, where par differs; the fifth hint shows par's whole routing with the differences marked and a button to take it. Sulphur in the straight-run cuts scales with the crude's assay, so a sweet crude's residue makes VLSFO and a sour one's doesn't.

Fourteen units with cards (purpose, three paragraphs, feeds, yield table, hydrogen, operating cost, and what it's running at now): crude and vacuum distillation, the naphtha hydrotreater, the reformer, isomerisation, the FCC and its naphtha post-treater, the hydrocracker, alkylation, the diesel and kerosene treaters, the coker, visbreaker, bitumen blowing, the hydrogen plant, the sulphur plant and an HVO unit; some 40 streams with their blending properties and a line each; six crudes from Bonny Light to Maya. The campaign is the syllabus, one unit at a time: hydroskimming; the vacuum unit and the FCC; sulphur (Urals); octane (isomerisation); the hydrocracker and the hydrogen plant (Arab Heavy); alkylation; the coker, visbreaker and bitumen (Maya); renewables (HVO, ethanol and FAME bought in, with the ticket value); and full complexity with the crude yours to choose. Yields, properties and prices are textbook-typical and labelled as such. Routing is saved per level, with bests and completion in the menu. `refinery-data.js`, `refinery-engine.js`, `refinery-levels.js`; `tests/refinery.mjs` checks the data, that par beats the default on every level, and the chemistry.

The page fits the screen: the level in two lines (its story behind About), a compact margin card, the units in one row that scrolls sideways, and Streams or Pools in two tabs taking the rest and scrolling inside; the Pools tab says how the pools stand ("Pools · 1 off spec" in red), so you see it while routing.

## Rush
Chess puzzle rush: tactical puzzles, one to four moves, as many as you can. Three minutes, five minutes, or survival (no clock); three wrong moves end the run. The page opens straight onto an easy warm-up puzzle; solving it starts the clock, and it counts for nothing (not the score, not the rating). The board is the full width of the screen. The board shows the position from your side, the opponent's move plays after a beat, and you move by dragging a piece or by tapping it and then a square, with the legal targets shown; every move has to be the puzzle's, except that any checkmate is accepted where the solution mates. Premoves: tap your next move while the opponent is replying and it plays the instant the reply lands, if it's legal then, and is judged like any other move (the squares show purple while it waits). A miss shows the line that was wanted before the next puzzle. Your rating decides what you're served: a running rating across every run, Elo-style against each puzzle's rating, that moves fast for your first twenty puzzles and slowly after, with gains halving at every miss in a run so a careless run can't climb; it shows in the header (with a ? while it's settling) and can be reset from the menu. A run starts about 250 below your rating and climbs 40 a solve to about 150 above it (a miss doesn't raise it); today's run ignores ratings so everyone gets the same puzzles. The results sheet gives the count, accuracy, your rating and its change over the run, the hardest solved, your best for the mode, and every miss with its line and a link to the puzzle on Lichess. A new run or today's run (the same puzzles for everyone that day); bests per mode. A timed run ends if the app goes to the background.

The bank is 20,000 puzzles from the Lichess puzzle database (CC0), popular ones with one to four moves to find, 2,000 in each of ten rating bands from 600 to 2,400+, in `puzzles/band-*.txt`. A run fetches a band the first time it climbs into it (about 200 KB each, so a run starts in a second and never downloads what it doesn't reach), and the service worker keeps fetched bands in a cache of their own that app updates leave alone, so they work offline afterwards. The rules are chess.js (BSD, in `vendor/`) and the pieces the cburnett set by Colin M. L. Burnett (CC BY-SA 3.0), via `chess-board.js`.

The page fits the screen: the board is square, as wide as the phone or as tall as the room left, whichever is less.

After a miss the board shows your move, then the wanted one as its two squares: yellow where it starts, green where it lands. (That square's mark was once called "hint", which picked up the suite's clue-button style, a 30-pixel box, and grew far past the board; it's "want" now.)

## Deck
The pile, reviewed: what you didn't know, brought back until you do. Every knowledge game records an item when you show you don't know it: Punt when you answer wrong, pass, or stake 20% or less; Quote when the truth is outside your market or the market is wider than three ranges; Chart when a pin is over 500 km off (unless it's within 600 km inside the right country, as Deck counts it) or you took a clue; Crates when a clue's crate is got wrong (through its own learn deck); Rush when you miss a puzzle. Items move through six piles with growing gaps, Leitner's boxes on Pimsleur's clock: ultra-short (due at once), short (10 minutes), medium (12 hours), long (a week), longer (a month), longest (three months); right in the longest and it's learned. Chess puzzles skip the short-term piles: a tactic seen ten minutes or twelve hours ago is still in mind, so they go ultra-short, then a week, then three months, then learned. (Until October 2026 the week was the last pile; what had been learned by then came back once, a month after it was learned, so nothing skips the long checks.) Right moves an item up a pile, in Deck or in the game that banked it (a confident right answer in Punt, a tight quote, a clean pin, a solved puzzle); wrong drops it to the first; right in the long pile and it's learned. Deck shows the piles and what's due per game, and a review asks each due item back in its own form: Punt's questions as options (drawings included), Crates' clues as which-country-or-commodity, Chart's places as which-country in the first pile and then on the map of their region (right within a share of the map's width that narrows up the piles, about 500, 310 and 190 km on Asia's map, or within 600 km anywhere inside the right country, since a lot of the countries are vast; a map whose region doesn't reach a place, as Asia's doesn't reach Norilsk, widens to take it in), Chart's countries on the map, anywhere inside the outline, Quote's numbers as a number judged in the question's own range, Rush's puzzles on the board. Up to 40 a review, the most revised first: the highest pile first (what's lasted longest is what a lapse would cost most), the longest overdue first within a pile, so moving a new miss up from the ultra-short pile waits until every longer check is done. The games' learning modes deal what's due in the same order.

Learning mode, on by default and switchable from Deck's, Punt's, Quote's or Chart's menu: Punt leads each block of a bank level with what's due (up to half the block, spread through it) and fills the rest with questions you haven't seen before any you have; Quote and Chart lead their sets the same way. Off, the games deal as they always did and still bank what you miss. Crates keeps its own learn mode, which feeds the pile. `pile.js` is the store and scheduler; `tests/pile.mjs` checks promotion, demotion, gaps and learning.

No clicks needed. A typed card (Quote's numbers) has its field ready to type in, and the field stays focused from one typed card to the next (on a phone the keyboard stays up): type, Enter to answer, then either Enter to move on or simply type the next answer, which starts the next card with it. On a card with options, 1 to 9 pick one and Enter moves on.

Parley's cards say their word as they appear, in the course's own language, with a loudspeaker beside it to hear it again. The voice is the phone's own (`voice.js`, shared with Parley). Browsers let a page speak only after a tap, and Review is that tap, so even the first card is heard; the first touch anywhere also primes the voice with a silent word.

No card can be learned by its position: a card's options come in a new order every deal ("all of the above" and its kind stay last), and a Punt card whose options come from Crates' bank gets fresh wrong answers each time (other countries or commodities of the kind; other clues the answer doesn't carry). A Chart place is a which-country card only in the first pile; from the second pile on it's plotted: tap where it is on the map of its region, right within a share of the map's width that narrows as the card climbs (a 25th, a 40th, a 60th: on Asia's map about 500, 310 and 190 km), with the distance and the true spot shown after.

## Parley
Three beginner courses from an English speaker's side: Chinese, French and German, on the pile's schedule. A course is units of about 14 words, each unit opening with its grammar pattern (three genders and *sein*; the accusative *den*; verb second; the partitive *du / de la*; *aller* and *au*; measure words and 块; 在 and 去; 了) and closing, once all its words are met, with a short text written only from words learned so far, read aloud by the phone, with three questions. Every word card carries the word with its article (French and German) or its characters and pinyin with tone marks (Chinese), the meaning, an example sentence, and a button that speaks it; a new word is shown on its card, spoken, and drilled at once. Study brings what's due first, then up to 12 new words a day, unit by unit. Every unit is open: Learn on its row teaches all of its words not yet met, whatever the day's count (choosing a unit is choosing the pace), its pattern opens from the row any time, and Read appears once its words are all met; a reading reached by jumping ahead says it may use words from units not yet finished.

A word is drilled harder as it climbs the piles: recognise it (word and voice, pick the meaning); pick it by ear (voice only), or for Chinese name the tone of its first syllable; produce it (type the word, accent-tolerant, and tap its article for a gendered noun; for Chinese type the pinyin with tones, ni3 hao3 or nǐ hǎo, and the sounds right but the tones wrong is a miss with a note); fill it into its sentence from four choices. The right answer sits anywhere among the choices, and no wrong one means the same or sounds the same (a Chinese homophone can't be told apart by ear). Right moves it up a pile, wrong drops it to the first, as everywhere; Deck reviews Parley's cards too. Six units each for now: hello, numbers and time, eating and drinking, places and going, shopping, work and trade (oil, prices, the boss). `parley-courses.js` is the content, written by hand and meant to grow; the German course is for a learner from English.

The page fits the screen: the units list, a drill's card and a reading (its passage and questions together) scroll inside themselves. The voice lives in `voice.js`, which Deck uses too when it reviews Parley's words.

## Brut
Wine, behind the "…" tile. The name is the driest common Champagne and the French for crude oil (*le brut*).

Blind: a flight of six classic wines (39 in all, from Chablis and Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc to Barolo, Amarone, Vintage Port and Dézaley Chasselas), each with what a taster finds at each stage. You see it first: the colour in a drawn glass (bubbles rising for sparkling wines) and the words for it; taste further for the nose, then the palate (sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body and finish as dots), then the conclusions (Old or New World, climate, oak). A right call pays 100 on sight, 70 on the nose, 45 on the palate, 25 on the conclusions, 15 off for each wrong call; the reveal gives the tell ("Pale in the glass but huge in tannin and acid, tar and roses: Nebbiolo"). A new flight or today's flight (the same for everyone); best flights kept; grapes on the candidates can be hidden from the menu for a harder flight. ## Harbour
Plan, then run. A harbour of hexes, ships you place where you like, and a program for each: one instruction per hour. Ships steer as ships do, relative to where they're heading: ahead; port, a 60° turn to the left and a hex on; starboard, the same to the right; astern, a hex back still facing the same way; and load, discharge, wait. A ship can't turn on the spot, so jetties sit on the channels round islands, and a dead end means backing out. A program written that way runs the same turned to any of the six directions, or mirrored with port and starboard swapped, so a group of ships and their programs can later be reused facing another way. Every program loops, the longest setting the loop for all of them, and they run in lockstep, so the whole plan is a machine you watch go round. A ship that would end on land or off the map runs aground; two that would end on one hex, or pass through each other, collide; either ends the run. A ship may follow another onto the hex it's leaving. Ships come in classes, the same in every level: a coaster holds 4 and hires at $20k, a Handy holds 10 for $30k (cheaper for what it carries); each level's fleet offers so many of each, shown under the map. The sizes aren't multiples of each other on purpose: a ship of N units blends in steps of 1/N (a coaster in quarters, a Handy in tenths), so a spec can suit one and not the other, and a fleet of both reaches totals neither does alone. Targets count units delivered. A jetty works a parcel an hour, in full or not at all, and one that can't happen does nothing: a load needs the parcel on hand and room for it aboard; a discharge takes the jetty's parcel out of the ship's blend, every product in its share, and needs at least that much aboard and the blend on spec (a refused cargo stays aboard, and the status line says why). What a discharge leaves stays aboard and blends into the next load. A refinery's tank fills at its rate up to what it holds, and the refinery waits when it's full. The levels to come build their logic on the rule that an impossible transfer is no transfer, the way Opus Magnum's machines rest on an arm that can grab nothing. The aim is Opus Magnum's depth over time: storage tanks ships both fill and draw from, plans tested against several runs of arrivals, ship-to-ship transfers and tides, stamped and reusable modules, and compliance tickets.

Any plan that delivers counts; tightening it is the second game. Four measures, each kept on its own: cost (ships hired, each at its class's rate, and product lifted at its price), hours (to the last delivery), water (every hex a ship was on) and instructions (each written instruction once, a loop's body once however often it plays, empty hours free). They pull against each other, so no plan is best at all four. Each level carries a par on each measure and the plans that reach it (found by searching every plan of up to three ships on each level's routes, from every starting point); `tests/harbour.mjs` runs those plans and holds the par there, so a rule change can't quietly make a level easier, harder or impossible. A ★ marks a par met, and Menu shows your best beside the par. Tap a par there to watch the plan that reaches it: it takes your plan's place until Back to your plan, runs and steps like yours, and you can pick and copy from it to paste into your own, but not change it, and running it keeps no bests. The plans live with the levels in `harbour-levels.js`, written compactly (one character an hour, a loop as its count and body in brackets: `(2S)`), and the tests run those same plans.

The harbour is drawn in 3D (`harbour-3d.js`, with three.js kept in `vendor/` like every other file, so it plays offline): hex tiles, shore tanks that fill, little ships carrying their cargo in its colours, gliding from hour to hour. It wears the suite's theme: Almanac a chart in brass and navy, Modern an architect's white model, Kontor an inked drawing office (at night a green trace on black), each by day and by night, and at night windows and jetty lamps are lit and ships show their sidelights. Where a device can't draw in 3D, or when Menu says so, the harbour is drawn flat from above (`harbour-flat.js`); both answer the same calls, so the game doesn't know which it has. Under the map is the fleet: tap a class to make the next ship that class, or, with a ship picked, to swap it to that class if one's left. Tap water to put a ship there, facing east; tap it again to turn it 60° to starboard; drag it to move it, onto land to scrap it. Pick an instruction from the tools and tap a program's hours to paint it (the same instruction again clears an hour). The square at the left of the tools switches to picking: tap an hour, then another, and everything between is picked, across ships too (a ship's number picks its whole row; with a mouse, drag or shift-click). Then Copy and Paste (pasting goes in before the picked hour, shifting the rest later), Insert empty hours, Delete, Loop, Back (copies the picked hours reversed with port and starboard swapped: the way home, once the ship has turned round), and the two arrows, which shift the picked rows an hour earlier or later inside the loop and keep going while held. A whole route pasted into an empty ship's row at hour h becomes the same loop h − 1 hours later, so a second ship on the route is one paste.

A loop plays its hours more than once. Loop makes the picked hours play twice; tap the count (×2) for one more pass, hold it (or right-click) for one fewer. Hold an instruction (right-click, with a mouse) and it becomes a loop of its own, that hour played twice. The tape still shows every hour, the body boxed and its later passes faded, so the timing between ships stays in view; painting a faded hour paints the body, and deleting faded hours takes passes away. Loops don't nest. The edits live in `harbour-tape.js` and are tested in `tests/harbour.mjs`.

A tool can also be dragged onto the programs: onto an hour it takes that hour's place; onto the edge between two hours (an hour's outer quarters and the gap) it goes in there for that ship, the hours after it shifting later; onto the hour numbers between two hours it goes in for every ship, a new column squeezed in. Each drop is one edit for Undo, and a tap still just picks the tool. The pen in the tools draws routes: with it on, drag on the map and the picked ship's route goes on from the dot where its program leaves it, an hour a hex (Ahead, Port, Starboard or Astern, whichever reaches it; a hex that would need a 120° turn is skipped, so draw a smoother line, and going back over the last hex takes that step off), added to its program on release, one edit for Undo. While you plan, every ship's route over a loop is drawn on the water from its own program (a ship never decides, so its route doesn't depend on any other ship). Under the tools: Undo and Redo, back to the start, one hour, run or pause, and 1×, 4× or 16× speed. A run keeps every hour it has played: the scrubber above the controls goes back to any of them and forward again as far as you've watched, B steps back an hour, and a crash is marked in both ships' programs at its hour; tap it for the hour before, where the status line says what's coming. Sound is made on the device (a horn leaving a berth, the pump, a chime for a delivery, a thud for a refusal, the sea under a run), and Menu turns it off. Any edit goes back to the start. On a computer, Ctrl or Cmd with C, X, V, A and Z copy, cut, paste, pick everything and undo; Delete deletes the picked hours, Escape stops picking; space runs and pauses, S steps, R goes back. Each level keeps its own plan and bests on the device (from the move to hexes, under new keys: square-grid plans don't carry over). Harbour took Survey's tile on the games screen; Survey is under "…".

A game stands still while you're in its menu, its rules or the games screen (`menu.js`: `onPause(pause, resume)`, resume told how long it stood): Crates', Slate's and Delta's board clocks stop, Rush's countdown stops and its start moves on by the pause, Pipes' oil holds where it is, Spot freezes, a running Harbour stops and runs on after, and Cartel's computer waits with its next move. A duo match's shared clock doesn't stop.

Flags are written as emoji (🇮🇪 is two "regional indicator" letters), and some systems, Windows above all, have no flag pictures and show the letters ("IE"). `flags.js` checks on every page whether the system draws a flag; where it doesn't, a font of just the flags (Twemoji Country Flags, MIT, 78 KB in `fonts/`) goes in front of the page's own font for the flag letters only. Systems that draw flags, like the iPhone, keep their own.

Vision, settled. The campaign is a chart of the world's real supply chains, as global and exotic as they come, not a list of regions. It starts with micro-puzzles (one harbour, one rhythm, one clever timing) and grows by layers, each built from the one below: a chain (harbours linked by voyages, with a plant converting feedstock between them), a network (chains sharing ships, terminals and contracts), the world (everything running for a month, each solved harbour a lit diorama on the chart). The rules stay the same at every scale (hours in a harbour, days at sea). Each layer brings one idea from real trade, taught first as a micro-puzzle: aggregation of many small origins (CNSL from West Africa and India, UCO from China and Southeast Asia), conversion with yields (HVO plants, cardanol), laycans (date windows to load), seasons (the Brazilian cane crush, the monsoon, low water on the Rhine at Kaub), decay in transit (LNG boil-off), production when the wind blows (RFNBOs, e-methanol: where seeded randomness comes in), certification (segregated against mass balance), a product's value set by where it's delivered (THG quota, RTFCs, RINs, LCFS credits: arbitrage as routing), and chokepoints with timing (Suez convoys passing in the Great Bitter Lake, Panama locks and drought drafts, the Bosporus's one-way windows). Design rules, settled. Ships never decide: every program plays exactly as written, hour by hour, whatever happens around it, and the only thing that depends on the harbour is whether a transfer happens. That keeps a plan readable as clockwork and keeps the timing puzzles alive (Rundown is timing ships to a tank). Conditions come later, and only with randomness, when they're needed: as equipment placed in the harbour and paid for, never as a free instruction. Lighthouses, gates, meters and signals that hold ships or plant until something is so, every hour of holding costing demurrage. Pure plans stay possible beside them, and the measures choose between them. Randomness, when it comes, is seeded: a level runs a fixed set of test runs, so every plan replays identically and every score can be verified.

## Arb

From one thing to another through the knowledge base's links, as cheaply as you can. Every step costs 1 + log2 of the links of the thing you step onto: an obscure thing costs 1, the United States about 9, so the cheap route runs through what you specifically know rather than the hubs. Par is the cheapest route there is (Dijkstra over the 4,329 linked things); matching it scores 100, more spent scores in proportion. Each choice shows its cost and kind, every hop the reason the two are linked, and the end the cheapest route. Dead ends aren't offered: a link appears only if it leads somewhere new (a thing linked only to where you stand, or only back onto your route, would force a step back), the goal always does. Routes run between things with 2 to 25 links, three to five hops apart and of different kinds; five a run, random or today's. `tests/arb.mjs` checks the graph.

## Ledger
Is it working? Ledger, under "…", is the record of how well you learn, kept from what the games already do. Recall: every item from the pile that comes back, in Deck or in a game, right or wrong, sorted by how long it was away (within the hour, two days, a week or two, a month, three months); away a week or more and still known is the headline, since that's what lasts, with a weekly line under it and the six piles' counts. Confidence: every bet in Punt, read backwards through Kelly's rule (staking a share f of the pot at decimal odds o, you act as if your chance were (1 + f(o − 1)) / o), plotted against how often those bets won, a dot a tenth, the diagonal the ideal; the average belief against the win rate says whether you stake surer than you are, the Brier score (the mean squared gap between that chance and what happened) sums it up (0 perfect, .250 always saying evens), and the win rate by stake size shows whether bigger stakes win more often. Markets: in Quote, how often the truth lay inside your bid and ask, and the grades. The games report as they settle, to `ledger-log.js` (the pile on every item that comes back, Punt on every bet, Quote on every market), which keeps weekly totals under `ledger:stats`, a few kilobytes however much you play, synced with the solo code like everything else; a game that feeds the pile is measured with no change of its own. As the games change the record can be started afresh (Menu, Settings) without touching the piles. Tested in `tests/ledger.mjs`: ISO weeks across year ends, the gaps, Kelly backwards, the totals, and the pile's once-only move of what was learned under the old four piles.

Knowledge, read two ways: each Punt bet also records the chance a pure guess had (one in the number of options; one in the ways to pick several right ones; none for a typed answer). A stake's Kelly chance less that, over what's left (the standard correction for guessing), is the share of the answer the stake claimed to know: a pass claims none, all in claims all, and at a fair price it's the stake itself. Your hit rate corrected the same way is what you knew (right half the time on four options is knowing a third). The Ledger sets the two side by side, overall and by stake, says whether your stakes rise with what you know, and counts only bets made since Punt began recording the guess's chance.

If both seats of a game are taken when you open it (say you've moved from laptop to phone), you choose which player to carry on as, as before. You can watch your partner's solo game: the partner sheet offers Watch while they play solo. The game you have open is mirrored to the pair's room as you play (every key of its own: its run, its settings), at most every 1.5 seconds while you keep moving. Your partner opens the same game with ?watch: that page doesn't run the game itself (it holds it) but shows it in a frame, the same page with &frame, where every read of that game's keys returns your copy and every write goes nowhere, so their own solo state is never touched. When you move, a new frame loads behind the one on show and takes its place once drawn, at most every 1.2 seconds, so the view changes in place instead of reloading in front of them (the first version reloaded the whole page on every save, which in a game that saves every second meant a page forever reloading). The frames take no taps and connect to nothing; a bar says who you're watching, with Stop. It works for every game without code of its own, because each keeps its state under its own prefix (Slate's is glyph:, its old name); Crates' run link and the suite's keys never go across. Tested on Pipes with ten saves in five seconds: four swaps, the page never reloaded, the view ended on the latest save, and the watcher's own state stayed as it was.

Three scores show on each tile: your solo best, your partner's (by initial), and your duo record: for a team game the pair's best result together (Punt's pot, Delta's fastest board, as a time), for a head-to-head one the wins each way ("Duo 2–1" on yours, "1–2" on theirs; a draw counts for neither). Each player's device reports a finished duo match from its own side (duo/GAME/MATCH/PLAYER: score, won, team or not), so a match counts once and both sides agree on the winner; games on `together.js` say what a match came to through its `result` option (Chart and Quote head to head; Delta and Punt as a team); Crates, Slate and Spot report from their own match code: Crates a board cleared together or not ("Duo 5/7", cleared of played), Slate a board solved together and its time (the fastest shows), Spot the pair's score. `suite.js` holds the pair, the presence, the requests and the records; `rooms.js` and `together.js` the rooms themselves.

## Together
Menu → Play together or Play hidden → send the link. Your partner opens it and joins. The team shares 4 lives and 4 clues per board.

One rule runs through both modes: you never see the result of your own action, your partner does.
- Your wrong guess: you see a life go, your partner sees whether it was one away (in Hidden, also whose pick is the odd one out). Everyone keeps their picks after a wrong guess.
- Your clue (Together): your partner reads the explanation and sees the crate colour. In Hidden, clues work differently (below).
- A crate you find: your partner names it.

**Together** — both of you see all sixteen words and each other's picks. Whoever started the game sets the settings.

**Hidden** — each of you sees only your own eight words; your partner's show as blank sealed tiles, and it's up to you to describe which one you mean. Both screens lay the tiles out identically. Every crate holds one to three words from each side, so a crate always needs picks from both of you.
- Before the first board you each set up your own side: your preset, difficulty and topics shape only the eight words dealt to you. Which countries and commodities can come up is shared and set by whoever started the game. The board is dealt once you've both pressed Ready.
- Clues come from the shared pool of four and are spent on tiles that are sealed on your screen: tap one, then its ?. You see that tile's explanation and crate colour (its ? takes the colour); your partner, who can see the word, only sees that a clue was used on it. The rest is up to the two of you.

## Offline, and installing it as an app
All four games work offline once the site has been opened online once: `sw.js` (a service worker) keeps every file on the device, the font included (it's served from `fonts/`, not Google). Playing together still needs a connection; offline, solo play works fully and Play together says it can't reach the server.

The site installs as an app called Crates, with all four games and the switcher inside:
- Windows: open it in Edge or Chrome, then the install icon in the address bar (or menu → Apps → Install). It gets its own window and a Start menu icon.
- iPhone: open it in Safari, Share → Add to Home Screen. (A home-screen app keeps its own saves, separate from Safari's.)
- Android: Chrome offers Install app.

The games screen (tap a game's name) has an Update button: it checks there's a connection, empties every stored copy of the app and reloads it fresh from the site, keeping saves, stats and settings (offline it changes nothing). Updates otherwise happen by themselves: a new version is fetched in the background and waits; it takes over when the app goes off screen and the page reloads onto it when it comes back (taking over mid-game could mix old and new code). When a partner's device is already on a newer version, the game switches straight away (`pwa.js`).

`sw.js` is written by the workflow `.github/workflows/pages.yml` and by nobody else: on every push to `main` it checks the banks against `kb/`, runs every game's tests and, only if they all pass, rebuilds `sw.js` (the file list and a version from their contents) and commits it as github-actions[bot]. Pages serves `main` as pushed, but installed copies only move to a new version when `sw.js` changes, so a push that fails a test never reaches them; and since nobody else writes `sw.js`, nobody collides on it. `node tools/build-sw.mjs --local` writes one to try offline play.

Several Claudes can work at once, one app each: `CLAUDE.md` holds the rules (shared files one at a time, pull and rebase before pushing, never force-push, never commit `sw.js`, rebuild built files rather than merge them, test before pushing).

## The knowledge base
What the knowledge games know lives once, in `kb/`, and the banks the games read are built from it (`tools/build-kb.mjs`; edit `kb/`, never a built bank).

- **Entities** (`kb/entities.js`, 4,395): every thing a game asks about or uses as a clue: Crates' 69 countries and 90 commodities, the rest of the world's countries (from the flags; a clue named for one, like Namibia under uranium, is that country), 3,483 clue things (Fjords, Equinor, Trading Places, Gouda…), Chart's places and features, 69 paintings with their 44 painters, 69 museums and 29 movements. Each has its **sets** (what it is: country, commodity, group:eu, group:soft, city, trade-place, museum, painting, feature:river…; as many as apply) and its **facts** (position, note, region, year, picture…). Crates' answers keep their board position (`crates`, append-only) and the names accepted when typed.
- **Links** (`kb/links.js`, 6,443): how things connect. A **clue** link is one of Crates' pairs, from the clue to the answer: the hand-written "because" (hint), its **aspects** (the topics you weight in Crates' settings, which belong to the link, not the thing: Equinor is a company to Norway and a firm to natural gas), difficulty, the other answers it also fits, and its place in the answer's list (append-only). A word under several answers is one entity with several links, each with its own sentence (992 such words); a clue that is itself an answer links a country and a commodity directly (372). Typed links: **in** a country (716), **painted-by**, **hangs-in**, **movement**.
- **Items** (`kb/items/`): the questions, each pointing at what it's about. **Pins** (Chart: the place's name, position, note and picture come from the entity), **estimates** (Quote: a painting's year comes from the painting), **choices** (Punt's knowledge banks, word for word, tagged with the paintings and countries they're about). `kb/geometry.js` holds the features' shapes.

The move into `kb/` (October 2026) read every bank and wrote them back identical before anything was replaced; `kb/MIGRATION.md` records it. Things of the same name became one entity (130: Geneva the Chart city and the Crates clue, Lake Titicaca's point and shape, Namibia the flag and the clue under uranium); a natural feature only when something says it's the same thing (the clue is for a country it's in, or its name or hint calls it a river, lake, falls…), so the Orange River stays apart from the Dutch colour, and rivers from the countries named after them (5 kept apart). Also listed: 20 facts that two sources gave differently (the questions keep their own, nothing changed), 78 words that may be two things under one name (Java: Indonesia's island, coffee's nickname, Sun's language) to check and split, and 12 country strings that name no country (Svalbard, Antarctica…). Joining the sources showed three Chart places filed in the wrong country, which the clues contradicted: Geneva (France), Jurong Island (Malaysia) and Kiruna (Russia), now Switzerland, Singapore and Sweden. `tests/kb.mjs` checks the banks are current with `kb/`, every link and item points at a real entity, Crates' order is unbroken, and nothing has fallen below the counts at the move. Punt's skill banks (maths, reasoning, words, patterns) aren't knowledge and stay as they are; Brut, Refinery, Blend and Parley are parked outside it for now.

Knowledge travels between the games. Every question the games read carries the entities it's about (Crates' answers and clues too), and a miss goes into the pile with them. Each game's learning mode then deals, after its own due questions, its own questions about what the other games found you weak on, most-missed first: miss who painted the Mona Lisa in Punt and Quote's next set asks the year it was painted; miss Geneva as a clue in Crates and Chart's next set asks you to pin it, and Punt's Cities level asks Switzerland's capital. Punt adds up to five such questions a block; Quote and Chart fit them within the due half of a set. `pile.weakElsewhere` and `pile.dealDue` do it; `tests/pile.mjs` checks it. Punt's Easy, Medium and Hard questions (built from Crates' clues) carry their clue's and answers' entities too, so a miss there travels like any other.

Deck's overview shows coverage by subject, across the games: for each subject of the knowledge base, how many of its things you're learning (and how many are due) and how many you've learned, of how many it holds, with a bar for the learned share; a thing counts once, whichever games asked about it. A thing with a set is in that set's subject (Countries, Commodities, Paintings, Cities, Physical features…); a clue thing in the subject of its links' commonest aspect, by Crates' topic labels, with the country and commodity versions of one topic joined (Companies & brands, Policy & institutions, Trade & shipping, Culture & arts): 35 subjects in all. The build writes them to `kb-index.js`.

What each clue thing is: `tools/kb-kinds.mjs` reads each of Crates' clue things' hand-written hints and their links' topics and gives it a kind (27 of them: person, company, organisation, place, nature, site, event, law, market, film, book, music, art, food, drink, animal, plant, disease, currency, vehicle, sport, custom, word, technology, material, craft, date) only when the wording clearly says so: the best kind has to score 3.5 or more and beat the next by 1.25 ("A soup thickened with it" is food; "The Cannibal of cycling", with the People topic, a person; "A currency whose name means crown" a currency before a word). That gave 1,047 of 3,356, each marked `kindBy: "words"` so a kind given this way can be told from one set by hand; a sample of 40 checked by hand had 35 right (the misses: the Red Sea filed under ships, oil fires under sites rather than events, call centres under companies, the Purity law under drink, coal exports under places). All the rest but 11, 2,298 in all, have since been settled by reading each one's hints in turn (`kindBy: "read"`: Copernicus a person, HS 0901 a customs code, La Tomatina a festival, Krone a currency, Troll a gas field). The 11 still open in `kb/KINDS.md` are left open on purpose: names that cover more than one thing (Java the island, the coffee and the language; Blue, Green and Grey as colours and as kinds of hydrogen or methanol) and bare descriptors (Ancient, Artisanal, Heavy, Lightweight). Kinds fold into the subjects (a company into Companies & brands, a festival into Culture & arts, a currency into Money & economy), so coverage by subject uses what a thing is where that's known and its links' topics otherwise.

Questions the knowledge base writes itself: a painting in `kb/` with its painter, museum, movement, year and picture gets Punt's who, where and movement questions and Quote's year from the build, unless it already has hand-written ones, and a museum with a position gets Chart's pin unless it has one; so a painting added to `kb/` (one entity and three links) reaches all three games at once. Wrong options are things of the same kind, nearest first: painters of the same movement, then of the nearest years (The Lacemaker: Rembrandt, Velázquez, Frans Hals); movements of the nearest years; museums Chart can place, then the rest. Options' order and ids come from the entity, so every build writes the same questions and the pile keeps track of them. The first four came in this way: The Lacemaker, The Laughing Cavalier, Netherlandish Proverbs and The Blue Boy, with Frans Hals, Thomas Gainsborough, and the Gemäldegalerie and the Huntington as new pins in Chart. Before that the art side was tidied: Chart's art places that aren't museums (Machu Picchu, Stonehenge, the Taj Mahal) are art places now, the seven museums that came in twice (Punt's answer and Chart's pin) are one each, and every museum has the short name its questions show, Punt's own wording where it had one. A painting with no museum (Salvator Mundi, privately held; the stolen Storm on the Sea of Galilee; Sunflowers in its many versions) gets no "where" question. `tests/kb.mjs` checks every painting is asked in Punt and Quote and every placeable museum is pinned.

## Files
`bank.js` is the word bank as base64-encoded JSON, so answers aren't visible at a glance. It is append-only: board codes point at answer and word positions, so words are only ever added at the end of an answer and answers at the end of the bank. `gen.js` builds and checks boards, including the split boards for hidden mode. `run.js` keeps a solo run in step across devices; `net.js` loads the Firebase connection when either needs it. `tests/fake-sync.js` stands in for Firebase in local two-tab tests.

## Bests that compare
A best is only worth comparing if everyone played the same thing. So in the ranked games (Punt, Chart, Quote, Crates; `RANKED` in `suite.js`) the best on the games screen, and the one your partner sees, counts only comparable runs: the daily, and the game's Standard preset (everything in, default settings, learning off). Every daily is dealt on the device from the date (`today()` in `suite.js`, the UTC date, one for every game, so it works offline and rolls over at the same moment everywhere) with nothing of yours in it: no questions due from your pile, no name-it from your history, no narrowed selection. Punt has Today's run (the Standard settings: Balanced, every difficulty, 15 questions, name-it off), Crates Today's board (the mixed puzzles, one go, scored by points then time), Chart and Quote their sets of the day on everything. Other runs still play, learning mode keeps its review in them, and they keep their own bests in each game. `noteComparable(app, score, day)` keeps a game's comparable best and today's daily under `GAME:ranked`, synced like any best; the games screen shows both (Best +41% · Today +12%).
